# Stimming and Belonging with AURORA

Twenty-eight performances, weighted toward live — because the argument is the body — and the physics of the light she shares a name with. The companion rack to [Warriors and Weirdos](https://starstuff.earth/aurora-zine.html), Zine No. 13.

Playlist & essay · Sound

One

## She watched herself back, and did not change a thing

Asked about the way she moves on stage, AURORA gave one of the more useful answers anybody has given about a body in public.

The first time I saw myself playing live… on a video was kind of a terrifying. I didn't know that I looked like I was doing an exorcism or having a heart attack while performing live… but I can't really do anything about it. I'm a very emotional person, and I guess when you have songs with lots of emotions… I have to use everything I got to sing those songs, and I have lots of things to work with.

AURORA, in the [Red Bull session](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fDVwakM7MxQ) for “Conqueror.” Lightly condensed from her spoken answer; ellipses mark cuts.

Read that again for the order of events. **She saw it. She was shocked by it. She kept it.** Not because she reframed it into something admirable, and not because someone reassured her — she says plainly that she can't do anything about it, and then, in the same breath, that she needs it. The movement is not decoration laid over the singing. It is part of how the singing happens. *I have to use everything I got.*

Anyone who has been told to keep their hands still knows how rare that sentence is. The usual sequence runs the other way: you catch sight of yourself, you feel the shock, and you spend the next twenty years editing.

Whose word is which

**She calls it emotion. We are the ones calling it stimming, and that distinction belongs on the page rather than buried in it.** AURORA has spoken publicly about being neurodivergent, using that word; she has not, as far as we can find, described her stage movement as stimming, and we are not going to put the term in her mouth.

What we are saying is narrower and, we think, still worth saying: **a great many of us watch her move and recognise something** — the whole body recruited, the motion arriving with the feeling rather than after it, the flat refusal to hold still for the comfort of onlookers. The recognition is ours. The title of this page describes what her work does for us, not a claim about what is happening inside her.

Two

## What the light she is named after is actually doing

The aurora takes its name from the Roman goddess of the dawn. So does she. That is a coincidence and we are going to make nothing of it — but the coincidence points at a piece of physics that turns out to be about exactly this.

Here is the part everyone knows: charged particles from the Sun spiral down the magnetic field into the upper atmosphere and the sky lights up. Here is the part that matters. **The colour is not the Sun's.** The solar wind delivers energy and nothing else; what you actually see is an oxygen atom, having been knocked into an excited state, dropping back down and releasing a photon at the one wavelength its own structure permits. The famous green is **557.7 nm**, and that number is not a property of the Sun or of the wind or of the weather. It is atomic oxygen's own line. **Energy came from outside. The colour is the atom's.**

Now the part that stopped us. That green line is a **forbidden transition**.

*“Forbidden” is a term of art. It does not mean impossible — it means the transition breaks the electric-dipole selection rules, so it happens *slowly*. An allowed transition is over in nanoseconds. The oxygen ¹S state sits for about **0.7 seconds** before it releases the green photon, and the ¹D state waits about **110 seconds** for the red.*

And that waiting is why altitude decides the colour. Low down, the air is crowded: long before the atom can complete its slow release, something collides with it and takes the energy away instead. Physicists call it **collisional quenching**, which is as bleak a phrase as it sounds. The green shows between roughly 100 and 150 km. The red needs to be higher still, because *110 seconds* of undisturbed waiting is not available anywhere denser.

The most famous light in the sky is a slow release that the rules call forbidden, and it only completes where nothing crowds it.

Both halves matter. The label was never a verdict on whether it could happen. And the room is what decides whether it does.

We are not going to spell out the analogy. You can see it. What we will say is what the physics licenses and what it doesn't: **“forbidden” is a description of a ruleset, not of a possibility** — the sky does it constantly — and **whether a slow thing gets to finish depends on the density of what surrounds it**, which is an argument about rooms rather than about atoms.

A rhyme, not a proof

Oxygen has no stake in any of this. Selection rules are not social rules, and quenching is not a metaphor for a classroom — the word is doing a specific job about collision cross-sections and it does not transfer. Nothing about the 557.7 nm line obliges anyone to make room for anyone. **We are noticing that the sky's best-known trick has the same shape as the thing we keep asking for, and noticing is the whole of the claim.**

Three

## The same song, again, and again

There is a word for the other half of this, and it is ours rather than the industry's. **Stim listening** — listening repetitively to a single song, album or playlist — was coined by **Jim Irion**, who describes using it to start work and to hold focus.

It is the listening practice almost nobody designs for. Streaming services are built to move you along: the next track, the fresh recommendation, the endless lateral drift. Plenty of us want the opposite, and want it for good reasons — the same song forty times because the fortieth is doing something the first could not, because repetition is regulation, because you have found an **auditory samefood** and samefoods are not a phase.

AURORA's catalogue takes this unusually well, and the rack below is arranged for it rather than against it: the same song appears more than once in different rooms, because *Conqueror* at a Red Bull session and *Conqueror* on late-night American television are not the same event, and running them back to back is a legitimate way to listen.

Ryan keeps a YouTube playlist called [*Stimming with Aurora*](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL4vHAg2MP4X982EMeNJdxjnNNKGcw4tAw), and it is worth noticing what is in it: **almost nothing but live performances.** Not the studio versions, not the official videos — the sessions, the radio spots, the concerts. That is not an accident of collecting. When the body is the argument, you need the footage where the body is.

Four

## Queendom, and a people that names itself

The belonging half of this page is argued at length in [Warriors and Weirdos](https://starstuff.earth/aurora-zine.html), so we will not repeat it here. One thing is worth restating, because it is the mechanism.

*Queendom* (2018) does not describe a place that exists. It describes one being declared — a country announced for everybody the existing countries had no room for, which is a different act from asking to be let into somewhere already standing. And her audience did the corresponding thing from the other direction: they took a name. **Weird stops working as an insult at the exact moment a people picks it up and uses it as an address.**

That is the same move the word *stimpunk* makes, and *crip*, and *mad*, and *neuroqueer*. It is also why the reclamation is never available to bystanders: the word only turns when the people it was used against are the ones turning it.

Five

## No cure, and the wounds nobody can see

Two of her songs do work this collection keeps needing, and they do it from opposite ends.

*Cure for Me* (2021) takes the brightest, most danceable arrangement on the record and puts it underneath a refusal — which is the correct setting for that refusal, because the demand for a cure is usually delivered warmly, by people who are sure they are helping. *Invisible Wounds* (2024) goes the other way and stays with the part that does not photograph: the injury nobody can see is still an injury, and the absence of a visible mark has never been evidence of anything except the limits of looking.

Between them sits the practical fact that every access conversation eventually runs into. **What can't be seen still has to be believed.**

Six

## What this page refuses

A page about a beloved performer's body carries specific risks, and it is better to name them than to hope nobody notices.

- **Not a diagnosis.** AURORA has said she is neurodivergent and we use her word, exactly as [Zine No. 13](https://starstuff.earth/aurora-zine.html) does. Aggregator and SEO pages have begun attaching specific conditions to her name; we are not repeating them, and neither should anyone citing this page.
- **Not her art explained by her brain.** “She makes music like that *because* she is neurodivergent” sounds like appreciation and works like a reduction — it turns a body of deliberate craft into a symptom with a soundtrack.
- **Not stimming attributed to her.** She describes emotion. We describe recognition. Those are different sentences and this page keeps them apart.
- **Not inspiration.** She is not evidence that being different works out fine if you have enough talent. Most of us are not going to be given a stage, and the argument for making room cannot depend on the person in the room turning out to be extraordinary.
- **Not stimming as acceptable only when it is beautiful.** This is the one that matters most here. Her movement is celebrated because it is on a stage, set to music, and lovely to watch. **The same body doing the same thing in a classroom gets a behaviour plan, and a child who flaps in a supermarket gets stared at by people who would applaud this.** If the movement is only permitted once it has been made into art, nothing has actually been conceded.
- **Not physics as permission.** The aurora does not authorise anything. It is a rhyme, and it is fenced as one above.

**The aurora.** Atomic oxygen's green line at 557.7 nm is the ¹S → ¹D transition, upper-state lifetime ≈0.7 s; the red line at 630.0 nm is ¹D → ³P, lifetime ≈110 s. Both are forbidden under the electric-dipole selection rules — *forbidden* meaning slow, not impossible. Collisional quenching removes the energy before emission at lower altitudes, so the green appears at roughly 100–150 km and the red only above ~150 km, occasionally much higher. Checked against atmospheric-optics and auroral-spectroscopy sources; altitudes are approximate and given as such. *Aurora* is the Roman goddess of the dawn, and the lights carry her name; we make no more of the coincidence with the artist's name than that.
