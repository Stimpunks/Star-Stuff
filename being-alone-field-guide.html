A Field Guide to
the Ways of Being Alone

Twelve ways of keeping your own company among our Terran kin. A lone wolf is not an outcast. It is a disperser — young, deliberate, crossing the empty ground between territories on its way to founding a pack. Then: the bees who are nearly all solitary, the panda writing letters to animals it will never meet, the puma who remembers who shared, and the ape whose sociability turns out to be a function of how much fruit there is.

Where this came from From Lone Wolfing: The Joys of Autistic Solitude, in the Stimpunks pattern language — which sits beside parallel play, penguin pebbling and dolphining as names for ordinary neurodivergent life. That page asks whether Autistic people are lonelier. This one asks what the rest of the animal kingdom does about company, and finds it does not agree on an answer.
Why the last entry is dashed Because the apparatus stops there. Twelve entries get a reframe table because the popular verdict on them is a misreading worth correcting. The octopus settlements get none: that verdict is not a misreading but a wish, and correcting a wish with a table would be the wrong instrument.

The four conditions

The guide is sorted by a rubric it did not invent. Writing in The Atlantic in 2017, Brent Crane quotes the developmental psychologist Kenneth Rubin: solitude is productive only if it is voluntary, if one can regulate one's emotions effectively, if one can join a social group when desired, and if one can maintain positive relationships outside of it. Crane's own gloss is the sentence this guide turns on — the difference between solitude as rejuvenation and solitude as suffering is the quality of self-reflection available in it, and the ability to come back when one wants to. Rubin's conditions are about people, and the animals below rhyme with them rather than prove them. But the rhyme is worth hearing: in every entry here, what makes the aloneness liveable is not its depth. It is the door.

Condition one · Voluntary

The leaving is a stage, not a verdict

Three animals whose aloneness is chosen, timed, or simply the ordinary form of the species — and in every case the popular reading has it as a failure. The wolf that leaves is not the wolf that lost. And the bee we built the whole metaphor of industrious togetherness on turns out to be the rare one.

Condition two · Regulated

Spacing is a practice, not an absence

Distance takes work. These three maintain their aloneness — with chemistry, with learned tolerance, with a territorial geometry no other mammal uses. None of it is withdrawal. All of it is effort spent on getting the amount of contact right.

Condition three · Rejoinable

The door back is the whole difference

The condition Rubin puts third and Crane puts last is the one this collection cares about most. These three animals are alone for hours, for a season, or for seventeen years — and then they are not, on a schedule they keep. Solitude with a door is rest. Solitude without one is a different word.

Condition four · Networked

Alone is not the same as unconnected

The three entries that finish the argument. A "solitary" carnivore keeping a ledger of who fed it. An ape whose sociability is set by the fruit crop rather than by its nature. A bird that spends a year alone over open ocean and comes back to the same bond. Relation does not require proximity, which is the same thing No. 21 says about a text message.

Where the apparatus stops

Not every verdict is a misreading

This one gets no reframe table, and the absence is the argument. Everywhere above, "sounds like / actually" corrects a popular verdict that got the animal wrong. Here the popular verdict — octopuses are secretly social after all! — is not a mistake about octopuses. It is a hope about ourselves, laid over what the evidence actually describes: a shortage of somewhere to live. You cannot correct that with a table, so we haven't tried.

Not: Not a gene for solitude. The sweat bee polymorphism involves a variant near syntaxin 1a, which is also an autism-associated gene — and that sentence is a trap, not a finding. A bee's nesting strategy is not our social life, and running the inference would be the naturalistic fallacy with a citation attached. Not mascots. No animal here is Autistic, and none of them is a stand-in for us. They are kin with their own business. Not a retraction of Symbioses. Interdependence is still the rule. Solitude is a form of relation, not its absence — the puma's ledger and the panda's scent post are both social acts performed alone. Not a defence of isolation. Rubin's third condition is the hinge, and it is load-bearing: imposed, doorless aloneness is not what any of this describes. Not settled. The dear enemy effect has a documented inverse, orangutan island differences are being revised, and the octopus sites are eight years of observation at two spots.
Star stuff, keeping its distance

Every animal here is built from calcium and carbon forged in dying stars, and each spends some of it on the machinery of not being crowded — scent glands, territory, a fortnight's timing seventeen years in the making. No. 20 found the same thing overhead: two-thirds of stellar systems in the Galactic disk are single, so the sky is not a promise that everyone is paired either.

What the twelve have in common is not how much company they keep. It is that the amount is theirs to set, and that there is a way back. Change the fruit crop and the orangutan's sociability changes with it — which is this collection's oldest argument, arriving through an ape: you change the field, not the animal.

Figures follow the primary and authoritative sources. The four conditions are Kenneth Rubin's, quoted in Brent Crane, "The Virtues of Isolation," The Atlantic, 30 March 2017 — the article cited on the Stimpunks page this guide grew from; Crane's "rejuvenation / suffering" gloss is quoted there too. Wolf dispersal — Mech, L. D. & Boitani, L. (eds.), Wolves: Behavior, Ecology, and Conservation (Chicago, 2003), with Morales-González et al., "Patterns and determinants of dispersal in grey wolves," Biological Reviews 97 (2022); dispersal typically at 9–36 months, secondary dispersal between packs documented, and the commonly reported share of a population that is lone or "floating" is roughly 15–20%, which is given as approximate because it varies by population and by how floaters are counted. Solitary bees — Danforth, B. N., Minckley, R. L. & Neff, J. L., The Solitary Bees: Biology, Evolution, Conservation (Princeton, 2019): social bees such as honey bees and bumble bees "comprise less than 10 percent of all bee species." The often-quoted total of roughly 20,000 described species is given as approximate. Sweat bee polymorphism — Kocher, S. D. et al., "The genetic basis of a social polymorphism in halictid bees," Nature Communications 9, 4338 (2018), with the draft genome in Genome Biology 14, R142 (2013); common-garden rearing kept each population's typical behaviour, indicating a genetic component. The syntaxin 1a association is reported in that paper and is deliberately not carried into an argument here — see the refusals. Giant panda — chemical signalling maintains a communication network among animals with little direct contact; over 90% of marks used the longest-surviving anogenital gland secretions and over 80% were oriented toward animal trails (Swaisgood and colleagues' body of work on panda chemical communication, and Zhou et al., Journal of Zoology, 2019). Dear enemy effect — Fisher, J. (1954), for territorial neighbours in passerines; the "nasty neighbour" inverse and the threat hypothesis are Temeles, E. J., Animal Behaviour 47 (1994). The effect is documented across birds, mammals, amphibians, reptiles, fish and invertebrates, and it does not hold everywhere, which is why both directions are stated. Cheetah — females solitary or with dependent young; males solitary or in stable coalitions of two or three, most often brothers, with roughly 30% of Serengeti coalition members unrelated; coalitions hold territories under 10% of a solitary female's home range, an arrangement described as unique among mammals. Foundational fieldwork is T. M. Caro's on the Serengeti. Fission-fusion — term first used by Hans Kummer (1971); framework formalised in Aureli, F. et al., "Fission-Fusion Dynamics: New Research Frameworks," Current Anthropology 49(4) (2008), defining variation along spatial cohesion, subgroup size and subgroup composition. Periodical cicadas — Magicicada, seven species, twelve 17-year broods and three 13-year broods in eastern North America; five nymphal instars underground feeding on root xylem; mass emergence is understood as predator satiation. Brown bears — aggregation and fidelity to foraging neighbourhoods while preying on Pacific salmon (Alaskan Ursus arctos, small-stream study, 2018); at Brooks Falls in Katmai, tolerance is sustained by the volume of food and structured by a dominance hierarchy. Puma — Elbroch, L. M., Levy, M., Lubell, M., Quigley, H. & Caragiulo, A., "Adaptive social strategies in a solitary carnivore," Science Advances 3(10), e1701218 (2017): directed network analysis over more than 1,000 prey carcasses in northwest Wyoming; tolerance at kills was not predicted by kinship, and individuals were 7.7 times more likely to share with those who had shared with them. Orangutans — semi-solitary, forming temporary parties more often than chance; average daily party size about 1.6–1.9 in West Sumatra against about 1.05–1.3 in East Sumatra and Borneo, with between-site differences attributed to fruit availability rather than to species, revising the older Sumatran/Bornean contrast (van Schaik's socioecological framework; see also the Sumatran and Tapanuli grouping study, American Journal of Primatology, 2020). Wandering albatross — biennial breeders with chick-rearing up to about 280 days and a "sabbatical" year at sea between attempts; pair bonds usually last until a partner dies, with divorce at about 0.3%; bonds take on average 3.2 years (males) and 2.3 years (females) to establish. Octopolis and Octlantis — Octopus tetricus, Jervis Bay, New South Wales; Octopolis found 2009, a shell bed 2–3 m on its long axis with up to 14 animals seen at once; Octlantis surveyed from December 2016, about 18 m by 4 m with roughly 23 dens in three clusters on rock and shell midden and 10–15 animals, reported by Scheel, Godfrey-Smith and colleagues in Marine and Freshwater Behaviour and Physiology. Interactions include den evictions, chasing, posturing and colour change. Held honestly: the 15–20% lone-wolf share and the ~20,000 bee species total are approximations, not counts. Rubin's four conditions are a claim about human solitude; applying them to other animals is this guide's framing device and is marked as a rhyme rather than evidence. The dear enemy effect is genuinely contested in direction. The octopus sites are two locations over a limited run of observations, and reading them as a housing shortage rather than a society is an interpretation — the honest position is that dense aggregation on scarce hard substrate does not by itself demonstrate sociality. The reframe throughout — that these are four conditions rather than a spectrum from lonely to social, and that the door matters more than the distance — is ours. Grew from Lone Wolfing: The Joys of Autistic Solitude (Stimpunks); on solitude as recovery rather than symptom, see Helen Edgar's Reclaiming Rest: Autistic Burnout, Monotropism, and Resistance (Autistic Realms). There is no standard amount of company.
