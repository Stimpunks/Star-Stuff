A Field Guide to the
Ways of Being Family

Thirteen ways of being family among our Terran kin, sorted by what the care actually flows through — which turns out, often enough, not to be relatedness. Care runs on a kept record, on a coalition of strangers, on a boundary crossed, on nobody being able to tell whose is whose. Sometimes it really does run on family, and two entries here show that plainly. Family is one of the things care runs on. It was never the rule.

Where this came from The companion to The Village Was Never Just Family (No. 64), which grew from the Stimpunks glossary entry Chosen Family. That zine asks is biological kinship nature's default, and answers no — across cooperatively breeding birds, the species that need helpers most are the ones least made of relatives. This guide asks the other question: if not relatedness, then what. Two of the zine's animals are handed off here in one line rather than told twice.
Why the last entry is dashed Because the apparatus stops there. Thirteen entries get a reframe table because a confident popular verdict misreads an animal. The families people make get none — chosen family is already the thing that gets assessed, by forms and hospitals and relatives, and it does not need another table.

Six things the care runs on

The sort is ours, and it came out of the entries rather than ahead of them. We started meaning to sort by how related the carers are, on a line from kin to non-kin, and that sort collapsed almost immediately — because a superb fairy-wren nest is full of chicks fathered from outside the group and fed by every male in it, which puts one nest at both ends of the line at once. What actually varies is the mechanism: what the care is keyed to. So the sections below run from care that keeps a ledger, through coalitions of unrelated equals, adoptions across a group or a species line, and a case where parentage is deliberately unknowable — then two entries where it genuinely does run on family, and two where the science had to be walked back. None of these ranks above another, which is the whole reason it is a catalogue and not a ladder.

One · A record

Care that keeps a ledger

Two animals whose helping is keyed to what has already been given rather than to a pedigree. In one the accounting beats relatedness by more than eight to one. In the other the stranger is driven off at first and admitted later — and only when the pair cannot manage the brood alone, which is an honest thing to have to say about hospitality.

Two · A coalition

Among unrelated equals

Three cases where the adults raising young together are not a family and are not helpers either — they are co-breeders. The middle entry is the sharpest fact in this guide: in superb fairy-wrens most of the brood is fathered by males from outside the group who contribute nothing, and every male in the group feeds it regardless.

Three · A crossing

Across a line that was supposed to hold

Adoption is the plainest case, and it keeps going further than expected. Past paternity, in the chimpanzees. Past the social group, in two bonobo females at Wamba. And past the genus altogether, at one atoll in French Polynesia, where a dolphin raised a calf of another species alongside her own.

Four · Not knowing

Where nobody can tell whose is whose

The strangest mechanism here, and the only one that is architectural. Banded mongooses do not override kinship; they make it unknowable, by giving birth on the same night into one pile of pups. The literature's own phrase for what that produces is a veil of ignorance.

Five · Family, really

Where it does run on relatedness

This section exists because the guide would be dishonest without it. Fifty-five per cent of cooperatively breeding bird species nest primarily with kin, and here are two that plainly do. One finds its relatives by accent. In the other, the grandmother who helps most is the one who has stopped having calves of her own.

Six · Walked back

Where the story needed correcting

Two entries that cut against this guide's own grain. In one, the helpers stay not because there is nowhere to go but because staying is the better offer — constraint as arithmetic rather than as a wall. In the other, the textbook eusocial mammal turns out not to be an obligate cooperative breeder at all. An overclaim in our favour is still an overclaim.

And one more

We are not going to grade your family

The obvious fourteenth card. It gets an entry and no reframe table, and the absence is the argument.

Not: Not permission from nature. Nothing here licenses anything about how anybody lives. A universe that could approve could also decline, and we would not take the verdict either way. Down to is not because of. Not a ranking. Six things the care runs on, not six rungs. The long-tailed tit that sorts its help by kinship is not behind the mongoose that cannot, and the fairy-wren is not the winner for having the most startling number. Not mascots. No animal here is Autistic, and none is a stand-in for us. They are kin with their own business, and the two apes in the crossing section are individuals whose motives we do not know. Not the claim that biological family is a fiction. Fifty-five per cent of cooperatively breeding bird species nest primarily with kin, section five is here because of it, and plenty of people are loved well by the relatives they were born to. The argument is against a ranking, not against anybody's mother. Not the claim that any of this was chosen. Kingfishers admit the stranger under hardship; warblers stay because the arithmetic favours it; the mongoose veil is a by-product of competition between breeding females. Care and constraint occupy the same burrow, and saying which is which is the work. Not settled. Two of these are single-figure reports — two bonobo adoptions, one dolphin — and stay reports rather than rates. The kin-recognition mechanism in long-tailed tits is evidenced and not closed. The coalition card is a category taken from a review rather than from the primary literature on its three species, and says so on its face.
Star stuff, keeping each other

Every carer here is spending calcium, carbon and nitrogen forged in dying stars on the same problem — somebody has to raise the young — and no two of them solved it the same way. The iron in a bat's regurgitated meal, the calcium in a mongoose pup's spine, the nitrogen in the milk a bonobo gave to an infant from another group: all of it assembled in stellar interiors and in the deaths that followed.

What the thirteen have in common is not a mechanism. It is that each of them gets raised. The mechanisms do not converge and they do not rank, and the ones a textbook would have called failures of family — feeding a stranger, feeding a brood mostly fathered elsewhere, feeding a pup you cannot identify, feeding a calf of another genus — are stable, repeatedly-evolved, unremarkable solutions.

There is no standard family. The full argument is in The Village Was Never Just Family (No. 64), which this guide is the catalogue for.

Figures follow the primary and authoritative sources, and where we did not reach the primary this note says so. Vampire bats — Carter, G. G. & Wilkinson, G. S., "Food sharing in vampire bats: reciprocal help predicts donations more than relatedness or harassment," Proceedings of the Royal Society B 280, 20122573 (2013); read in the primary; source of the 20 bats, 48 induced sharing days over two years, the mixed-relatedness and equal-familiarity design, "Food received was the best predictor of food given across dyads, and 8.5 times more important than relatedness," "Sixty-four per cent of sharing dyads were unrelated, approaching the 67 per cent expected if nepotism was absent," donors initiating more often than recipients, the allogrooming correlation, and the roughly 70-hour fasting limit. Stated in one card and argued in No. 64. Pied kingfishers — Reyer, H.-U., "Flexible helper structure as an ecological adaptation in the pied kingfisher," Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology 6, 219–227 (1980), and "Investment and relatedness: a cost/benefit analysis of breeding and helping in the pied kingfisher," Animal Behaviour 32, 1163–1178 (1984); the primary/secondary helper distinction, primary helpers usually being sons of the breeding male, secondary helpers being unrelated males who arrive after hatching, are warded off and later tolerated, feed the female, contribute less on defence and provisioning, and are accepted when conditions are poor enough that the pair's own time and energy budget will not raise the brood. Greater anis — Christina Riehl's study system at Gatún Lake, Panama, under the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute; communal nesting by unrelated pairs sharing one clutch, with the absence of kin discrimination reported in "Social living without kin discrimination: experimental evidence from a communally breeding bird," Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology 69 (2015), and the fitness value of stable bonds between unrelated females in "Stable social relationships between unrelated females increase individual fitness in a cooperative bird," Proceedings of the Royal Society B 285, 20180130 (2018). Group sizes are given as "several pairs" rather than a figure, because we did not verify a distribution. Superb fairy-wrens — Mulder, R. A., Dunn, P. O., Cockburn, A., Lazenby-Cohen, K. A. & Howell, M. J., "Helpers liberate female fairy-wrens from constraints on extra-pair mate choice," Proceedings of the Royal Society B 255, 223–229 (1994); source of 76% of offspring (138 of 181) sired by extra-group males contributing no care, 95% of broods (38 of 40) containing extra-group young, and all group males contributing to feeding and defence. Coalitions — brown skuas, Galápagos hawks and green wood-hoopoes are named as examples of coalition formation in Riehl, C., "Evolutionary routes to non-kin cooperative breeding in birds," Proceedings of the Royal Society B 280, 20132245 (2013), which we read; we did not read the primary literature on these three species and print no figures for them. The same review is the source of the three-route framing and of the 55%/15%/30% group-composition split quoted in section five. Taï chimpanzees — Boesch, C., Bolé, C., Eckhardt, N. & Boesch, H., "Altruism in forest chimpanzees: the case of adoption," PLoS ONE 5(1), e8901 (2010); 18 adoptions, half by males, "only one of these proved to be the father," three males genetically confirmed unrelated, 27 years of observation. Bonobos — Tokuyama, N., Toda, K., Poiret, M.-L., Iyokango, B., Bakaa, B. & Ishizuka, S., "Two wild female bonobos adopted infants from a different social group at Wamba," Scientific Reports 11, 4967 (2021); the first report of cross-group adoption in great apes; one adoptive mother already had two dependent offspring and the other was an old parous female whose own offspring had emigrated; care included carrying, grooming, nursing and food sharing; no aggression by group members toward the out-group adoptees; the authors state the adoptions were unrelated to kinship and to pre-existing relationships between the adoptive and biological mothers. Two cases, reported as such. Cross-genus adoption — Carzon, P., Delfour, F., Dudzinski, K., Oremus, M. & Clua, É., "Cross-genus adoptions in delphinids: one example with taxonomic discussion," Ethology 125, 669–676 (2019); Rangiroa Atoll, French Polynesia; a female common bottlenose dolphin reared a presumed melon-headed whale (Peponocephala electra) calf alongside her own presumed biological calf, from about one month old until presumed weaning in April 2018; the adoptee suckled, socialised with the community's other juveniles and surfed with them. The word presumed is the paper's, and the taxonomic discussion in its title is why we keep it. Banded mongooses — birth synchrony with 64% of females giving birth on the same night, communal litters, and escorting are from the Banded Mongoose Research Project literature: Cant, M. A. et al. on reproductive competition and extreme birth synchrony (Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2010/2011); Hodge, S. J., "Pup escorting in the communal breeding banded mongoose: behavior, benefits, and maintenance," Behavioral Ecology 15(6), 952–960 (2004), for escorted pups surviving better and for the absence of assortment between escorts and pups by parentage or relatedness; Marshall, H. H. et al., "Biased escorts: offspring sex, not relatedness, explains alloparental care patterns in a cooperative breeder," Proceedings of the Royal Society B (2017); and Marshall, H. H. et al., "A veil of ignorance can promote fairness in a mammal society," Nature Communications 12, 3717 (2021), which supplies the phrase. The Rawlsian framing is the literature's own, not ours. Long-tailed tits — Hatchwell, B. J., "Social organization of cooperatively breeding long-tailed tits: kinship and spatial dynamics," Journal of Animal Ecology 70 (2001); Hatchwell, B. J. et al. on ecological and demographic correlates of helping (2013), for redirected helping by failed breeders and cooperation peaking at intermediate nest predation; and "Helping decisions and kin recognition in long-tailed tits: is call similarity used to direct help towards kin?", Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 375, 20190565 (2020), for the correlation between call similarity and kinship, failed breeders choosing to help males with calls similar to their own, breeders reacting aggressively to non-kin calls near the nest, and the finding that provisioning effort was not correlated with vocal similarity. That last negative result is kept because it limits the claim. Killer whales — Nattrass, S., Croft, D. P., Ellis, S., Cant, M. A., Weiss, M. N., Wright, B. M., Stredulinsky, E., Doniol-Valcroze, T., Ford, J. K. B., Balcomb, K. C. & Franks, D. W., "Postreproductive killer whale grandmothers improve the survival of their grandoffspring," PNAS 116(52), 26669–26673 (2019); post-reproductive grandmothers having a greater effect than breeding grandmothers, with the proposed reason that a grandmother with her own calf is constrained in movement and activity and can invest less. Seychelles warblers — Komdeur, J., "Importance of habitat saturation and territory quality for evolution of cooperative breeding in the Seychelles warbler," Nature 358, 493–495 (1992); the Aride Island translocation, young birds breeding in high-quality territories where competition was absent, and becoming helpers, budders or floaters as high-quality habitat saturated; young on high-quality territories staying as helpers rather than taking vacancies on poor ones; helpers raising reproductive success on good territories and reducing it on poor ones. We reached this paper through secondary accounts and the surrounding literature rather than the paywalled original, and the two-part title is the reason we present quality alongside saturation rather than saturation alone. The constraints model itself is Emlen, S. T., "The evolution of helping. I. An ecological constraints model," The American Naturalist 119(1), 29–39 (1982). Damaraland mole-rats — Thorley, J. et al., "Damaraland mole-rats do not rely on helpers for reproduction or survival," Evolution Letters 7(4), 203–215 (2023), for pairs reproducing without helpers, solitary natal dispersal being the commonest route to breeding in both sexes, inheritance of dominant positions being uncommon, and the assessment that similarities to obligately eusocial insects have been over-emphasised; and Zöttl, M. et al., "Differences in cooperative behavior among Damaraland mole rats are consequences of an age-related polyethism," PNAS 113(37) (2016), for the absence of fixed developmental castes. Held honestly: the sort into six mechanisms is ours, arrived at after the entries and not before them — our first attempt sorted by degree of relatedness and collapsed on the fairy-wren, which belongs at both ends of that line at once. Two cards are handed off in a line rather than told twice, following the weaver in A Field Guide to Nests. The puma is deliberately absent: its non-kin reciprocity finding is already the ledger card in A Field Guide to the Ways of Being Alone, and re-carding it here would be a third telling. Brood parasitism is also absent, though it is the obvious dark inverse — it is already spent in Nests and Eggs, and a guide about what care runs on does not need a third pass at what happens when it is exploited.
