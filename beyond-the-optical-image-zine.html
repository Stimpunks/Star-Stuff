Dark Matter Field

She named it first, and that order matters

In My Monotropic Galaxy (More Realms, June 2026), Helen Edgar maps twenty constellations of her own Autistic self. The first of the twenty is the Dark Matter Field.

Much of the lived experience of being Autistic for many people is weighed down by what I have called the Dark Matter Field in my galaxy. This is the heavy darkness of neuronormativity, reflected in the accumulated weight we carry around of a world that does not meet our needs, and does not love us down to our star stuff.Helen Edgar · My Monotropic Galaxy · More Realms, 2026

She reached for the cosmic vocabulary on her own, for her own reasons, before anybody asked what the physics of it was. We have left this constellation alone twice — No. 45 and No. 46 both took other namings from her map and said in their colophons that Dark Matter Field was being deliberately kept back. This is the piece that spends it.

The direction of travel is the whole discipline of the thing. Her naming came first; the astronomy is set beside it afterward, to see what the careful version of that inference actually looks like. The reverse move — physics first, then a claim about people because the physics said so — is the one this whole collection exists to refuse. It has a name, and it has a body count.

So: nothing on the next eleven spreads is evidence about a nervous system. What follows is a method, worked all the way through by people who were very good at it, and then the same method held up against how the question got answered about us.

Coma, 1933

He counted the light and weighed the cluster

Fritz Zwicky did something very simple, with seven galaxies. He measured how fast they were moving relative to each other, and asked how much mass it would take to keep them from flying apart.

Then he counted the light and asked how much mass that implied, assuming the stars were like our Sun. The two numbers did not agree. They did not nearly agree.

the average density in the Coma system would have to be at least 400 times greater than that derived on the basis of observations of luminous matterZwicky 1933 · Helvetica Physica Acta 6 · English translation

Four hundred times. Not a rounding error, not a calibration problem, not something you argue away over lunch. The discrepancy was the finding — a gap between what a model predicted and what the bodies were actually doing.

And he did name it. The translation has him writing that this “would lead to the surprising result that dark matter exists in much greater density than luminous matter.”

So the word was there from the start. What he did with it next is the part nobody quotes.

What he declined to do

And then he called it an unsolved problem

Zwicky had a factor of four hundred and a name for it. He could have announced a discovery. Here is how the section actually ends:

These considerations indicate that the large velocity dispersion in the Coma system (and other dense clusters of nebulae) holds an unsolved problem.Zwicky 1933 · closing the argument

Read that again with the four hundred still in mind. He had the measurement. He had the word. He filed it as unsolved.

That is not modesty and it is not hedging. It is a precise report of what he was entitled to say: a discrepancy of this size exists, and I do not know where it lives. He lists three candidate accounts in the paper and settles on none of them.

The first thing a careful person does with a discrepancy is decline to place it.

Hold onto that sentence. In ninety-three years it does not get improved on, and on spread ten we are going to want it badly.

ApJ 238, 471 · 1980

The paper everyone cites never says dark

Vera C. Rubin, W. Kent Ford, Jr. and Norbert Thonnard took spectra of twenty-one Sc galaxies out into their faint outer regions and published the rotation curves. It is the paper the whole story hangs on. Almost everything said about it is slightly wrong.

the retelling · and the abstract

Rubin discovered dark matter“must have significant mass located beyond the optical image”

The curves are not flat; they are still climbing where the light gives out. The negative claim in the abstract is narrower still and more careful than either version: “Neither high nor low luminosity Sc galaxies have falling rotation curves.” Not they are flat — they do not fall. That is what the data could carry.

And then the conclusion, which is the sentence this zine is named after: Sc galaxies of all luminosities must have significant mass located beyond the optical image.

Look at what that sentence does. It says where. It refuses to say what. The word “dark” does not appear in the abstract at all. Forty-seven years after Zwicky, with better instruments and twenty-one galaxies instead of seven, the claim is still only about the location of mass — and the paper is filed under internal motions, because that is what was measured.

PASP 112, 747 · 2000

Twenty years on, she still would not place it

In 2000, Rubin wrote a retrospective called One Hundred Years of Rotating Galaxies. By then she was the most cited person alive on this question. Here is what she was willing to say.

Surprisingly, we cannot yet rule out a modified gravitational potential, rather than dark matter, as the explanation of our observations.Vera Rubin · PASP 112, 747 · 2000

Twenty years after the famous paper, the woman the discovery is named for is still holding two rival accounts open: maybe there is unseen mass, or maybe our law of gravity is wrong at these distances. She does not pick. She reports that the observations cannot yet decide.

And then this, which is the reason this zine exists:

It is folly to believe that we know what a galaxy is, while the extent, the density distribution, and the composition of ≥90% of its mass are still a dark mystery.Vera Rubin · the same essay

Folly. Her word, about her own field, about the object she spent a career measuring. Not we are nearly there. Not the details remain. It is folly to believe that we know what a galaxy is.

ApJ 648, L109 · 2006

Then they placed it — and this is where we were wrong

Two galaxy clusters collided. The hot gas — which is most of the ordinary matter — got slowed by the collision and left behind. The galaxies sailed on through. So for once, the ordinary matter and everything else were in different places.

Clowe and colleagues mapped the gravity by how the cluster bent the light of things behind it, and found that the gravity was not where the gas was. It was out with the galaxies. Their title is not shy: “A direct empirical proof of the existence of dark matter.”

An 8-sigma significance spatial offset of the center of the total mass from the center of the baryonic mass peaks cannot be explained with an alteration of the gravitational force law, and thus proves that the majority of the matter in the system is unseen.Clowe et al. · ApJ 648, L109 · 2006
This zine was proposed on a sentence that is wrong, and we are leaving the correction on the page. The pitch said the field is “never directly observed, only detected by its distorting effect” — and offered gravitational lensing as evidence of that. But lensing is the instrument that made the direct detection, and this paper explicitly rules out the modified-gravity account Rubin was still holding open six years earlier. The premise had to go. We publish our own errors; that is the house.

Two claims, held apart

Unseen is not the same as unfindable

Here is the thing the smoothed retelling flattens in both directions at once. The mass is established. The identity of that mass is not. Those are different claims and the field keeps them scrupulously apart.

Deep underground, in tanks of liquid xenon, people have spent decades waiting for one of these particles to bump into something. LZ has now reported hundreds of live days of exposure and found nothing. XENONnT, tonne-years of exposure, nothing. The searches are widening to lighter and stranger candidates — axion-like particles, dark photons — because the obvious ones keep not being there.

Ninety-three years after Zwicky, the ledger reads: we know it is there, we know roughly how much, we know where it sits, and we do not know what it is.

Both halves of that get abused. Say only the first half and you have a settled science that brooks no questions. Say only the second and you have “scientists don't even know what dark matter is” deployed as if it discredited the measurement. The honest version is harder to shout and it is the only one that is true.

The structural question

Three places a discrepancy can live

Strip away the galaxies and this is the shape of the whole thing. A model predicts what a body will do. The body does something else. The gap has to go somewhere.

There are exactly three places to put it. In the mass: something is there that we are not seeing. In the law: our model of how things work is wrong at this scale. In the measurement: we are reading the instrument badly.

Astronomy has spent ninety-three years arguing all three, out loud, in journals, with the rival accounts named and the failures published. Zwicky listed candidates and picked none. Rubin held mass and law open for twenty years past her own famous result. Clowe closed one of them and said precisely how — 8-sigma, and here is the configuration that made it possible. The null results from the xenon tanks are published, which is why anyone knows the particle is missing.

None of that is a courtesy. It is the machinery that makes the eventual answer worth anything. A verdict is only as good as the alternatives that were allowed to compete with it.

The same question, asked about us

Asked about a child, the question was never opened

A developmental model predicts what a child will do. The child does something else. Identical shape. A measured gap, and three places it could live.

It went into the child. Not into the fluorescent hum or the unannounced transition or the six hours of masking already spent before lunch. Not into the double empathy problem — Damian Milton's (2012) point that the failure of understanding runs both ways and is a property of the gap between two parties, not of one of them. Not into what Nick Walker named the neuronormative paradigm: the assumption that there is one right way to be, one correct way to think.

Read the argument precisely, because the sloppy version of it is not ours. We are not claiming the environmental attribution is obviously the right one, or that a body never contributes to a gap. We are claiming something narrower and much harder to answer: it was never treated as an open question.

No rival account was funded. No competing attribution was named in the criteria. Nobody wrote “surprisingly, we cannot yet rule out” and left it standing for twenty years. Where astronomy built a ninety-three-year public controversy, we got a default — and a default with a citation attached is much harder to argue with than an insult.

Where it breaks

This is not evidence about you

The proposal for this zine called the parallel “an exact structural match.” It is not, and here is the failure at full strength rather than tucked in a hedge.

Dark matter is hidden by nature. Neuronormativity is hidden by convention. That is a different kind of hiding, and it changes everything about what follows.

The field warping us is not undetected. It is written down. It is in the diagnostic criteria, the intervention targets, the compliance goal on an education plan, the phrase “culture fit” in a hiring rubric, the seating chart, the lighting spec. You do not need a tonne of liquid xenon a mile underground. You need to read the paperwork.

The right word is not invisible — it is unmarked. It goes unnamed because it is the baseline, and the baseline is the one thing a measurement never lists among its objects.

And that difference is good news. A particle that has evaded ninety-three years of the best instruments ever built may simply be beyond us for another century. A convention is a decision, written by people, and it can be read, quoted, contested and changed this year.

One field, two readings

The physics chooses neither, and that is the tell

Helen's Dark Matter Field is the accumulated weight — the heavy darkness, the thing pressing down. That reading is hers and it is true to her life.

Now take the identical fact. Unseen mass is most of what holds a galaxy together. Without it the thing flies apart. Read that way, the very same physics is the perfect emblem of something else entirely: the co-regulation nobody logs, the care work nobody bills, the friend who answers at 2am, the accommodations that never made it into a report. The unmeasured is not the unreal, and it is often the unmeasured that is holding everything in place.

Same field. Same evidence. Same ninety-three years of measurement. Crushing weight and the only reason anything is still standing — and the physics does not adjudicate between them, because it was never asked about you.

If one fact can carry two opposite morals, the moral was never in the fact.the whole reason we mark it

This is the cleanest demonstration we have of why this collection keeps saying a rhyme, not a proof. Someone has to choose which reading to make, and then own the choosing rather than hiding behind the galaxy. Helen chose weight, in her own galaxy, about her own life, and said so in the first person. That is what an honest use of a metaphor looks like.

Not:dark matter proving the field is real. Ninety-three years of rotation curves say nothing whatsoever about a nervous system. If you needed astrophysics to believe us, the problem was never the evidence.
Not:an argument against evidence. The opposite. Careful public attribution is the thing we are asking for. Nulls published, rivals named, verdicts withheld — give us that and we will take our chances.
Not:modified-gravity advocacy. Rubin's 2000 sentence was true in 2000. Clowe answered it in 2006 and we quote the answer, at 8-sigma, on spread seven.
Not:“you are mysterious.” Rubin's folly line is about a galaxy. Turning it into mystique about Autistic people is the move we are refusing — being unexplained is not the same as being unknowable, and we are neither.
Not:the inspiration story about Vera Rubin. She was shut out of observatories and she was scrupulously careful. Only the second one is our argument here, and borrowing the first would be doing to her what gets done to us.
Not:a claim that bodies never contribute. A gap can have more than one source. The objection is to the question being closed, not to any particular answer.
Not:Helen's reading corrected. Her galaxy is hers. Spread twelve adds a second reading to the same physics; it does not take hers away.

The sentence this zine is named for is the one to keep. Rubin, Ford and Thonnard had twenty-one galaxies and every reason to reach, and they wrote: mass located beyond the optical image. Exactly what was measured. Nothing about what it was made of, or what it meant, or what anybody deserved.

Ask for claims about us that narrow, and that honest. There is a great deal of you beyond the optical image — and the loving does not wait on the inventory.

Reflection

Where in your life has a gap been attributed to you, without the other two doors ever being opened?

What would it take to get someone to write “we cannot yet rule out” about a verdict already made about you?

Which of the unmarked conventions around you is actually written down somewhere you could go and read?

Whose unmeasured mass is holding your life together right now — and does anybody's paperwork know it?

A method, not a proof. Ninety-three years of astrophysics knows nothing about us and demonstrates nothing about us. What it demonstrates is what care looks like when a discrepancy is measured and nobody yet knows where it lives. Every place this zine sets a physical fact beside a lived one, it is noticing a shape in both and saying so — and on spread eleven it says where the shape stops.
