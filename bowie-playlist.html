Nobody Got His Eyes Right

Thirty-one songs across a career — and the two things everyone knows about David Bowie that are not true. Both of them make the difference tidier than it was. The companion rack to Turn and Face the Strange, Zine No. 12.

Playlist & essay · Sound
One

He did not have different-coloured eyes

This is the most-looked-at face in the history of pop music, and the single fact everybody repeats about it is wrong.

Bowie is very widely said to have had heterochromia — two irises of different colours. He did not. Both of his irises were the same colour. What he had was anisocoria: two pupils of different sizes, one of them permanently open.

In February 1962, when he was fifteen, his schoolfriend George Underwood punched him in the left eye during a row over a girl. A fingernail caught the eyeball, and the muscles that contract the iris were left paralysed. From then on the left pupil could not close. A pupil stuck wide is a hole full of shadow, so the left eye photographs darker — and next to a right eye whose pupil is doing its ordinary job, the pair reads as two different colours. That is the whole illusion.

So the correction is not pedantry about a word. It changes what kind of thing the famous feature was. Heterochromia is a variation you are born with. Anisocoria from a punch is an injury, and this one had consequences: a pupil that cannot contract cannot limit the light coming in, and Bowie was reported to have had impaired depth perception for the rest of his life. The look that a million people have called otherworldly is one eye working and one eye damaged.

Glamour is not an accommodation.

Nobody ever seems to ask what a fixed pupil is like under a follow spot.

He and Underwood stayed friends for the rest of his life, and Underwood — a painter — went on to make artwork for Bowie's records. Bowie himself said the eye gave him an interesting appearance and was, on balance, useful to him. Both things are allowed to be true. He made an injury into a signature, and it was still an injury, and neither half of that cancels the other.

Two

And the constellation is not a constellation

The second thing everyone knows is nearly as widely repeated and fails in the same direction.

In January 2016, days after he died, the MIRA public observatory in Belgium and the radio station Studio Brussel announced a constellation for Bowie: seven stars near Mars, arranged in the lightning bolt from the Aladdin Sane sleeve. It is a genuinely lovely gesture and thousands of people posted about it.

It is not a constellation. There are 88 constellations, the International Astronomical Union fixed the list in the 1920s, and it has not changed since. What MIRA made was an asterism — a line figure, a way of joining dots — and to their credit they said so themselves, describing it as an invented Bowie-asterism rather than an addition to the sky's official furniture. Nobody can register a constellation. Nobody can sell you a star, either, whatever the certificate says.

Both errors lean the same way

Notice what these two have in common, because it is the reason this page exists rather than just a rack of songs. Neither mistake is random. Heterochromia is prettier than a punch in the eye. An official constellation is grander than a radio station's tribute. Both errors take something real — a damaged iris, a nice gesture by a small observatory — and round it up into something more elegant, then travel further than the truth did.

That is the exact mechanism Too Good to Check is about, and it is the reason we keep a field card for it. A claim that flatters its subject gets less scrutiny, not more.

And there is a cost that is not just tidiness. When the story about somebody's difference is more beautiful than the difference, the practical part stops getting discussed. Sixty years of writing about that eye, and almost none of it about light sensitivity, or depth perception, or what it takes to perform under those conditions. The myth is a very comfortable place to stop.

Three

What he actually did was set selves down in public

The argument about Bowie and difference is made at length in Zine No. 12, so this is the compressed version plus one thing the zine does not have.

The zine's claim is that Bowie inverts what masking means for the rest of us. Many of us wear the involuntary kind — kept up to pass as normal, kept up until it costs something. His masking was authorship: selves made, shown in full view, and then put down. Ziggy, Aladdin Sane, the Thin White Duke, Major Tom. Unmasking, on that reading, does not have to mean excavating one true self from underneath. It can mean the freedom to be several things out loud and mean all of them.

The thing worth adding is that he demonstrated the putting down as literally as anyone ever has. On 3 July 1973, at the Hammersmith Odeon, he told a sold-out room it was the last show they would ever do — and killed Ziggy on stage, mid-tour, at the height of it. Most of the band found out at the same time as the audience.

The popular telling is a little too clean

You will often read that he told nobody. Not quite: Mick Ronson knew, and by some accounts the stage manager was told shortly before, and the road crew were in on it. It was most of the band who were blindsided, not all of it.

We are correcting a detail that makes the anecdote marginally less punchy, on a page whose whole first half is about stories getting rounded up. It would be a poor look to do the same thing ourselves two sections later.

A persona you can end on a Tuesday in July is a persona you were authoring. That is the difference between a costume and a diagnosis, and it is why the zine reads his career as direction rather than deficit.

Four

The ending was written down first

And then, at the very end, he did the same trick one last time, in public, with the timing controlled to the day.

★ was released on 8 January 2016, his sixty-ninth birthday. The video for Lazarus — in which he sings from a hospital bed and then backs into a wardrobe and out of sight — went up the day before. He died on 10 January. Almost nobody outside his circle knew he had been ill.

He made Blackstar for us, his parting gift. I knew for a year this was the way it would be.

Tony Visconti, his producer, on 11 January 2016 — the day after.

What that means for this collection is specific. The L★S Playlist already notes that Blackstar is not the bright burning kind of star but the collapsed kind — still massive, still present. Set beside the eye, the arc finishes an argument the whole career was making: the parts of you that got damaged, and the parts you decided to author, end up in the same body of work, and you do not have to sort them into the good ones and the bad ones before you are allowed to be here.

That is what we mean by star stuff, said as plainly as we can say it. Not the difference redeemed. The difference included.

Not:

  • Not a diagnosis. Anisocoria is a documented physical injury with a date and a named cause. We are not reading anything else into his mind, his neurology, or his sexuality from his songs, and nothing on this page should be cited as though we had.
  • Not damage as the source of the art. The punch did not make him Bowie. Suffering-as-fuel is one of the oldest and laziest stories about difference, and it conveniently excuses never fixing anything.
  • Not the injury erased either. The opposite error, and the more common one here: calling the eye iconic and stopping. It cost him something, permanently, and the celebration usually skips that part.
  • Not one true self, located and held still. The zine's refusal and this page's too — be yourself as an instruction to pick one and stop is not liberation.
  • Not weird as a phase. Difference is not a stage on the way to normal.
  • Not a correction offered as debunking. We are not scoring points off people who said heterochromia; nearly everyone did, including publications with fact-checkers. The point is how a flattering claim travels, not that anyone was foolish.
One — The capsule · 1969–1971

Untethering, read both ways at once: as loss, and as the only available exit.

Cut loose
Space Oddity
David Bowie · 1969

Major Tom drifts out past the capsule and decides not to come back, and the song refuses to tell you whether that is a death or a release. The ambiguity is the whole point and he never resolved it.

Meeting yourself
The Man Who Sold The World
David Bowie · 1970

A song about running into a self you thought you had finished with. Later carried to a much larger audience by somebody else's cover, which is its own kind of Bowie joke.

Turn and face it
Changes
Live, Hammersmith 1973 · song 1971

The anthem the zine is named for. It turns to face the adults doing the assessing and tells them not to say the kids will grow out of it — the children being spat on are perfectly aware of what is happening to them.

The next lot
Oh! You Pretty Things
David Bowie · 1971

Addressed to the generation arriving to replace the one in charge, and cheerfully unbothered about what that will cost them. Hunky Dory's most quietly threatening song.

Watching from outside
Life On Mars?
David Bowie · 1971

A girl with mousy hair at the pictures, watching a world she has no part in. The most precise song anyone has written about being on the outside of the story and looking up instead.

Two — Selves, worn in public · 1972–1974

The personae, made in the open and set down in the open. Authorship, not concealment.

The clock starts
Five Years
David Bowie · 1972

The opening of Ziggy Stardust, and an unusual way to start a record about a rock star: with a deadline, and a long list of ordinary people noticed on the way to it.

Someone's out there
Starman
David Bowie · 1972

An alien who has been watching and wants to meet the kids specifically. Performed on Top of the Pops in a way that a great many people have since described as the moment they understood something about themselves.

Freak, claimed
Moonage Daydream
David Bowie · 1972

He names himself the alligator, the space invader, the mama-papa — a pile-up of self-descriptions that refuse to settle into one. Reclamation by sheer accumulation.

The persona itself
Ziggy Stardust
Live · song 1972

A song about a made-up rock star, sung by a real one pretending to be him, describing his own downfall in the third person. The seams are left showing deliberately.

You're not alone
Rock 'n' Roll Suicide
David Bowie · 1972

The closing track, and the reason the album is not actually bleak: it ends by insisting, at volume and directly to you, that you are not alone. Read it as a keep-on-livin' song, because it is one.

The bolt
Aladdin Sane
David Bowie · 1973

The next self, and the source of the lightning bolt that a Belgian observatory would later join seven stars into. Mike Garson's piano solo is the sound of a persona coming apart on purpose.

Permission slip
Rebel Rebel
Live · song 1974

Four bars of guitar and a flat statement that your mother's inability to tell what you are is not your problem. The most useful thing on this rack to hand to a fifteen-year-old.

Three — Plastic soul, then Berlin · 1975–1979

The stretch where he was least well and made some of the best of it. Not a redemption arc.

Borrowed and admitted
Young Americans
Live · song 1975

He called this phase plastic soul himself, which is more honest than most artists manage about a genre shift. Borrowing, named as borrowing, is a different act from passing.

Hold on
Golden Years
David Bowie · 1975

Made during the period he later said he could barely remember, and it sounds like glamour with something badly wrong underneath. The instruction in the chorus is to hang on; take it literally.

Blue room, low stimulus
Sound and Vision
David Bowie · 1977

A pop single about sitting in an empty room waiting to be able to work again, with the vocal arriving a minute and a half late. As accurate a description of a recovery week as anything on this rack.

No words needed
Warszawa
David Bowie · 1977

Six minutes with Brian Eno and almost no language — the vocal is in no actual tongue. Here because not everything that needs saying is available in sentences, which is a fact this community knows well.

Just for one day
“Heroes”
David Bowie · 1977

Note the quotation marks in the title — they are his, and they are ironic. The heroism on offer lasts one day, is available to two ordinary people by a wall, and is not offered as a permanent identity. That limit is what makes it bearable.

Camp as a crowbar
Boys Keep Swinging
David Bowie · 1979

A song listing the privileges of being a boy, sung so archly that the list collapses. The video, in which he plays his own backing singers in drag and wrecks the illusion on camera, is the argument.

Four — Ashes, and the years of being enormous · 1980–1986

Where he becomes a stadium, and the strangeness gets harder to find and is still in there.

Revisiting the capsule
Ashes To Ashes
David Bowie · 1980

Eleven years on he goes back for Major Tom and finds him a junkie — a sequel that refuses to flatter the original. Very few artists return to their most beloved character in order to disappoint you about him.

Compulsory style
Fashion
David Bowie · 1980

Not a song celebrating fashion — a song about being told to turn to the left, turn to the right, and do it because everyone is doing it. Read it as a compliance song and it gets much colder.

Give love one more chance
Under Pressure
Queen & David Bowie · 1981

Improvised in a room in Montreux and never really rehearsed. Its subject is the ordinary unbearable weight of things, and its answer — love, one more go — is delivered by two people who are both, plainly, out of their depth and singing anyway.

The enormous years
Let's Dance
David Bowie · 1983

The biggest record he ever made, and the video is about the dispossession of Aboriginal Australians — a fact almost nobody dancing to it in 1983 registered. He put the difficult thing in the most popular place he ever had.

The alien, loved
Loving The Alien
David Bowie · 1984

From a record he later disowned, and the best thing on it. Loving what you have been told is foreign, while knowing the story you were handed about it is wrong — which is roughly the job description of this whole collection.

Nothing much, and everything
Absolute Beginners
David Bowie · 1986

A vast, unembarrassed love song from a period nobody rates, in which he admits he knows almost nothing about anything except this. Sincerity with no persona in front of it, which was rarer for him than strangeness.

Five — The long way back · 1996–2013

Including ten years of saying nothing at all, which turned out to be a decision rather than an ending.

Spaceboy again
Hallo Spaceboy
David Bowie & Pet Shop Boys · 1996

The Pet Shop Boys remix, which pulls the Major Tom thread back in and asks, more or less, whether the whole gender arrangement is not something you could simply decline. In 1996, on the radio.

Ten years of silence, broken
Where Are We Now?
David Bowie · 2013

Released with no warning on his sixty-sixth birthday after a decade of public silence that everyone had read as retirement. Quiet, slow, and about Berlin. He came back by being smaller, not louder.

Fame, examined
The Stars (Are Out Tonight)
David Bowie · 2013

Stars as predators who need to be watched to stay alive. Coming from him, at that point, it reads less as complaint than as a technical report from someone who has done the job for forty years.

Six — ★ · 2016

Released on his sixty-ninth birthday. He died two days later. Nearly nobody knew.

The collapsed kind
★ (Blackstar)
David Bowie · 2016

Ten minutes, jazz musicians, no interest at all in being a single. Not the bright burning kind of star — the collapsed kind, which is still massive and still present and bends everything near it.

Staged as a rising
Lazarus
David Bowie · 2016

He sings from a hospital bed, with buttons over his eyes, then backs into a wardrobe and closes the door. The video went up the day before the album and three days before he died. Nothing about the timing was accidental.

The plain one
Dollar Days
David Bowie · 2016

The least costumed thing he ever recorded: an admission that he is not going to see England again, delivered without a single persona standing in front of it. Forty-seven years after Major Tom, no capsule.

The last track
I Can't Give Everything Away
David Bowie · 2016

The final song on the final record, and its title is a refusal — after a career of bringing everything to the front, he keeps something back. The right place to stop, and he chose it.
