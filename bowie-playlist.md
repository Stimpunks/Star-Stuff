# Nobody Got His Eyes Right

Thirty-one songs across a career — and the two things everyone knows about David Bowie that are not true. Both of them make the difference tidier than it was. The companion rack to [Turn and Face the Strange](https://starstuff.earth/bowie-zine.html), Zine No. 12.

Playlist & essay · Sound

One

## He did not have different-coloured eyes

This is the most-looked-at face in the history of pop music, and the single fact everybody repeats about it is wrong.

Bowie is very widely said to have had **heterochromia** — two irises of different colours. He did not. Both of his irises were the same colour. What he had was **anisocoria**: two pupils of different sizes, one of them permanently open.

In **February 1962**, when he was fifteen, his schoolfriend **George Underwood** punched him in the left eye during a row over a girl. A fingernail caught the eyeball, and the muscles that contract the iris were left paralysed. From then on **the left pupil could not close.** A pupil stuck wide is a hole full of shadow, so the left eye photographs darker — and next to a right eye whose pupil is doing its ordinary job, the pair reads as two different colours. That is the whole illusion.

*Iris colour is drawn identical in all four, because it was. The apparent difference appears **only in light bright enough to make a working pupil close** — which is to say under stage lights and camera flashes, which is where nearly every photograph of him was taken. *Schematic, not a portrait.**

So the correction is not pedantry about a word. **It changes what kind of thing the famous feature was.** Heterochromia is a variation you are born with. Anisocoria from a punch is *an injury*, and this one had consequences: a pupil that cannot contract cannot limit the light coming in, and Bowie was reported to have had impaired depth perception for the rest of his life. The look that a million people have called otherworldly is one eye working and one eye damaged.

Glamour is not an accommodation.

Nobody ever seems to ask what a fixed pupil is like under a follow spot.

He and Underwood stayed friends for the rest of his life, and Underwood — a painter — went on to make artwork for Bowie's records. Bowie himself said the eye gave him an interesting appearance and was, on balance, useful to him. **Both things are allowed to be true.** He made an injury into a signature, and it was still an injury, and neither half of that cancels the other.

Two

## And the constellation is not a constellation

The second thing everyone knows is nearly as widely repeated and fails in the same direction.

In January 2016, days after he died, the **MIRA public observatory** in Belgium and the radio station **Studio Brussel** announced a constellation for Bowie: seven stars near Mars, arranged in the lightning bolt from the *Aladdin Sane* sleeve. It is a genuinely lovely gesture and thousands of people posted about it.

It is not a constellation. **There are 88 constellations, the International Astronomical Union fixed the list in the 1920s, and it has not changed since.** What MIRA made was an *asterism* — a line figure, a way of joining dots — and to their credit they said so themselves, describing it as an invented Bowie-asterism rather than an addition to the sky's official furniture. Nobody can register a constellation. Nobody can sell you a star, either, whatever the certificate says.

Both errors lean the same way

Notice what these two have in common, because it is the reason this page exists rather than just a rack of songs. **Neither mistake is random.** Heterochromia is prettier than a punch in the eye. An official constellation is grander than a radio station's tribute. Both errors take something real — a damaged iris, a nice gesture by a small observatory — and **round it up into something more elegant, then travel further than the truth did.**

That is the exact mechanism [Too Good to Check](https://starstuff.earth/too-good-to-check.html) is about, and it is the reason we keep a [field card](https://starstuff.earth/six-ways-broadside.html) for it. A claim that flatters its subject gets less scrutiny, not more.

And there is a cost that is not just tidiness. **When the story about somebody's difference is more beautiful than the difference, the practical part stops getting discussed.** Sixty years of writing about that eye, and almost none of it about light sensitivity, or depth perception, or what it takes to perform under those conditions. The myth is a very comfortable place to stop.

Three

## What he actually did was set selves down in public

The argument about Bowie and difference is made at length in [Zine No. 12](https://starstuff.earth/bowie-zine.html), so this is the compressed version plus one thing the zine does not have.

The zine's claim is that Bowie inverts what masking means for the rest of us. Many of us wear the involuntary kind — kept up to pass as normal, kept up until it costs something. **His masking was authorship: selves made, shown in full view, and then put down.** Ziggy, Aladdin Sane, the Thin White Duke, Major Tom. Unmasking, on that reading, does not have to mean excavating one true self from underneath. It can mean the freedom to be several things out loud and mean all of them.

The thing worth adding is that he demonstrated the *putting down* as literally as anyone ever has. On **3 July 1973**, at the Hammersmith Odeon, he told a sold-out room it was the last show they would ever do — and killed Ziggy on stage, mid-tour, at the height of it. **Most of the band found out at the same time as the audience.**

The popular telling is a little too clean

You will often read that he told *nobody*. Not quite: **Mick Ronson knew**, and by some accounts the stage manager was told shortly before, and the road crew were in on it. It was most of the band who were blindsided, not all of it.

We are correcting a detail that makes the anecdote marginally less punchy, on a page whose whole first half is about stories getting rounded up. It would be a poor look to do the same thing ourselves two sections later.

A persona you can end on a Tuesday in July is a persona you were authoring. That is the difference between a costume and a diagnosis, and it is why the zine reads his career as *direction* rather than deficit.

Four

## The ending was written down first

And then, at the very end, he did the same trick one last time, in public, with the timing controlled to the day.

**★** was released on **8 January 2016**, his sixty-ninth birthday. The video for *Lazarus* — in which he sings from a hospital bed and then backs into a wardrobe and out of sight — went up the day before. He died on **10 January**. Almost nobody outside his circle knew he had been ill.

He made *Blackstar* for us, his parting gift. I knew for a year this was the way it would be.

Tony Visconti, his producer, on 11 January 2016 — the day after.

What that means for this collection is specific. The [L★S Playlist](https://starstuff.earth/ls-playlist.html) already notes that *Blackstar* is not the bright burning kind of star but **the collapsed kind — still massive, still present**. Set beside the eye, the arc finishes an argument the whole career was making: *the parts of you that got damaged, and the parts you decided to author, end up in the same body of work, and you do not have to sort them into the good ones and the bad ones before you are allowed to be here.*

That is what we mean by star stuff, said as plainly as we can say it. Not the difference redeemed. The difference included.

**Not:**

- **Not a diagnosis.** Anisocoria is a documented physical injury with a date and a named cause. We are not reading anything else into his mind, his neurology, or his sexuality from his songs, and nothing on this page should be cited as though we had.
- **Not damage as the source of the art.** The punch did not make him Bowie. Suffering-as-fuel is one of the oldest and laziest stories about difference, and it conveniently excuses never fixing anything.
- **Not the injury erased either.** The opposite error, and the more common one here: calling the eye *iconic* and stopping. It cost him something, permanently, and the celebration usually skips that part.
- **Not one true self, located and held still.** The zine's refusal and this page's too — *be yourself* as an instruction to pick one and stop is not liberation.
- **Not weird as a phase.** Difference is not a stage on the way to normal.
- **Not a correction offered as debunking.** We are not scoring points off people who said heterochromia; nearly everyone did, including publications with fact-checkers. The point is how a flattering claim travels, not that anyone was foolish.

### Sources & credits

**The eyes.** Bowie had **anisocoria**, not heterochromia: both irises the same colour, the left pupil permanently dilated after **George Underwood** punched him in February 1962 and a fingernail scratched the eyeball, paralysing the muscles that contract the iris. Traced to Kevin Hunt, *[The remarkable story behind David Bowie's most iconic feature](https://theconversation.com/the-remarkable-story-behind-david-bowies-most-iconic-feature-52920)* (*The Conversation*, 2016), which is explicit that the different-coloured-eyes reading is an illusion produced by the fixed pupil. **The impaired depth perception is reported rather than primary-confirmed**, and is marked as reported on the page; Hunt does not make that claim. The figure is schematic and says so.

**The constellation.** The *Stardust for Bowie* tribute — seven stars near Mars in the shape of the *Aladdin Sane* bolt — was announced in January 2016 by the **MIRA public observatory** (Belgium) with **Studio Brussel**. MIRA described it as an invented asterism, not an official constellation. **The IAU's list of 88 constellations was fixed in the 1920s and has not changed.** We deliberately do not reproduce the seven star names: the versions circulating in press coverage are internally inconsistent (one list gives both “Spica” and “Alpha Virginis,” which are the same star), so we cite the gesture and not the catalogue.

**Ziggy's retirement.** Hammersmith Odeon, **3 July 1973**. The page says *most* of the band rather than the band: Mick Ronson knew, and by several accounts the stage manager and road crew did too. **Blackstar / ★** released **8 January 2016** (first-release date confirmed against a release database, not recalled), the *Lazarus* video the day before, and Bowie died on **10 January 2016**. Tony Visconti's “parting gift” statement is from his own public post on **11 January 2016**.

**Songs and years.** All 31 embeds were resolved through the oEmbed endpoint before publishing, so each card is confirmed to point at the recording it names. Years are the song's own, taken from album first-release dates in a release database rather than from memory: *Space Oddity* 1969; *The Man Who Sold The World* 1970; *Hunky Dory* 1971; *Ziggy Stardust* 1972; *Aladdin Sane* 1973; *Diamond Dogs* 1974; *Young Americans* 1975; *Golden Years* single 1975; *Low* and *“Heroes”* 1977; *Lodger* 1979; *Scary Monsters* 1980; *Under Pressure* single 1981; *Let's Dance* 1983; *Tonight* 1984; *Absolute Beginners* single 1986; *Hallo Spaceboy* single 1996; *The Next Day* 2013; *★* 2016. Where a card names a live version, the year given is the song's, not the performance's.
