One Body at a Time

David Byrne from 1977 to Who Is the Sky? — twenty-five songs, and the argument that runs through all of them. Repetition is the mechanism. Subtraction is an access strategy. The room fills up.

A Sound rack · companion to Zine No. 66
One

He was not being weird at you

The standard account of early Talking Heads is that the singer was doing something clever: art-school irony, a put-on, a nervous affect adopted for effect.

Byrne has written his own account, and it is less flattering and more interesting. Looking back at himself auditioning at CBGB, he describes being as twitchy and Aspergery a stage presence as I was in those days, and then does something almost nobody does about their own early work — he declines to call it either genuine or fake:

My stage presence wasn’t fake, as weird as it looks to me in retrospect, but it wasn’t altogether unconsciously oddball either.

David Byrne, How Music Works (2012), p. 44

That is a better description of most of our public selves than either of the options usually on offer. Not a mask and not an unmediated outpouring — a person operating with the equipment available, in front of people, on purpose, and not entirely sure afterwards which parts were chosen.

Which is why the ordinary reading — he was being weird at you — is the one this rack sets aside. And it is worth saying plainly that Byrne’s own statements about being Autistic are hedged: he wrote that he diagnosed himself with a very mild (I think) form of Asperger’s syndrome, and he has said he never thought of it as a disability. He did not come out. He wrote a sentence with a qualifier in it, and the qualifier did not travel. Zine No. 66 spends a spread on exactly that.

Why this rack exists anyway

Because the argument does not need a diagnosis. Everything below is about what the records do — how they are built, what they ask of a body, and what got removed to make them playable by the person who made them. None of that depends on a label, and none of it is ours to assign.

Two

The loop is the point

The urge to move to music is measurable, and it is agreed upon across listeners to a surprising degree.

Petr Janata, Stefan Tomic and Jason Haberman took “groove” — a word musicians use and researchers had mostly avoided — and got it to hold still (Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2012). How strongly a passage compels movement turns out to be consistent between people, which means specific auditory features are unusually good at engaging the motor system. They describe the result as a pleasurable drive toward action.

And the feature doing most of that work is repetition. A figure has to come round again before a body can meet it rather than chase it. Prediction needs a period.

So look at how Remain in Light (1980) was actually made. Instruments recorded largely one at a time, each part a short figure held without developing, stacked into looping grooves — drummer Chris Frantz described them as loops performed live. The influence was named openly: Fela Kuti and West African polyrhythm, with Brian Eno producing. The Library of Congress put the record in the National Recording Registry in 2016.

One player, holding one small figure, for a very long time, while it stacks up into something enormous.

which is also a description of a thing many of us are told to stop doing

We are not claiming anyone in that studio thought of it as stimming, and the influences are West African before they are anything else — that credit is not ours to relocate. The narrow claim is the one that survives: the technique is sustained repetition of a single small figure, and it produced one of the most physically compelling records anybody has made.

Three

What the suit was for

Before the big suit, there was a list of things the band refused to do — and Byrne wrote the list out.

Early Talking Heads, in his own account: no show-off solos, no rock moves or poses, no pomp, no drama, no rock hair, no rehearsed patter. He announced the song titles, said thank you, and stopped. And the instruction to club lighting people was to turn the lights all on at the beginning and turn them off at the end.

He calls it a performance style defined by negatives, and describes the early band as less a band than an outline for a band — a sketch, the bare elements needed to lay out a song.

Read that as an access strategy, because functionally it is one. Strip out the unpredictable social apparatus of a rock show and what remains is a set of tasks a nervous person can execute. He did not become comfortable on stage. He removed the parts that required him to be.

And then the suit

Byrne was in Japan between tours, looking at Kabuki, Noh and Bunraku; the designer Jürgen Lehl told him drily that everything is bigger on stage. Byrne’s own stated reason for the result is the sentence the companion zine takes its title from — he wanted his head to appear smaller, and the simplest way was to make his body bigger, because music is very physical and often the body understands it before the head.

A costume whose proportions subordinate the head to the body. It is a claim about where understanding happens, worn where everyone can see it.

Four

Stop making sense is a permission slip

The film’s structure is the argument, and you can watch it happen in order.

It opens with one man alone on a bare stage with a boombox, singing a song whose narrator cannot face up to the facts and cannot relax. Then a second musician walks on. Then a third. The set is assembled in front of you by visible crew. By the end the stage is full and the man who could not relax is dancing.

Nobody fixed him. The room filled up.

There is measured work underneath this. Moving in synchrony with somebody changes how you regard them: Michael Hove and Jane Risen found that in-phase coordination with a partner predicted liking (Social Cognition, 2009), and Scott Wiltermuth and Chip Heath found people who had moved in synchrony cooperated more afterwards, even at personal cost (Psychological Science, 2009).

The honest size of it

A 2017 meta-analysis (Mogan, Fischer & Bulbulia, Zeitschrift für Psychologie) finds the synchrony-and-prosociality effect real but modest. It is a tendency, not a lever. And a finding about coordination is not a moral argument: nothing here obliges any room to include anybody. We would rather say that than let a good sentence do a mechanism’s work.

What it does support is less romantic and more useful: shared timing is a route into a group that does not run through conversation. For a lot of us that is not a curiosity — it is the difference between a room we can be in and one we cannot.

Which is what the title of the film is, once you stop reading it as whimsy. Stop making sense is not an instruction to be nonsense. It is a permission slip. You are not required to arrive legible. The band will start anyway, and the room will fill up around you, and your body will have worked it out well before you can say what it worked out.

  • Not the superpower deal. Byrne has used the word about himself, on camera, and that is his to use. Promoted into a standard, it becomes a fee the rest of us get billed — and most of us cannot pay it.
  • Not the tortured-artist deal either. He refused that one in print, citing and then declining the mad-genius myth. So do we.
  • Not a diagnosis. We are not diagnosing him and we are not upgrading his hedge. He said what he said.
  • Not celebrity as proof. One person with a stage getting through is not evidence the system works. It is one data point about who it lets through.
One — Nervous, and saying so · 1977–1979

The band as an outline of a band. Almost everything is subtracted, and what is left is legible enough to play.

Tense and nervous
Psycho Killer
Talking Heads · 1977

The song the film opens with, alone, to a drum machine. Read as a portrait of somebody who cannot face up to the facts and cannot relax, it is also the most-quoted description of a bad day in this whole collection.

A small life, defended
Don't Worry About the Government
Talking Heads · 1977

Civil servants, highways, a nice apartment, people he likes. A song that wants an ordinary manageable life and is not embarrassed about it — which in 1977 was far stranger than rage.

Language, refused
I Zimbra
Talking Heads · 1979

The words are Hugo Ball's 1916 Dada sound poem, so the lyric means nothing on purpose and the rhythm carries the whole load. The first sign of where the next record was going.

A bar where nothing happens
Heaven
Talking Heads · 1979

Heaven is a bar where the band plays your favourite song all evening, and nothing ever happens. Offered as tedium and received by a great many of us as a description of relief.

Overload
Memories Can't Wait
Talking Heads · 1979

A party in your head that you cannot leave and cannot turn down. There are not many songs that get sensory and cognitive overwhelm this exactly right, and fewer that decline to resolve it.

Function, under pressure
Life During Wartime
Talking Heads · 1979

No time for dancing or lovey-dovey: an inventory of what gets dropped when survival takes the whole budget. Anyone who has run out of capacity by Tuesday knows this list.

Two — The loops · 1980–1981

One figure each, held without developing, stacked. This is the section the argument rests on.

The heat goes on
Born Under Punches (The Heat Goes On)
Talking Heads · 1980

The opening of Remain in Light and the clearest demonstration of the method: no part of it is going anywhere on its own, and all of it together is going somewhere considerable.

Facts, unsorted
Crosseyed and Painless
Talking Heads · 1980

Facts all come with points of view, facts don't do what you want them to. A song about the uselessness of information without a frame, running on a groove that refuses to give you one.

Everything at once
The Great Curve
Talking Heads · 1980

The most layered thing on the record — several simultaneous parts, none of them the lead. If you want to hear what a stack of held figures sounds like at full height, it is this.

How did I get here
Once in a Lifetime
Talking Heads · 1980

A man asking how he arrived inside his own life. The answer the song gives is water flowing underground — the ordinary substrate, moving whether or not anybody named it.

Found sound
America Is Waiting
David Byrne & Brian Eno · 1981

From My Life in the Bush of Ghosts, built on found recordings over loops. The record's use of uncredited sampled voices was contested at the time and is worth knowing about rather than skipping past.

Three — The suit · 1983–1984

The same subtraction, at scale, with the apparatus deliberately left showing.

The single
Burning Down the House
Talking Heads · 1983

Their biggest hit, and its origin is a chant Chris Frantz brought back from a Parliament-Funkadelic show. The band built the song out of a crowd noise.

Home as a person
This Must Be the Place (Naïve Melody)
Talking Heads · 1983

He called it naïve because the parts are played simply and by the wrong hands. It is the tenderest thing he wrote, and its claim is that home is not a place but whoever you are standing next to.

Bodies arriving
Slippery People
Live · song 1983

In the film this is where the stage is filling up, and the song's call-and-response is the mechanism doing its work out loud: nobody is leading, everybody is locking in.

The title, said out loud
Girlfriend Is Better
Live · song 1983

The song the film's title comes from — stop making sense arrives inside it, in the big suit. Not a demand for nonsense. A permission slip.

Four — After the band · 1985–1989

The pop years, and then out into music that was never going to chart.

Going nowhere, cheerfully
Road to Nowhere
Talking Heads · 1985

A gospel-choir opening, a marching band, and the flat news that we are on a road to nowhere. The invitation in the chorus is come on inside — which is the same move the film makes.

Dissolving
And She Was
Talking Heads · 1985

A woman lies down in a field and floats out of her own body over the rooftops. One of the very few songs that treats dissociation as something other than a symptom.

Wild, wild life
Wild Wild Life
Talking Heads · 1986

From True Stories, Byrne's film about ordinary people in a Texas town treated with complete seriousness and no condescension. The song is the party at the middle of it.

The reversal
(Nothing But) Flowers
Talking Heads · 1988

A man in a rewilded world missing the shopping malls, with Johnny Marr on guitar. It refuses to let you feel simply good about either the parking lot or the meadow.

Somewhere else entirely
Make Believe Mambo
David Byrne · 1989

Rei Momo, recorded with Latin American players and arrangers who are named in the credits. Whatever you think of the borrowing, it was done in the room with the people whose music it was.

Five — Collaborations, and the long groove · 2002–2012

Two decades of working with other people, which is its own argument about how he operates.

Lazy, reclaimed
Lazy
X-Press 2 featuring David Byrne · 2002

A dance record in which he declares himself lazy and entirely comfortable about it. Handed to a community that gets called lazy for having a disability, it reads differently than it was probably meant to.

Overtones
Strange Overtones
David Byrne & Brian Eno · 2008

Back with Eno, nearly thirty years on, in a song about writing a song. The title is doing the same job the harmonics do — the character lives in what was not asked for.

Two, in step
Who
David Byrne & St. Vincent · 2012

Love This Giant, built for brass, with Annie Clark as a full collaborator rather than a guest. Watch the choreography: two people who move oddly, moving oddly together on purpose.

Six — The room he ended up building · 2018–2025

Twelve barefoot people, no cables, nothing hidden. Thirty-five years to arrive back at the same idea, larger.

Everybody, in
Everybody's Coming to My House
David Byrne · 2018

From American Utopia. Byrne has said that a Detroit school choir sang it back to him with the opposite feeling — as welcome rather than anxiety — and that their reading was better than his.

Still going
Everybody Laughs
David Byrne · 2025

From Who Is the Sky?, and the reason this rack does not end in the eighties. Nearly fifty years after the CBGB audition, still working, still moving oddly, still surrounded by people.
