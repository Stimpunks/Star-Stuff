Lived experience · the warrant

Two nights, six feet away

Twice. Once in a small club, standing about six feet from him. Once in a concert hall. Both times, transcendent.

And Stop Making Sense so many times the count is gone. He has always been an ambassador of weird — kindred, neurokin, long before any interview said so out loud. His grooves are made for stim dancing and for spontaneous self-expression through movement, which is a thing a body does before anybody asks it to mean something.

That recognition is where this zine starts, and it is worth being precise about what kind of evidence it is. It is not a diagnosis and it is not a claim about him. It is the ordinary experience of watching somebody move the way you move and understanding that you are not the only one.

Three other writers got here independently — Aisling Walsh, Raine Petrie, Anna Lina Litz — all in 2023, all reading the same film the same way. When several of us look at one performance and see our own nervous system, that convergence is data of a kind, even though it proves nothing about the man.

Recognition is the oldest instrument we have, and it was working long before anyone published a word about it.

What he wrote · what arrived

“I think”

He did not come out. He wrote a sentence, and the sentence had a hedge inside it.

Years later I diagnosed myself as having a very mild (I think) form of Asperger’s syndrome.

David Byrne, How Music Works (2012), p. 37 — “My Life in Performance”

Every load-bearing word there is a qualifier. I diagnosed myself. Very mild. And then, inside its own parenthesis, I think — a man doubting his own self-assessment in the middle of stating it.

What travelled was “David Byrne is autistic.” One widely-read essay upgraded it to proudly identifies as autistic. Elsewhere he has said he has never thought of it as a disability. None of that is a lie, and nobody set out to distort him. The hedge just doesn't survive the trip.

And here is the part that makes this a spread rather than a footnote: the same failure runs the other way through the same paragraph. Byrne follows his sentence by citing a 1994 study — 69 percent of creative people, he reports, had mental disorders. “That’s a lot of nutters!”

We went and read it. Felix Post, British Journal of Psychiatry 165(1), 1994. The figure is real: 69.4% had “episodic psychiatric conditions.” The next sentence says that in just over half of those — 35.4% — they caused no serious disability at all.

The qualification was printed in the sentence immediately after the number, and it did not travel.the same fault, twice, in one paragraph

There is more that didn’t travel. Post picked his 291 men by “the availability of sufficiently adequate biographies” — the cohort was selected by how much had been written about them, then diagnosed from what was written, by DSM–III–R, after they were dead. His own abstract reports that these men were mostly “emotionally warm, with a gift for friendship and sociability,” and that severe psychoses were probably less frequent than the general population would predict.

So the study largely undercut the mad-genius myth, and its headline number went out and fed it. Byrne read the number, not the next sentence — and then, remarkably, refused the myth anyway, on his own, in the same breath: he writes that he hopes very much one does not have to be unwell to be creative.

He got the ethics right while the citation was already degraded. That is not a small thing, and it is why this page treats him as a reader in good faith rather than a source of error.

Hedges don't travel. Numbers do. This is why we publish our own corrections instead of hoping.see also: Too Good to Check

Lived experience · the baseline

Leaping up, then back
into the shell

The sentence that follows the self-diagnosis is the one nobody quotes, and it is the better sentence.

Leaping up in public to do something wildly expressive and then quickly retreating back into my shell seemed, well, sort of normal to me.

David Byrne, How Music Works (2012), p. 37

Read what that actually says. Not I overcame my shyness. Not performing cured me. The oscillation itself — enormous expression, then withdrawal, then expression again — is described as his baseline. Sort of normal. He even stops to worry that normal is the wrong word, and keeps it, because it worked.

Most of us have been handed the opposite reading of that same pattern. The leap gets called attention-seeking and the retreat gets called shutting down, and both get written up in the same file as evidence of the same deficit. Byrne describes the whole cycle as one working arrangement — which is what it is, when nobody is grading it.

And this is the shape of a great deal of Autistic life that gets pathologised from outside: not a failure to regulate, but a rhythm of regulation. Big output, then recovery. The recovery is not the system breaking down. The recovery is part of how the output is possible at all.

Asked in an interview about how he and the people he works with are, he put it about as plainly as it can be put.

Whatever we are, we don’t know how to be another way.David Byrne, in conversation about being autistic — video, at 1:44

Not a boast and not an apology. A description of the situation, offered without either. There is no other way available, so the question of whether this way is acceptable was never really the interesting one.

Attention · the settled fact

A groove is a loop with
a body attached

Here is the checkable thing this zine is built on, and it is about repetition.

The urge to move to music is measurable, and it is remarkably consistent between people. Petr Janata, Stefan Tomic and Jason Haberman — Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2012 — treated “groove” as a psychological construct and got it to hold still: how strongly a given passage compels movement is agreed upon across listeners, which means particular auditory features are especially good at engaging the human motor system. They describe what it produces as a pleasurable drive toward action.

Two things follow, and neither is a metaphor. Movement to music is not decorative — it is a coupling between hearing and moving. And the feature that does most of the work is repetition: a figure has to come round again before a body can meet it.

Repetition is not the deficit. Repetition is the mechanism. A thing must recur to be predictable, and it must be predictable before you can move with it rather than after it.

Which is roughly how Remain in Light (1980) was made. Talking Heads and Brian Eno built the record out of short repeated cells — instruments recorded largely one at a time, layered into looping grooves, drawing openly on Fela Kuti and West African polyrhythm. Chris Frantz described the rhythm parts as loops performed live. The Library of Congress added the album to the National Recording Registry in 2016.

One player, holding one figure, for a long time, while it stacks up into something enormous. The record is made of stims.

Nobody involved described it that way, and we are not claiming they would. What we are claiming is narrower and harder to argue with: the technique is sustained repetition of a single small figure, and that is a thing plenty of us are told to stop doing.what the fact does and doesn't carry

Attention → relational · his own paragraph

The same predictor,
pointed at a beat

Three hundred pages later in the same book, Byrne gets to the argument himself — and puts both halves of it in one paragraph.

He is writing about prediction: that when we watch an action or a gesture we already have a good idea what is coming next. Then he says, in his own case, that some people on the spectrum might not intuit all of that as easily, and that he is sure he is not alone in having been accused of missing what friends thought were obvious cues or signals.

And then, without changing paragraph, he turns the same machinery on music: we sense the rise and fall of a melody, a repetition, a musical build, and we form expectations about where it is going — expectations that get confirmed, or slightly redirected. He notes Daniel Levitin’s point that too much confirmation bores us and little variations keep us alert.

Look at what he has just done. The faculty people told him was faulty in the room is the faculty he says the music runs on. Same prediction, same expectation, same reward for a small deviation from an established pattern — declared a deficit when the pattern is a face and a talent when the pattern is a bar of music.

He didn’t get a worse predictor. He got one that locks onto a beat instead of a face.the extension is ours; the paragraph is his

That is our step past him, and we mark it as ours. Prediction pointed at rhythm rather than at social signal is not a lesser instrument, and it is not a compensation for one. It is the same instrument, aimed somewhere that will actually hold still long enough to be predicted.

And we decline his mechanism. Byrne credits all of this to mirror neurons, which was the best-known popular account available in 2012. It has not held up. Antonia Hamilton’s systematic review of 25 studies (Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, 2013) found the data mixed with no overall evidence of abnormal mirror responses in autism, and Yates & Hobson revisited the same question in Autism (2020) and reached no better news for it. His observation survives. The wiring he hung it on does not.the broken-mirror account of autism is not something we will repeat

We are saying this because we are using his paragraph, not despite it. A page that quoted the good half and quietly kept the bad half would be doing the exact thing this zine spent a spread complaining about.

Environment · subtraction as method

A performance style
defined by negatives

Before the famous stage, there was a list of things they refused to do — and Byrne wrote it out as a list.

Early Talking Heads at CBGB, by his own account: no show-off solos. No rock moves or poses. No pomp, no drama, no rock hair. No rehearsed patter — he announced song titles, said thank you, and stopped. And no rock lights: their instruction to club lighting people was to turn them all on at the beginning and turn them off at the end.

He calls it, exactly, a performance style defined by negatives, and says the early band was less a band than an outline for a band — a sketch, the bare elements needed to lay out a song, like an architectural drawing where you have to imagine where the walls go.

This is the convention this whole collection runs on, arrived at independently and thirty years early: our zines carry a “Not:” set — the traps a piece refuses, named on its face. Byrne built a band that way.

And subtraction turns out to be an access strategy, whether or not anyone called it that. Strip the unpredictable social apparatus of a rock show — the patter, the poses, the lighting cues you have to hit — and what is left is a set of tasks a nervous person can actually execute. He did not become comfortable. He removed the parts that required him to be.

The room was not made welcoming. It was made legible. Those are different projects, and the second one is the one you can build.

Then, in 1984, the same method with the volume up. Stop Making Sense (dir. Jonathan Demme) shows you the crew. The set gets assembled in front of you. The band lit their own show. Nothing is hidden, because hiding the apparatus is the thing that makes an apparatus frightening.

Which brings us to the suit. Byrne was in Japan between tours, looking at Kabuki, Noh and Bunraku; his friend the designer Jürgen Lehl told him, drily, that everything is bigger on stage. Byrne’s own stated reason for the result is the one this zine took its title from — he wanted his head to appear smaller, and the simplest way was to make his body bigger, because music is very physical and often the body understands it before the head.

He built a costume whose proportions subordinate the head to the body. That is not a gag. It is a claim about where understanding happens, worn where everyone can see it.

Relational · regulation

One body at a time

The architecture of Stop Making Sense is the argument, and you can watch it happen.

It opens with one man, alone, on a bare stage, with a boombox — playing a song whose narrator cannot face up to the facts and cannot relax. Then a second musician arrives, and a third, and the set builds up around them piece by piece, until the stage is full of people and the man who could not relax is dancing.

Nobody fixed him. The room filled up.

And there is a measured thing underneath that, which is why it gets a spread rather than a sigh. Moving in synchrony with another person changes how you regard them. Michael Hove and Jane Risen found that the degree of in-phase coordination with a partner predicted how much people liked them (Social Cognition, 2009). Scott Wiltermuth and Chip Heath found that people who had acted in synchrony cooperated more afterwards, across three experiments, even at personal cost (Psychological Science, 2009).

So affiliation is not only something a group decides about you. Some of it is something a group does with you, in time, through the body, before anyone has formed an opinion.

Belonging is not only a verdict the room passes on you. Part of it is a phase-lock the room does with you.
And the honest size of it: a 2017 meta-analysis of the synchrony-and-prosociality literature (Mogan, Fischer & Bulbulia, Zeitschrift für Psychologie) finds the effect real but modest — not a lever, a tendency. Nor does any of this oblige a room to include you: a finding about coordination is not a moral argument, and we are not going to dress it as one. A rhyme with a measurement attached is still a rhyme.what the synchrony literature will and won't carry

What it does support is a much less romantic and more useful claim: shared timing is a route into a group that does not run through conversation. For a great many of us that is not a curiosity. It is the difference between an evening we can attend and one we cannot.

This collection has two zines on the distinction — shared-air space and shared-signal space, Helen Edgar’s coinages. A stage where twelve people move together and nobody has to make small talk is a shared-signal space with a lighting rig.

Systems · the part that costs money

Two escapes from the
same trap

In 2012 he refused the demons. Later, on camera, he reached for a superpower. Both are escapes from the same room, and neither is a rule for anybody else.

The refusal first, because it is in print: having quoted the mad-genius number, Byrne says he hopes very much that one does not have to be unwell to be creative, and that you can escape your demons and still tap the well. That is a man declining to pay for his work with a diagnosis.

Then the other direction. Asked about his own mind, he has said — of having his own experience rather than someone else’s brain — “I guess it’s my superpower, I can use this in my way.”

This site refuses superpower framing. We are not going to pretend otherwise, and we are not going to pretend he said something else. So: a person’s first-person account of their own mind belongs to them. He is describing what he has and what he does with it, hedged with a guess and closed with in my way. Read as an autobiography, that sentence is unobjectionable and rather modest.

What we refuse is its promotion. The moment a superpower becomes the reason you are tolerated, it is a toll. It means the ones who cannot demonstrate one owe an explanation, and the ones who can are only safe while the demonstrations keep arriving. Post’s 69 percent and the superpower are the same trade with the sign flipped: both make a person’s worth contingent on what their difference produces.

And then the part that is not about framing at all.

David Byrne got to be an auteur. The same nervous system, in a kid with no record deal, gets a behaviour plan.

He was twitchy and, in his own word, Aspergery, on a stage in front of Hilly Kristal, and what he got was a slot opening for the Ramones. Elsewhere, the identical presentation — the leap, the retreat, the flat affect, the one figure held too long — is written into a file, and the file recommends interventions.

The difference between the eccentric genius and the disordered child is not in the nervous system. It is in the budget, the postcode, and who is holding the pen. Byrne is not the exception that proves us wrong about pathologisation. He is what pathologisation looks like when somebody with a stage happens to escape it.

Not:Not the superpower deal. His sentence about himself is his. Turned into a standard, it becomes a fee we charge each other for existing — and most of us cannot pay it.
Not:Not the tortured-artist deal either. The mad-genius myth pays you in mystique and bills you in care. Byrne declined it in print and so do we.
Not:Not a retrospective diagnosis. We are not diagnosing David Byrne, and we are not treating a hedged self-description as a clinical finding. He said what he said.
Not:Not celebrity as proof. One famous person doing well is not evidence that a system works. It is one data point about who the system happens to let through.
Not:Not broken mirror neurons. We used his paragraph and dropped his mechanism, on purpose, and said so on the spread where we used it.

L★S The poor soul up there was just doing what he thought was right, given the skills and techniques available to him. So were you. So are you.

That clause is Byrne’s, from page 44, looking back at his own young self on a stage and deciding to be fair to him. It is also, word for word, the presumption of competence — applied retroactively, without theory, by someone who had no reason to know the term.

It is what we want said about every one of us, in every file, at every meeting: not he was inappropriate, not she was dysregulated, but that person was doing what they thought was right, with what they had. Start there and most of the paperwork changes.

And down to your star stuff is a measure of reach, not a reason. Not loved because you are made of carbon forged in a dying star — loved all the way down to it, past the diagnosis and the file and the phenotype, into the elements, which were manufactured in stars, and which the universe has never once sorted into acceptable and unacceptable atoms.
