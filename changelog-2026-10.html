October 2026

1 dated entry from October 2026 — pieces added, pieces revised, and every fact-check, corrections included. One month of the changelog.

This log is backfilled from the collection's full commit history and kept up from here. It records three kinds of change: pieces added, pieces substantially revised, and fact-check and attribution audits — including the errors we found in our own work and exactly how we fixed them. Small typo passes and styling tweaks are left out; anything that changes what a piece claims is in.

Why publish our corrections

We braid real science with ideas credited to named thinkers, so the facts and the attributions have to be right. We're human, and we've gotten things wrong — a paraphrase dressed as a Sagan quote, a Martin Luther King Jr. line credited to Baldwin, a forest-wide fungal network asserted as settled fact because it rhymed so well with mutual aid. Naming those in public is part of the method, not an embarrassment to bury.

The working guidelines and the full per-piece ledger live in FACTCHECK.md. If you spot an error, open an issue or reach us at stimpunks.org. Corrections are mutual aid.

Glimmer Wire, edition eight: nine papers read end to end, three of them read and then held

The weekly scan behind Glimmers, at its own page. Eight filed, eight held, three seeds. Median lag 19 days, range 0 to 236 — and the zero is literal: a dinosaur was described and reported on the same day, after waiting a hundred and two years in a museum.

NewEight filed — five verified, one contested, two plausible

Graded verified, all five read end to end at PubMed Central: Rogers et al. in Nature Communications, mapping 3D genome folding across squid, cuttlefish and octopus and coining regulatory entanglement for what they find — the accumulation of interdependencies until regions brought near each other “become embedded within nested regulatory architectures” — and then naming its cost, a hypothesis that the octopus lineage’s deeper entanglement “limit[s] the capacity for further genome expansion”; Fusar Bassini et al. in Nature, whose mass-spectrometry atlas of the mouse brain resolves 539 lipid territories that partly mirror the cell-type map and partly do not, because some take their chemistry from axon terminals belonging to cells elsewhere; Yu et al. in Science Advances, who located the residues deciding which blast-fungus effector a cereal immune receptor recognises and used them to build dual-specificity receptors that answer to effectors their parents could not see; Alcott et al. in Nature Communications, tracking phosphorus through two Great Oxidation glaciations and concluding that the feedback drove instability “persisting for over 200 million years” rather than a smooth rise; and Sanchez et al. in Nature Communications, who measured magnesium and silicon together in an ultra-hot Jupiter’s atmosphere and so gave the first empirical test of an assumption their own abstract calls “largely untested” — that a planet’s rock-forming ratios match its star’s. That one carries the longest lag this page has filed, 236 days.

Two are plausible on complete deposited abstracts, because neither publisher would open. Morrison et al. in Royal Society Open Science describe Invictibellatrix amberina, the first megaraptoran known from Europe, from a braincase the Natural History Museum acquired in 1924 — and the abstract’s methodological claim is the better half, that theropod braincases “are often regarded as morphologically and phylogenetically conservative” and are not. Yu et al. in Science record the lineage of a mouse in its own DNA, reconstructing from a single embryo “a time-calibrated, parsimony-supported, zygote-rooted phylogeny of 1.28 million transcriptionally profiled cells.” One is contested: Katlowitz et al. in Nature, recording a human hippocampus under propofol still discriminating oddball tones, improving at it over ten minutes, and carrying semantic and grammatical information about speech. The recording is solid; whether it settles anything about consciousness is what the paper’s own first sentence calls disputed, and its authors name propofol and the hippocampus as limits on their claim.

Fact-checkEight held, and three of the eight were read end to end first

The cleanest hold this page has made turns on a grammatical subject. A Nature paper titled “A Jurassic mammaliaform swimmer and transformation of the mammalian pharynx” ran as “this tiny jurassic predator helped pave the way for the human brain”. The brain appears exactly once in the coverage, in a co-author’s quoted inference three evolutionary steps long; the paper itself is closed, with no PubMed Central copy and its abstract elided by the publisher, so the only thing we could verify is that the headline’s sentence is not the title’s.

A second hold is about punctuation. A Light: Science & Applications paper we read in full ran under the headline “Physicists discover ‘astonishing’ property of light”. The word astonishing occurs six times on that page — in the <title>, in og:title, in the <h1> and three times in auto-generated citation blocks that repeat the headline — and nowhere in the body, in any quoted sentence, or in the paper. Quotation marks promise that somebody said the thing; the university’s own newsroom is unreachable from here, so we cannot say whether anyone did, and not being able to say is why it is held rather than filed or accused.

Two more of the familiar shape, and one that is ours rather than theirs. “Scientists make stunning discovery that could help explain why life exists” rests on a Science Advances paper of 25 August 2023 — a 1,141-day lag, and the third consecutive edition to hold an item carrying an old result in the present tense. “The secret to easier exercise as an adult may start in childhood” is fourteen weeks of limb loading in growing fowl, a real and striking 22% result that the abstract never extends to children, and the paper could not be read. And a National Science Review paper on the Moon’s two faces, read in full, is held because its authors write “we propose” where the headline writes “solved” — and because its sharpest sentence, that “the mantle displays no clear compositional dichotomy”, is a negative result the headline drops. Two further items — two cats with a Marfan variant, and an estimate of the Australian Indigenous population before invasion — are declined on our own grounds of register and standing rather than on any doubt about the work, and the page says so plainly.

Two claims checked against the papers and found to belong to the coverage alone. The word intelligence occurs zero times in the octopus genome paper, whose own phrase is “large and elaborately structured nervous systems, novel organs, and complex behaviours”. And habitable occurs three times in the exoplanet paper — twice inside an author’s institutional affiliation and once in a reference title — so the release’s line that the result helps investigate potentially habitable worlds has no basis in the work. A third case was filed rather than held and named on its face: the Great Oxidation release speaks of oxygen being “sustained” where the paper says instability, twice.

CorrectionWe nearly published that the DOI system was down. It was not

The reachability list on this page is built by probing each host, and the probe was wrong. Following redirects, a request to doi.org returns nothing, which reads exactly like a dead resolver — and doi.org was on its way onto the page as a failure. With redirects disabled it returns HTTP 302 and a 305-byte body carrying the correct target, every time. The resolver worked; the publisher at the end of the chain did not. A probe that follows a redirect reports the health of the last host and files it under the name of the first. The method is corrected, the correction is on the edition page in its own paragraph, and the lesson is the house’s own: our own claims are the unchecked ones.

A gate caught the other one before a reader could. The new cards on the collection page and the front page used a mono class copied from the edition page, which defines it, onto two pages that do not — an intention that would have silently not happened. check-classes.mjs failed both, which is the exact fault that check exists for. Fixed before shipping.

SiteA fourth identical byte count, a preprint the lag axis cannot see, and a second picture census

PubMed Central’s wrong-path home page measured 67,236 bytes for a fourth consecutive week, the same figure logged on 25 September, 2 October and 9 October. A genuine missing-article path returns a proper 404, so the fault is one search path rather than the archive. One of nine articles requested met reCAPTCHA at 200 in a 20,422-byte body, inside last week’s 20,416–20,485 band, and cleared on the third user agent. science.org’s 403 body measured 5,689 bytes against 5,657 last week; biorxiv.org returned 429 on every article path for a fourth week. Refused at the network layer: Royal Society, Nature, Oxford, Taylor & Francis, arXiv, Europe PMC, EBI, Unpaywall and UEA.

The lag axis’s standing blind spot showed itself, so it is reported rather than inferred. Searching Crossref for posted content under the lineage paper’s own title returns a bioRxiv preprint posted 30 July 2026, whose deposited abstract is the same text as the journal’s. The bar on the page reads 3 days; the work has been public for 73. The preprint could not be read either, so the comparison is between two abstracts and the page says so. No other filed item returned one.

The picture census was run a second time and deliberately not compared. Deduplicating eight ScienceDaily topic feeds gives 43 releases in the seven days to 16 October, of which 42 were fetched; 6 carry an image credited AI/ScienceDaily.com and 22 more a Shutterstock photograph — 28 of 42, two thirds whose picture of a finding comes from neither the research nor the institution. Last week’s 14-of-76 was built from a different sweep, and the two denominators are not subtractable; one method will be used from here. One filed item carries a machine-made image and is named in its own entry rather than quietly left out.

One open item is logged against our own pages. No. 84 opens its Great Oxidation spread with “the moment the accounting flips”. After the Alcott paper, moment is the wrong unit by two orders of magnitude. A re-reading against the paper rather than against our own summary is owed, and is deliberately not done in this pass.

2026 · October 9

Glimmer Wire, edition seven: five papers read end to end, every one of them through the same archive

The weekly scan behind Glimmers, at its own page. Six filed, six held, four seeds. Median lag 68.5 days, range 1 to 216 — and for the first time in four editions the lags are spread rather than clumped at the ends.

NewSix filed — four verified, one contested, one plausible

Graded verified, all four read end to end: Breedy et al. in ZooKeys, describing a new deep-sea octocoral family from seamounts off Isla del Coco — and reporting that three standard barcoding markers placed it in three different families, with mtMutS giving 100% ultrafast bootstrap support for a family of corals that have no axis, while phylogenomics over 153 loci and 37,654 base pairs backs a fourth reading; Horvath et al. in Nature Communications, who realised Hans Bethe’s 1931 strings — many-particle bound states that can exist only in one dimension — in caesium atoms held in thousands of one-dimensional tubes, identified by comparing an expansion inside the tubes against a release into three dimensions where the strings cannot survive; Lestina et al. in PeerJ, on a cavity-nesting wasp hollowing out the same Macaranga stems that house the trees’ mutualist ants, at 12 of 43 trees in oil palm against 1 of 41 in logged forest; and Majolo, Wakes & Ruta in Evolution Letters, who scored five kinds of aggression separately across 100 primate species and found the lethal kinds correlated with each other and with mild aggression not at all.

One is graded contested on its second claim rather than its first. Lole Durbin et al. in Science Advances show that a maximum age calibration for the origin of animals is an argument from absence, and test it by finding a second exceptionally preserved microfossil deposit — the Kheseen Biota of Mongolia, 550 to 526 million years old — that contains no animals either, at a time when animals demonstrably existed elsewhere. That part is well made. The ~800-to-700-million-year origin it supports is a clock estimate specialists will argue over for a decade, and the headline’s “200 million years earlier” appears in no abstract. One is plausible on a deposited abstract: Hobbs & Kranzusch in Science, reporting that bacterial CBASS immunity is triggered by the phage’s own prohead protease cutting an activation loop — the enzyme a phage needs to build its capsid. science.org returns 403 and there is no PubMed Central copy, so nothing but the publisher’s abstract is on the page.

Fact-checkSix held, four of them because the thing in the headline is not the thing in the paper

The one we most wanted was the one with three faults. A Communications Earth & Environment paper imaging a giant magnetofossil in three dimensions finds a single magnetic vortex optimised for sensing variation in field intensity — good evidence for an ancient navigational sense. But its Methods place the imaged particle in Palaeocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum sediments from IODP Hole U1403B, tens of millions of years younger than the 97 million years in the headline, which is the age of the oldest sediments in which such fossils are known at all; the paper attributes them to “(as yet unidentified) eukaryotes” where the coverage says animal; and its own key sentence begins “if confirmed”. Lag 345 days.

Three more holds of the same shape. A genetic-code story carried in the present tense is McGowan et al., PLOS Genetics, published 5 October 2023 — a ciliate in which UAA and UAG are reassigned to different amino acids, which is a fine result and a 1,098-day lag; the release concedes the date in its fourth paragraph and the headline does not. A Nature Communications X-ray study of proton-coupled electron transfer ran as “how nature moves energy so efficiently”; the object measured is a synthetic ruthenium polypyridyl model complex, and the release itself notes that its mechanism was already understood. And an Annals of Human Biology catalogue of 18 undescribed Drimolen fossils ran as “Homo erectus 200,000 years earlier than expected”; its own abstract says it tests existing hypotheses about H. erectus at ~2.0 Ma and that one specimen gives additional evidence for 2.04–1.95 Ma — a date published by Herries et al. in Science on 3 April 2020.

Two held for want of any source. A carcass-feeding study of zoo lions is exactly the kind of finding this site exists for — the behaviour was missing from the schedule, not from the animal — and it rests on three lions with no reachable text: sciencedirect.com refused, no abstract at Crossref, "abstract": null at Semantic Scholar, no PMCID. Its lag cannot even be stated. And a 30-year forestry result arrived as a researcher-written piece in The Conversation carrying no journal reference at all; we traced the paper ourselves from a link inside the piece and still could not read it, and the piece’s “win-win-win” frame is an argument its authors are making in public rather than a result.

SiteOne archive stood between this edition and nothing, and 14 of 76 news pictures were drawn by a machine

Every one of the five full texts read this week came from PubMed Central, and nowhere else was possible. Four of the five are CC BY — at Pensoft, PeerJ, Oxford and Nature — and all four of those publishers refused the connection outright. Last week this page wrote that an open licence is a permission rather than a road; this week the road was singular. Refused at the network layer: zookeys.pensoft.net, peerj.com, tandfonline.com, sciencedirect.com, ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, Europe PMC, EBI, OpenAlex, and both nobelprize.org addresses. Refused by the publisher: science.org with a 403, and nature.com with a 303 to an authentication endpoint, in front of CC BY articles. biorxiv.org returned 429 a third week running. PMC repeated both documented behaviours — the home page at 200 for a wrong path at the identical 67,236 bytes recorded on 25 September and 2 October, and reCAPTCHA at 200 for three of seven articles, cleared by retrying with another user agent.

A measurement of our own, and it is new. Of the 76 ScienceDaily releases carried in the seven days to 9 October, 14 lead with an image credited to a machine — thirteen Credit: AI/ScienceDaily.com and one Credit: NASA; background by AI/ScienceDaily.com — and 37 more lead with a Shutterstock stock photograph. That is 51 of 76 whose first picture of a result comes from neither the research nor the institution. The credit lines are honest and that is the entire defence; the ratio is the finding, and two of the six held items carry one. Last week the thing in the abstract slot was written by a model; this week the thing in the photograph slot was drawn by one. One week is a measurement and not a trend, and the page says so.

One fact is on the page marked unconfirmed, which is the honest alternative to cutting it. We wanted to note that Hans Bethe’s 1967 Nobel was for work on how stars produce energy — the nucleosynthesis this whole site is named out of — and could not confirm the citation at its source, because both nobelprize.org addresses are refused here. It is labelled as unconfirmed on the page and should not be quoted off it.

The lead list promised a ruling and got one. It had grown for three editions with nothing coming off it. One lead was worked — the Geology paper on the Zaonega Formation, whose deposited abstract reports a thermogenic origin for a 2-billion-year-old carbon signature many have read as global, with an apparent temperature gradient from 72 to 350 °C declining upward from a gabbro sill. The other two are closed unworked and named. And no new list was started, because a list that only grows is a way of looking thorough while deferring the work.

The shallow-clone fault was present for a third week and caught before anything was generated. This checkout arrived with 50 commits of 463, dating Bone Song to 13 September 2026 instead of 17 July. git fetch --unshallow ran first; no derived file was written against the truncated history.

Wired in: a card on the collection page and on the front page, prev/next edited on both editions, a sitemap entry added, and the collection’s prose counts re-derived by summing the seven edition tallies rather than incremented — seven editions, forty-nine filed, thirty-six held, twenty-one verified.

2026 · October 2

Glimmer Wire, edition six: six papers read end to end, and the first story held because its only summary was written by a machine

The weekly scan behind Glimmers, at its own page. Eight filed, six held, four seeds. Median lag 20 days, range 4 to 91 — less than a quarter of last week’s median.

NewEight filed — five verified, one contested, two plausible

Six versions of record were read in full, which is the best this page has managed — four at PubMed Central and two at PLOS. Graded verified: Sullivan, Gerstein & Kajiura in Integrative Organismal Biology, who flew a drone over wild blacktip sharks and found them turning away from a sound source from at least 62 m, with 71.5% of 165 responses initiated in the acoustic far field — where a shark, having no swim bladder, was long inferred to be unable to hear at all; Krzewińska et al. in Science Advances, who sequenced 142 ancient genomes from three Swedish cemeteries and found that children buried with adults were almost never closely related to them, at 12.0% of multiple burials at Västerhus and 12.5% at Sigtuna; Levy et al. in Autism Research, raising the estimated prevalence of Phelan–McDermid syndrome from 2.5–10 per million births to about 1 in 7,300 — a correction whose causes are referral rates, insurance criteria and an assay that misses half the cases; Postberg et al. in Science Advances, on 961 Cassini mass spectra showing Enceladus’ ice grains sorted into at least five salt chemistries by slow freezing and wall collisions on the way out; and Seiler et al. in PLOS One, who built a papyrus scroll, wrote on it in leaded ink, carbonised it in a low-oxygen furnace and read it back by X-ray tomography, so that an algorithm for the real Herculaneum scrolls can be marked against a known answer.

One item is graded contested on the method rather than on our access. The Bat1K consortium’s new bat phylogeny — 103 genomes, all 21 families, 44 pre-Quaternary fossils — places the origin of bats and of powered flight in Europe in the late Palaeocene. A continent of origin inferred from a fossil record that is best sampled in Europe is the kind of claim specialists argue over for a decade, and the paper hedges with probably where the coverage does not. The headline’s “65 million years ago” is in neither the abstract nor the university’s release, both of which say late Palaeocene — conventionally about 59.2 to 56.0 million years ago, so the headline is roughly six million years early and puts the origin at the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary rather than well after it.

Two rest on publisher-deposited abstracts: a revision of the Australian stick-insect genus Anchiale that takes two species to five while resurrecting one name from synonymy, sinking another and fixing three to designated lectotypes; and satellite tracking of nine yellow-morphotype green turtles in the Galápagos, none of which left the archipelago, and whose home ranges are “highly variable among individuals” by a spread roughly twice their own mean.

Fact-checkA new kind of refusal: a fluent summary, correctly labelled, written by a language model

Where a publisher elides an abstract, Semantic Scholar’s API returns "abstract": null and, in the same object, a tldr field holding fluent, specific, paragraph-shaped prose about the paper, generated by a language model. The archive names the model, which is to its credit and is the whole defence. For the Nature paper arguing that Cretaceous zhelestid mammals are zalambdalestoids — a group read for forty years off its teeth and reassigned on a nearly complete Gobi skeleton — that text was the only summary reachable from here, Crossref deposits none and nature.com is refused. It is not a source, and nothing about its shape says so. We did not quote it; the story is held rather than filed, and the page says why. A status code is not a source, a byte count is not a page, and a well-formed field in the right place is not an abstract.

Two holds turn on a number rather than a word, and in one of them the dropped figure is the one we would have led with. A PLOS Medicine study scoring brain ageing across nine conditions reports Alzheimer’s at d = 0.97 and tobacco use disorder at 0.72 — and autism at d = 0.06 (p = 0.36) and ADHD at 0.01 (p = 0.98), which is no difference at all. Its own abstract opens “PAD was consistently greater across disorders” and reaches those two exceptions four clauses later; the headline keeps the opening. We held it anyway, because filing it would mean accepting a predicted-age-difference as a measure of brain health in order to enjoy a null, and a page does not get to switch instruments depending on the answer. And a Psychological Medicine meta-analysis ran as “cannabis users were twice as likely to commit violence”: the factor of two is its cross-sectional figure, while its longitudinal estimate — the only design that can speak to order in time — is OR 1.17.

The other three holds: a review, a date and a route. A Biomolecules paper carried as a finding about mulberry and gut bacteria is a narrative review of preclinical models, and its own verbs say so. A MIND-diet cohort result ran on 30 September having been published on 17 March — a lag of 197 days, longer than every filed item on the page — and its “2.5 years” is an equivalence computed from a grey-matter volume slope, not a measurement of brain age. And a Nature paper on 1.75-to-1.4-billion-year-old eukaryote fossils is held because the only abstract we could obtain came from a third-party index rather than the publisher, and because its mitochondria claim is explicitly an inference from where the fossils occur.

SiteWhat refused, three open-access papers we could not open, and a shallow clone caught before it shipped

Two host behaviours repeated from last week, exactly. A wrong search path at PubMed Central again returned the PMC home page at HTTP 200 — 67,236 bytes, the identical figure recorded on 25 September, for four different queries a week apart. And PMC again served Google’s reCAPTCHA at 200 for every article on the first attempt; a retry with a different user agent returned all four papers in full. export.arxiv.org was refused while arxiv.org answered, as last week.

Refused at the network layer, named on the page: nature.com, frontiersin.org, academic.oup.com, onlinelibrary.wiley.com, doi.org, export.arxiv.org, ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, Europe PMC at both addresses, OpenAlex and Unpaywall. science.org answered with a real 403 and biorxiv.org with a 429. Three of this week’s refusals sit in front of openly licensed papers — the CC BY stick-insect revision, the CC BY turtle tracking and the CC BY bat phylogeny — which is the third consecutive week an open-access paper could not be opened from here. An open licence is a permission, not a road.

The shallow-clone fault from last week was checked for before anything was generated, and it was present. This checkout arrived with 50 commits of history rather than 461, and the test recorded on 25 September — ask git log --diff-filter=A for the creation date of Bone Song — returned 2026-09-12 instead of 2026-07-17. git fetch --unshallow first, generators afterwards; the derived files were never written against the truncated history. Last week this cost a 1,463-line diff that every gate passed. This week it cost one command, because the lesson had been written down.

One fault caught before publishing, and it is the third instance of the same one. The first draft of this edition’s collection and front-page cards used <span class="mono"> for a status code — and neither the front page nor the collection page defines .mono, so the text would have rendered in the body face with nothing to show for the markup. check-classes named it on both pages. The ledger already records this against edition four’s collection card and edition five’s changelog entry; a gate catching the same mistake three times is the gate working and the author not learning, and the fix is the same both previous times: <code>, which every page styles.

A new month is a new file. This entry opens the October archive page, copied from September with its <title>, canonical, meta, og:, twitter: and JSON-LD all rewritten for the month, prev/next edited on both pages, a sitemap row added and the index rebuilt from the month pages.

Wired in: a card on the collection page and on the front page, prev/next edited on both editions, a sitemap entry, and the collection’s prose counts re-derived by summing the six edition tallies rather than incremented — six editions, forty-three filed, thirty held, seventeen verified.
