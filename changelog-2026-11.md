# *November* 2026

1 dated entry from November 2026 — pieces added, pieces revised, and every fact-check, corrections included. One month of [the changelog](https://starstuff.earth/changelog.html).

---

This log is backfilled from the collection's full commit history and kept up from here. It records three kinds of change: **pieces added**, **pieces substantially revised**, and **fact-check and attribution audits** — including the errors we found in our own work and exactly how we fixed them. Small typo passes and styling tweaks are left out; anything that changes what a piece *claims* is in.

Why publish our corrections

We braid real science with ideas credited to named thinkers, so the facts and the attributions have to be right. We're human, and we've gotten things wrong — a paraphrase dressed as a Sagan quote, a Martin Luther King Jr. line credited to Baldwin, a forest-wide fungal network asserted as settled fact because it rhymed so well with mutual aid. Naming those in public is part of the method, not an embarrassment to bury.

The working guidelines and the full per-piece ledger live in [FACTCHECK.md](https://github.com/Stimpunks/Star-Stuff/blob/main/FACTCHECK.md). If you spot an error, [open an issue](https://github.com/Stimpunks/Star-Stuff/issues) or reach us at [stimpunks.org](https://stimpunks.org). **Corrections are mutual aid.**

## Glimmer Wire, edition twelve: eight papers read end to end, and a species removed from an explanation

[The twelfth edition](https://starstuff.earth/glimmer-wire-2026-11-13.html) of the weekly graded scan is live — **eight items filed, six held, three seeds**, lags from 13 to 192 days with a median of 57.5. **Eight versions of record were read end to end, the most in twelve editions and two more than the previous best**, because PubMed Central challenged none of the eight requests made of it. *That is luck rather than method, and the edition says so at the top rather than in a footnote.*

NewEight filed — five verified, two plausible, one contested

**Graded verified, all five read end to end at PubMed Central.** Ostfeld *et al.* in *PNAS* test thirty years of monitoring in New York oak forest and find nymphal black-legged tick abundance “not correlated with the abundance of white-tailed deer, the prior abundance of larval ticks, or seasonal temperature extremes” — “despite sixfold variation in deer abundance across years” — predicted instead by red oak acorns two years earlier by way of white-footed mice, and concluding that risk “arises from the dynamics of the ecological community, rather than from the dynamics of any single population of hosts”. Korneisel *et al.* in *Nature* measure ten lake sturgeon on a bending rig to model early tetrapod necks, find the soft-tissue column “surprisingly rigid” at under 6° of lateral movement, and conclude that “mobility in the tetrapod neck did not serve as a functional driver of head–shoulder separation” — forelimb-driven locomotion being the better account. Marrero *et al.* in *iScience* rank 119 people against three mosquito species and find the rankings correlate at 0.3492, 0.1431 and 0.06034: “Mosquito species can rank the attractiveness of individual humans differently.” Ibrahimi *et al.* in *PNAS* show the textbook “expansion-induction” rule for hair-follicle patterning is an output rather than a mechanism, and that a spiny mouse’s very different coat “is not generated by an expansion-induction mechanism, but is recapitulated by an anisotropic chemotaxis model”. And Anslow *et al.* in *Scientific Reports* find that successful cometary delivery of prebiotic feedstock “was an exceedingly rare event”, with a median of zero successful impacts on Earth against “744± 27” on Mars.

**Two are plausible and one is contested.** The *Zootaxa* description of *Colobura cryptica* — a conspicuous butterfly that was two butterflies, with the Andean split “correspond\[ing\] with differences in ventral UV reflectance” — rests on a deposited abstract, because www.mapress.com does not resolve from this container. **The marine-snow item is plausible *despite* a full text**, which is a first here: the *Science* paper carrying the carbon claim answered 403, and what was read end to end is its companion methods paper in *Nature Communications*. *Reading one paper of a pair does not verify the other.* The release’s “six times more carbon” is not in the deposited abstract and is marked on the page as the release’s figure rather than the paper’s. The contested item is Schmidbauer & Nieder in *Science*, whose crows decide in about 235 ms, “roughly twice as fast as *has been observed* in monkeys or humans” — a comparison to a literature, which the headline turns into a race that was run.

Fact-checkSix held — two after reading the paper, two for a claim that is not in it, two we could not open

**A headline gentler than its paper, which is the inverse of the usual direction.** A *Journal of Neuroscience* study of 65 children was carried as “a surprising brain difference”; the paper’s own title says “problem-solving *deficits*” and its text says *deficits*, *impairments*, *atypical* and *altered* throughout. Its most interesting result is that brain–behaviour relations “reversed in direction between groups” while “the groups differed not in how strongly individual regions activated” — two mappings, where *altered* requires somebody to have already picked which is standard. **We also found a count we cannot reconcile:** its Introduction reports “65 children (30 DD, 35 TA)” and its Methods give the analysed sample as 28 DD and 37 TA. *That is our observation and the authors have not been asked.*

**And one close to home.** A *Sports Medicine – Open* meta-analysis was headlined “Walking and weight training may not be enough to keep your bones strong”. It is almost entirely null — including “Bone Mineral Density showed no significant changes after endurance or power training” — with one positive result across 193 people, and it states in its own text that these markers “do not always imply that bone mineral balance has subsequently increased or decreased”. **Two internal contradictions are ours, found by reading it:** the abstract reports GRADE certainty as “moderate to low” where the Results say “high”, and a confidence interval printed in the Results crosses zero where the abstract’s does not. The release’s “45-46 percent” appears nowhere in the text we read and is not repeated.

**Also held:** a University of Ottawa release reporting that the universe “could be 26.7 billion years old”, where the paper it names is a rotation-curve comparison whose own conclusion is that the model “describes rotation curves as well as galaxy-by-galaxy fitted MOND” and whose headline age claim appears nowhere in it; an unpublished American Heart Association conference abstract whose “11 times more reports” is a disproportionality signal over a voluntary database with no denominator; and a *Lancet Planetary Health* modelling study carried as “2 billion metric tons of CO₂ a year just by staying alive”, which the release itself corrects four paragraphs later and which we could not open. **The item we most wanted to file is held:** a palaeoecological review arguing Europe was a herbivory-maintained woodland–grassland mosaic rather than closed forest — www.sciencedirect.com does not resolve here, Crossref holds no deposited abstract, and the coverage does not mention that the closed-forest question has been argued since the 1990s. *A review taking a side in a long dispute is a different object from a measurement.*

SiteA bot check in its third state in three weeks, and a picture census whose denominator moved

**PubMed Central challenged nothing this week.** Eight article paths were requested and eight returned full text. That is the third distinct state in three weeks — every request blocked on 30 October, four of seven challenged on 6 November, none now — and the sequence is why the edition records it as a fact about the minute rather than about the archive. *It is also why this edition reports eight full texts where last week’s reported three*, which is a difference in luck, not in effort. **An open licence bought something for the first time in six weeks:** six of the eight full texts arrived through PubMed Central, five of the eight filed items being CC BY or CC BY-NC-ND. The licence still did not open a publisher’s door; a public archive did.

**The picture census, and the denominator moved for two reasons.** Nine ScienceDaily topic feeds were requested; strange\_science answered 404 again *and so did top\_science*, which carried content last week — so seven feeds carried rather than eight, deduplicating to **83 unique releases in the seven days to 13 November**. Five lead with an image credited AI/ScienceDaily.com and 39 more with a Shutterstock photograph: **44 of 83**. Last week was 40 of 66, and *the two must not be subtracted* — a feed dropped out and the denominator rose by a quarter. **None of the eight filed items leads with an image credited to a machine**, and four lead with one from the research or its institution, two of those credited to the papers’ own authors.

**One seed ends the way last week’s did, and that is recorded rather than smoothed.** Two filed items this week share a shape — a correct observation with a functional explanation attached afterwards and then quoted as if established — and [Too Good to Check](https://starstuff.earth/too-good-to-check.html) has no row for it. *Two instances is not a pattern.* Last week’s preprint-versus-published seed asked for the same count and nobody has done it; this one asks again and says plainly that it is asking twice.

## Glimmer Wire, edition eleven: six papers read in full, and three of them held

[The eleventh edition](https://starstuff.earth/glimmer-wire-2026-11-06.html) of the weekly graded scan is live — **eight items filed, six held, three seeds**, lags from 4 to 232 days with a median of 29.5. **Six versions of record were read end to end, the most in eleven editions, and three of the six are held items rather than filed ones.** Holding is not what happens when we cannot get the paper; this week it is mostly what happened when we could.

NewEight filed — three verified, three plausible, two contested

Graded **verified**, all three read end to end at PubMed Central: Boyette *et al.* in *Molecular Ecology*, who swab 327 museum frogs collected across the Brazilian Atlantic Forest’s unexplained 1970s decline and find “no evidence of Bd infection prior to the decline, followed by widespread infection immediately thereafter” — nothing before 1979, 58.8 per cent in the 1980s — while stating in their own conclusion that “by focusing exclusively on frogs collected at Boraçeia, we cannot extrapolate our findings to the broader Atlantic Forest”; Foo *et al.* in *Ecology and Evolution*, who track individual crown-of-thorns feeding scars at low seastar density and find that “26% of scars showed signs of regenerating coral tissue” by 22 weeks, a result the wire converted from a hedge about community diversity into a verdict that the reef’s most notorious animal is “actually helping it”; and Fu *et al.* in *Nature*, who recover enamel proteins from six Middle Pleistocene *Homo erectus* specimens in China and argue that the super-archaic stretches of the Denisovan genome, “some of which later passed to modern humans, are likely to have originated from *H. erectus*”.

**Three are plausible on complete deposited abstracts, because none of those publishers would serve the paper.** Zhang *et al.* in *Molecular Cell* describe type VI retrons, which “do not produce detectable msDNA in the absence of phage yet still provide defense” — the molecule used to detect such systems being absent, the lead author is quoted in the Gladstone release saying “we wondered if they might be broken” — and find the DNA *derepresses* a toxin where every other retron’s restrains one. Caulus *et al.* in *New Phytologist* rotate poplar stems inside a uniformly lit sphere to cancel gravity and light, and watch them counter-curve anyway by laying tension wood “on the convex side of curved stems until straightness is recovered”, which they say revises “our textbook view of tension wood formation”. Koga *et al.* in *Nature Astronomy* report “all five canonical nucleobases” in Hayabusa2’s Ryugu samples, concluding only that this “reinforces the hypothesis” of asteroid contribution to Earth’s prebiotic inventory.

**Two are contested, and both turn on one word.** Jost *et al.* in *Science* report a chiral magnon in “the altermagnetic *candidate* manganese telluride”, which the wire called conclusive proof of a third type of magnetism. Grice *et al.* in *PNAS* report heme-derived tetrapyrroles in a 115-million-year-old theropod bone, in a paper whose own first sentence is that “detection of endogenous biomolecules in vertebrate fossils remains controversial” — a live dispute the coverage does not mention, under the headline “dinosaur blood”.

Fact-checkSix held, three of them after reading the paper in full

**The two we took most care with are about us and about children.** A *Science Advances* paper titled “Maternal-fetal immune conflict contributes to male-specific impairments in *a mouse model of neurodevelopmental disorders*” was carried as a clue to autism’s origins; what it measures is fetal weight and sensory organ development in mouse embryos 24 hours after an immune challenge, and its own closing phrase is “acute, sex-restricted developmental deficits”. We read every word of it and are not grading our own existence as something a body is derailed into — nor repairing it into a gift, which keeps the same ranking. And a JAMA Network Open cohort reporting *associations* between ultra-processed food intake, striatal response and adiposity gain was headlined as a “troubling brain response”; the word is the headline’s alone, and it relocates a property of a food system inside a child.

**The sharpest contradiction this page has found between a release and its paper.** A Texas A&M release says a “major new study challenges these assumptions” about reading; the paper’s own abstract says “the findings were generally consistent with previous research”. It is a cross-sectional meta-structural equation model over correlations among test subtests, it followed no child and ran no intervention, and **it was published on 16 August 2025 — a lag of 444 days**, the longest this scan has recorded by journal date.

**Also held:** a PNAS paper on foveolar cone patterning, read in full, in which the string *vitamin A* occurs zero times and *retinoic acid* eleven, carried as a vitamin A discovery 260 days late and conducted in organoids; a *Physical Review D* survey of the conditions under which black holes could fragment — all of which require higher dimensions or modified gravity, the abstract’s second sentence being that the area law “forbids such processes” in classical general relativity — reported as a loophole somebody found; and a new genus of seahorse-lookalike fishes known only from museum drawers, **held with nothing wrong with it that we could find**: it is published in a journal whose site does not resolve from this container, so its editorial process could not be established, and soundness in taxonomy is partly a property of the venue.

SiteA bot check that got worse by getting smaller, and a hedge an editor added that nobody put back

**Last week PubMed Central challenged every request; this week it challenged four of seven.** We did not attempt to defeat the challenge and did not disguise the caller, which is the same rule as last week. The edition says plainly why the intermittency is the worse condition: a total block is a fact about the archive that reproduces, while an intermittent one means *which* three papers were read in full is partly a coin toss, and a reader repeating the work will get a different three.

**And a mechanism [Too Good to Check](https://starstuff.earth/too-good-to-check.html) has no row for.** The altermagnon result sat on arXiv from 29 January 2025 — **644 days** before the wire carried it, against a journal lag of 6 — and the two abstracts differ: the preprint claims “the *first* direct experimental evidence” of “a *new* class of magnetic excitations”, where *Science* published “direct experimental evidence” of “a *discrete* class”. Peer review removed a priority claim and softened a novelty claim; the coverage then asserted something stronger than either. That is the opposite direction from the marmoset case recorded on 23 October, and the pair is filed as a seed rather than a finding — **two instances is not a pattern, and the edition says so.**

**And one ordering fault in our own tooling, hit for the first time this week.** `llms.txt` states how many of the site’s pages have a Markdown sibling, so it has to be written *after* `build-markdown.mjs` creates them — and the order this project has had written down since the siblings shipped runs `build-derived` first. It has never mattered before, because that count only moves when a page *joins* the Markdown set, which this edition did. `check-derived.mjs --write` caught it by checking its own work: it wrote `llms.txt` saying “70 of the 220 pages”, then generated the new sibling, then reported `llms.txt` STALE against the true 71 of 221. *A second run of the generator fixes it, and the documented order should be amended rather than the habit of running it twice.*

**The picture census:** eight ScienceDaily feeds carried content, deduplicating to 66 unique releases in the seven days to 6 November. Seven lead with an image credited `AI/ScienceDaily.com` and 33 more with a Shutterstock photograph — 40 of 66. Last week was 57 of 88 and *the two must not be subtracted*: the denominator moved by a fifth, and a ratio over a different mix is a different measurement.

## The code goes MIT, and the collection stays CC BY-SA

From today the code in [this collection’s repository](https://github.com/Stimpunks/Star-Stuff) is under the MIT License: the build and check tools, and the two scripts the pages share. **Every zine, field guide, broadside, diagram and word stays [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/)**, exactly as before.

SiteTwo licences, and the licence file says which covers what

**Why.** Creative Commons advises against its licences for software, and the host this site runs on asks open-source projects for a software licence. It was Ryan Boren’s call, made across every Stimpunks repository on the same day, and Helen Edgar agreed to it for the code she has written here.

**What is code:** everything in `tools/`, and `starstuff.js` and `edit.js`. **What is not, even inside those files:** the words and pictures a script carries — the chain spines, the L★S mark, the prose the build tools write into derived pages — the scripts written inside a page, the stylesheets, the guest pieces under their own notices, the typefaces and anybody else’s pictures. [The licence file](https://github.com/Stimpunks/Star-Stuff/blob/main/LICENSE) now leads with the MIT section and keeps the full CC BY-SA 4.0 text, word for word, beneath it. Copies of the code taken before today may still be used under CC BY-SA.

**One sentence of ours had to change with it.** [The design system page](https://starstuff.earth/design.html) said it was CC BY-SA “like everything else here”, which stopped being true this morning; it now says the words and pictures are CC BY-SA and the tools that measure them are MIT.
