Not on the Front Page

A Star Stuff collection · five eggs

Real pieces, made to the same standard as everything else here, deliberately kept off the index. There are two small doors in the site footer, and search will find them. Nothing is hidden from the search index, the sitemap, or the checking tools — only from the front page.

What an egg is here

An easter egg on this site is not a lower standard. Every piece filed here carries the same requirements as any other: sources traced to primaries, claims graded, a logged entry in FACTCHECK.md, a dated line in the changelog, and a clean pass through all six checking gates. If a piece could not survive that, it would not be an egg. It would just be sloppy, and being tucked away would make it worse rather than better.

What makes it an egg is only where the door is. These pieces are about the site rather than about the world — its numbering, its own mistakes, its furniture. Putting them in the index grid would ask every first-time reader to care about our internals before they had read anything. So they sit one step off the path, for the reader who is already looking closely.

Two conventions this page breaks, on purpose

This collection is the exception to two rules the rest of the site follows, and the house habit is to say so on the page rather than let it look like an oversight.

  • It has no section on the index. Every other collection here exists because a section of the front page needed a home, and owes that section an introduction and a link. This one inverts that: a collection page with nothing pointing at it from the grid. That is the entire mechanism of the thing — if it were listed, it would not be off the path.
  • It was created at one member, below the two-member floor. That floor is real and it was argued for: How We Got Here was deliberately deferred at one member and created only when a second piece arrived with the same shape, because collections here are outputs, not inputs — what earns a page is a form observed in finished work, never a folder opened in advance. This page was a folder opened in advance, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. It reached two members the same day, which vindicates nothing — the floor exists so that a page has to be earned rather than guessed at, and getting the guess right is luck, not method.

The reason we took that trade: an egg needs a home that the working papers could not honestly give it, and the alternative was a hand-written exemption inside one of the checking tools — a page the tools were told to stop asking about. A collection page is visible and a tool exemption is not, and between a convention bent in public and a gate quietly narrowed, the first is the safer mistake. It is recorded in DECISIONS.md with the same reasoning.

How to find them

Three ways, none of them a puzzle. There is no code to crack here and nothing is gated on cleverness — an easter egg that punishes people for not spotting it is just a page you hid.

  • The doors. Two, in the footer of the front page. A bare 37 sits among the ordinary links where it reads as boilerplate, and goes straight to No. 37. The other is a drawn cosmic egg at the bottom of the page, on its own, which comes here — it replaced an egg emoji on 14 August 2026, because at 14px wedged between two credits nobody read the emoji as a door. Off the path is not the same as too small to see. The drawing is one of twelve; the rest are in The Hatchery. Both doors carry text labels, so a screen reader announces them as plainly as it announces About or Search — an egg only sighted readers can find is not an egg, it is an exclusion.
  • Search. Every egg is fully indexed, down to the individual spread — so searching for what a piece is actually about will surface it like anything else. The collection name is stripped as chrome, the same as every other collection name, so “easter eggs” is not itself a search term.
  • This page. Which you have, so that worked.
Not:

Not a lower bar. Same sourcing, same grading, same ledger, same gates. The only difference is the location of the link.

Not hidden from the tooling. Eggs are in sitemap.xml, in search-index.json, and inside every check. A page the gates cannot see is a page that rots, and hiding one from them would be the actual failure this collection is arranged to avoid.

Not a place to put weak work. If something is not good enough for the index it is not good enough for here either. This is not the drawer.

Not a licence to backfill the numbering. No. 37 occupies one deliberate gap and spends a spread marking itself for it. Every other gap in the sequence stays empty, because the numbers mean when and an archive that gets edited stops being evidence.

Zine No. 37
Out of Order
Easter Eggs · the gap, annotated

There was never a No. 37, and that was not an accident.

The one hole in our numbering, filled by a page that admits it does not belong there. No. 37 was assigned to a zine on 11 August 2026, held back so a question could go to Helen Edgar rather than assumed, and arrived two days later as No. 45 because seven pieces had shipped past it — so the gap was a receipt, not a slip. The zine corrects its own premise on spread two, then takes three famous 37s apart: Wunderlich's 37 °C, averaged in 1868 from a million armpit readings and now about 0.4 °C out of date; the 37% of any search you should spend looking and refusing everything; and the number people reach for when told to be random, which is marked contested because it comes from an unrefereed survey. Then it refuses to connect them, because a coincidence dressed as a mechanism is the fault this whole site was built to name. Closes on the honest arithmetic — g(5) = 37, the third star number, the mirror with 73 — and on a page younger than No. 46 whose own number says otherwise. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 47
True Facts About True Facts
Easter Eggs · a credit, paid late

The animal is never the punchline. That is the part we took.

Six zines here and three field guides exist because Ryan watched Ze Frank's True Facts with a young person in our community whose special interest is sharks — and until this page, his name appeared nowhere in this project at all. The debt slipped through because the citation habit points at claims rather than causes. What we actually took is narrower than “it makes science fun”: the comedy lands on the narrator, on human expectation, on language, and never on the organism for being a defective version of something else — ridiculous is not deficient — which is the same turn the field guides' reframe tables make from the opposite end, without needing the table. Then his own line, from Pamela Hill Nettleton's U.S. Catholic interview: “flawed and wonderful” in one breath with no ranking between them. Then the arc that runs against every incentive — a channel whose first outing “included neither truth nor facts” and which went toward NOAA archive footage and named researchers. And then the fact-check the title owes: the origin of True Facts exists in three incompatible published versions, and the dates suggest a 2022 profile confused the original with its own 2013 redo. Closes on why the tone stays where it is — same joke, same goodwill, different room. Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. He has no involvement and we did not ask.

The Hatchery
Twelve Cosmic Eggs
Easter Eggs · the specimen sheet

A drawing is not evidence. The source line under it is.

The sheet behind the door. The cosmic egg — a universe that was closed and then opened — is one of the most widely repeated images people have, and this page names where it appears (Hiraṇyagarbha in the Rigveda, the Ogdoad's at Hermopolis, Ilmatar's in the Kalevala, Pangu's, Taʼaroa's, the Dogon Amma) and then draws none of them — the same line Field Guide No. 10 drew around its five turtles people made. A borrowed shape is not a borrowed belief. The twelve are ours, and each carries one thing we can check: the leatherback, which has no bony carapace and dives to 1,280 m on hundreds of irregular ossicles; the Arrau, filed as a silent animal until a hydrophone caught 2,122 sounds in eleven types, hatchlings calling from inside the shell and females answering; collagen, not hydroxyapatite, as the source of bone's piezoelectricity — the error our own Manifesto made twice; painted turtle hatchlings supercooled below −10 °C, where survival turns on what touches them rather than how tough they are; endosymbiosis as an egg that arrived rather than grew; the double empathy problem as two jagged edges that don't interlock, with the gap in neither half. And the one on the door has our own argument against it printed underneath: it began as a spiky profile, which No. 21 refuses as a better chart is still a chart — so the drawing keeps the jag and throws away the baseline and the grid. Closes on a dotted, empty twelfth. Unnumbered on purpose.

Twelve drawings→
Field Guide No. 13
A Field Guide to Watching Animals
Easter Eggs · the watching list

Watch the video, then read what the literature actually says.

The companion catalogue to No. 47, and the watching list that produced half our animal writing. Sixteen animals, each with a True Facts video and field notes taken from the papers underneath it. Twelve pair with something already published here and link back — the mantis shrimp to our Umwelt zine, where the famous claim collapses (they discriminate wavelengths worse than we do); five entries to the nervous systems guide; the crab to Five Times a Crab, whose real finding is that the shape was lost more often than gained; the shark to the skeleton that turns out to have lost its bone rather than never grown it. Then four more: the aye-aye, whose tapping survived every attempt to explain it as simple echo; the sea cucumber, which throws its organs and spends five weeks regrowing them; the nudibranch, which eats stinging cells and mounts them, still loaded, on its own back; and the hedgehog, here because nobody knows why it foams and because the word (Redo) in its title is the exhibit in No. 47's fact-check. The reframe table corrects the popular verdict on the animal — never anything in the videos, which is the point of the companion zine — and one entry deliberately has no row aimed at a belief. Nothing loads until you press play.

Zine No. 49
One Atom of Justice
Easter Eggs · the method, credited

Grind the universe down and show me one atom of justice.

A credit to Terry Pratchett that turned into three corrections. The visible debt was small and correctly filed — Great A'Tuin in No. 35, the turtles people made section of Field Guide No. 10. The real one had been sitting on stimpunks.org since 2024: First Sight and Second Thoughts, from Wintersmith, which is this project's working method described better than our own documentation manages, and which was never once named as a source. Then the fact-check the credit owed. The Science of Discworld books alternate — Pratchett's Discworld chapters, Ian Stewart and Jack Cohen's science chapters — so Pan narrans, the storytelling chimpanzee, sits in the science half, and lies-to-children is Cohen and Stewart's from 1994, five years before Discworld carried it anywhere. Narrativium is genuinely his. Aggregators file all of it under the name that sells. The zine then turns lies-to-children on our own simplifications, reads the Industrial Revolution novels as asking who a technology costs, and explains the clacks Overhead and the X-Clacks-Overhead header this site now sends. It closes on Death's challenge in Hogfather, quoted verbatim from the book: there is no atom of justice in the powder, which means our own masthead is a promise we make, not a fact we found. Eleven spreads, nine diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. The estate has no involvement and we did not ask.

Three corrections→

The Turtle Moves

“Mountains rise and fall, and under them the Turtle swims onward. Men live and die, and the Turtle Moves. Empires grow and crumble, and the Turtle Moves. Gods come and go, and still the Turtle Moves. The Turtle Moves.”

Didactylos, in Terry Pratchett's Small Gods (1992)

Small Gods is a book about an institution that kept every instrument of belief and mislaid the thing itself — a church with an enormous apparatus and a god reduced to a tortoise nobody can hear. The Turtle in it is not a doctrine. It is the thing that goes on being the case whether or not anyone believes it, and the heresy that gets people tortured is the flat refusal to stop saying so.

Asked whether it is really true, Didactylos does not argue. He says the Turtle exists, that this will go on happening either way, and then the three words the whole book has been walking toward: “It is real.” That is why the passage sits at the foot of this collection rather than anywhere else. The eggs are the pages about our own furniture — our numbering, our mistakes, where our method came from — and this is the sentence underneath all of it. No. 49 spends eleven spreads conceding that the universe contains no atom of justice and that our masthead is a promise rather than a finding. The Turtle is the other half of that, and the half we never get to choose: some things are simply so, and go on being so through every empire that would rather they weren't.

One correction the drawing owes, because this is the kind of page that owes them. A turtle carrying elephants carrying the world is not Hindu cosmology, though it is endlessly repeated as such. The stack appears in John Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690) as a story about “an Indian,” and Bertrand Russell repeats it in 1927; scholarship on the epics and major Puranas finds no world-upholding elephant in them at all, and Kurma is a pivot for churning rather than a world-bearer — which No. 35 already says. So what Pratchett is riffing on is a European joke about India, not anyone's scripture. This drawing is therefore a novelist's invention drawn as a novelist's invention, and it is deliberately not filed beside the five turtles people made. They are not the same turtle, and that is the connection.
