No Standard Anything

A Star Stuff collection · twenty guides, 246 entries

Twelve of these guides arrive at the same sentence, and none of them set out to. Catalogue enough ways of being a star, a nervous system, a shark or a turtle, and it turns up on its own.

A form, not a register

Five of the collections on this site sort by register — what kind of argument a piece is making. Print broke that pattern by sorting on medium, and said so on its own face rather than letting it pass as an oversight; Sound is the second on that axis and repeated the disclosure. This is the third departure, and it is a different one again: the Field Guides are sorted by form.

Not what they argue, and not what you hold — how they are shaped. A field guide here is a catalogue: a run of entries in one category, each built the same way, each standing alone, none of them ranked. You can read one entry and stop. That is the whole distinction, and it cuts across register cheerfully — the elements guide is settled physics doing the argumentative work, and the constellations guide is frank invention, and they are the same kind of object.

Twenty guides, 246 entries. Or 263 cards, if you count the seventeen that are not filed as entries — the five turtles people made, the octopus settlements, the rooms people build, the egg-crack accounts, the two questions the answer is not in on yet, the palaeontologist who was not a specimen, the corrections nobody has published yet, the families people make, the three the rainbow guide hands back rather than tell twice, and the Exclusion Zone two research programmes cannot agree about — which their guides deliberately withhold from the apparatus, for reasons that turn out to be the most interesting thing on this page. Both numbers are printed rather than the awkward one rounded away, the same way Sound prints 230 cards and then explains in prose why that is not 230 songs.

What a field guide is here

The shape settled early and has barely moved since guide one. Every entry carries:

  • A diagram, drawn not decorated. An atom, a route shape, a nervous-system architecture, a place on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. It has to carry information the prose doesn't.
  • A short brief — the thing itself, in a few lines, readable without opening anything.
  • A reframe table. Two or three columns setting the received verdict against what the animal or object is actually doing: sounds like / actually, to us / to them, Earth's rule / its own rule / result.
  • Full field notes, collapsed behind a click, with the citations in them.
  • A “Not:” refusals set on the guide as a whole, naming the traps that particular subject invites.

The reframe table is the one that has an exception, and the exception is a rule rather than a lapse. That table exists to correct a verdict somebody else handed down — sounds like is always somebody's mistaken reading. So it can only be pointed at a subject that has been misread from outside. Being a Turtle found the limit first and kept its five turtles people made in a separate section with a dashed border and no table, because running “sounds like / actually” over somebody's cosmology would be obscene. My Monotropic Galaxy is the first guide to carry no reframe table at all, for the same reason at full scale: all twenty of its entries are one person's naming of her own life, and there is no verdict in them to correct. The format difference is the argument. A subject that cannot take the apparatus gets a different card, not a smaller one.

Guide No. 14 found a third reason to withhold the table, and it is not the first two. The Ways of Being Alone gives its twelve animals a reframe table each and gives none to its thirteenth card, the octopus settlements at Octopolis and Octlantis. Not because a cosmology is at stake, and not because there is no verdict — there is a very loud one, that octopuses turned out to be secretly social. It is withheld because that verdict is not a misreading of the octopuses at all. It is a wish about us, laid over what the surveys actually describe: dens crowded onto the only hard substrate for miles, and evictions at the edges. A table corrects a reader who got the animal wrong. It has nothing to say to a reader who got the animal right and wanted something else to be true.

The collapsed notes are worth one sentence of housekeeping, because they are the guide. On paper there is nothing to click, so the shared print stylesheet forces every .entry-notes block open and hides the expander — otherwise a printed field guide carries only the briefs and silently drops the entire body of the work. Printed guides run long. They are meant to.

Four things every guide turned out to do

These were read off eleven finished guides rather than handed down before the first one, which is the honest order and also the order this whole site claims to work in: the categories are outputs, not inputs. They are listed in the order they do their work.

  1. Every entry is a real, checkable case. Not a representative example invented to make the point. The bonnethead really does digest seagrass, and it was tested in 2018 — which is what turned “sharks are carnivores” from a law into a summary of what nobody had checked. Where a beloved detail could not be stood up it comes out: the tortoise guide drops the lovely story that the pancake tortoise inflates itself inside a rock crevice, because that is now thought to be wrong.
  2. Name the deficit reading, then refuse it. This is the reframe table, and it is the engine of the form. The vocabulary hides the verdicts — failed dwarf, primitive net, simple worm, living fossil — and every guide goes after the word rather than around it. Two entries in the shark guide are there because of a phrase rather than an animal.
  3. No ladder, and no best in category. The entries are siblings, not a ranking, and the guides say so out loud when the temptation is strongest: the elephant's 257 billion neurons and the slime mold's zero sit in the same catalogue, in no order that means anything. Us included — the nervous systems guide files humans as one arrangement among eleven and not the top of anything.
  4. State the moral, and hold it as a rhyme. Each guide says plainly what it thinks the catalogue means for how we treat each other — and then declines to pretend that biology proves it. A turtle breathing through the wrong end is not evidence about human belonging; it is a demonstration that the category was drawn too narrow, which is a different and smaller claim. A rhyme, not a proof, everywhere.

Where they come from

Thirteen of the nineteen are companion catalogues, built to sit beside a zine that had more cases than it could hold: Nervous Systems beside Every Nervous System (No. 27), Ways of Migrating beside Always Migrating (No. 30), Being a Shark beside the shark set (Nos. 32–34), Being a Turtle beside Everywhere That Turtles Go (No. 35), Being a Tortoise beside the same zine, My Monotropic Galaxy beside The Cloud Phase (No. 45) and Companion Stars (No. 46), The Ways of Being Alone beside How Anything Gets Built (No. 54), Nests beside What Looks Like Chaos (No. 55), Eggs beside The Shell Was Your Calcium (No. 57), Resting beside The Rest You Keep (No. 59), and Unfinished Animals beside Thirteen Changes (No. 61), and Rainbows beside both Your Shadow Is the Centre (No. 69) and Both Arcs, One Drop (No. 70). Two of them answer to an earlier guide as well as to a zine — Tortoise to No. 10, The Ways of Being Alone to No. 6. The remaining six stand alone: Constellations, Elements, Impossible Places, Worlds, Ways of Being a Star and Symbioses. This figure has now been wrong three times, every time because it was read off markers instead of off the guides’ own colophons, so it is derived by grepping all seventeen for what each says it accompanies.

That is a useful division of labour rather than an afterthought. A zine has to argue, which means choosing three cases and letting the rest go; a catalogue can hold fifteen without any of them having to carry the whole point. When a zine keeps wanting a fourth example, that is the signal a guide is owed.

The last two stretch the pairing in a way worth naming, because it is the direction of the debt that changed. The first four were built when a zine overflowed: the cases went into the catalogue because the argument had no room for them. Nests runs the other way — it hands the weaver back to No. 55 in a single card rather than re-running an argument that zine already made properly, and the last card in the guide exists because of what No. 55 says about the observer in the doorway. A companion can be the piece that knows when to stop.

The other six stand alone. And the tortoise guide is the one that came from a guide rather than a zine — the turtle catalogue turned out to have a land-shaped hole in it.

Field Guide No. 6
A Field Guide to Symbioses
Field Guide · 12 entries

Mutual aid, written into biology — and the honest one-way case.

Twelve partnerships across the tree of life.

Twelve partnerships across the tree of life — fungi and roots trading underground, a cleaner fish trusted by predators who could swallow it, the fig and its wasp.

12 symbioses→
Details

Mutual aid, written into biology — twelve partnerships across the tree of life, read as mutual aid. Fungi and roots trading underground, bacteria making soil, a cleaner fish trusted by predators who could swallow it, algae building reefs that shelter a quarter of ocean life, the fig and its wasp that cannot live without each other. Interdependence is the rule, not the exception: Kropotkin showed mutual aid is a factor of evolution; Margulis, that life “took over the globe not by combat, but by networking.” This isn't “nature is nice” — parasites are real too, and the ghost pipe (Monotropa uniflora) is the honest one-way case, a myco-heterotroph that only takes, modelling receiving without reciprocating — and a bridge to monotropism (with Helen Edgar's tribute to Dinah Murray). It's that nothing living stands alone. What biology has always done, disability-justice communities name and practice: the human specimen is our own care webs and spoon shares (Spade, Mingus, Piepzna-Samarasinha, Kafai, Lewis), and a closing coda swaps the language of management for the language of living systems — symbiosis over competition, competency networks over leadership, coordination over management. Each entry maps who gives what and gets what, with full field notes. Solidarity, not charity; interdependence, not independence.

Field Guide No. 8
A Field Guide to the Ways of Migrating
Field Guide · 15 entries · companion to No. 30

Say “migration” and most of us picture one shape. Almost nothing does that.

Fifteen ways of migrating — the Arctic tern flies ~71,000 km a year and refuses the straight line. The companion catalogue to Always Migrating (No. 30).

15 ways→
Details

Fifteen ways of migrating — the companion catalogue to Always Migrating (Zine No. 30). Say “migration” and most of us picture one shape: a long line, north in spring, back in autumn. Almost nothing does that. The Arctic tern flies ~71,000 km a year and refuses the straight line, riding wind in vast S-shaped detours because the long way is the cheap way. A juvenile godwit crossed 13,560 km in 11 days without landing — held with care, since it shrinks its own organs to do it and a migrant who rests is not a lesser migrant. No monarch finishes the round trip: several short-lived generations work north and one “super” generation, living 8–9 months instead of weeks, flies the whole way back. Salmon find one stream by the magnetic field they imprinted on, then die and carry the ocean up into the forest. The largest migration on Earth is a few hundred metres straight up, every night, in the dark — distance was never the measure. Spiderlings rig silk and let atmospheric electricity carry them; trees migrate at metres a year, seed by seed; the Sahara crosses an ocean as dust and fertilises the Amazon; a star gets handed to a wider orbit and the Sun is probably not from here. The centre of the guide is partial migration — populations holding both migrants and residents, described in the literature as the commonest form, the one all others derive from. Variation within a population isn't the anomaly; it's the mechanism. Closes on us: the crossing made in place, when the word arrives and every year behind you rearranges. Held with care throughout — this reads migration as a way of being, never as a frame for forced human displacement. Fifteen entries, route-shape diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Twelve ways to be a shark, written for a young person in our community whose special interest is sharks. Companion to Zines 32, 33 and 34.

12 ways→
Details

Twelve ways to be a shark — the companion catalogue to the shark set (Zines 32, 33 and 34), written for a young person in our community whose special interest is sharks. These are not distant relatives: one clade, one body plan, four hundred million years. And inside it, almost nothing holds still. The whale shark reaches ~18.8 m and lives on plankton; the dwarf lanternshark tops out near 20 cm and erases its own silhouette by lighting its belly to match the light coming down. The goblin shark cannot give chase in the dark, so it doesn't — the jaws fire forward at up to 3.1 m/s, the fastest protrusion known in any shark. The thresher hunts with the far end of itself, whipping its tail overhead to stun several fish at once. The epaulette shark walks between tide pools on its fins and rides out near-anoxia by suppressing its own metabolism. The cookiecutter, at fifty centimetres, takes a neat plug out of animals hundreds of times its size and everything swims off alive. The bonnethead eats seagrass and — tested in 2018 — digests it, which made "sharks are carnivores" a category nobody had checked rather than a finding. Two entries are here because of a phrase rather than an animal: the great hammerhead's head does not sharpen its electrosense (tested directly, it widens the search area instead), and the frilled shark is not a living fossil, because nothing alive is. One points up, one points down, and both skip what the animal is doing. Twelve entries, schematic diagrams, a "Not:" refusals set. There is no standard shark.

Twelve ways to be a turtle, and five turtles people made.

Twelve ways to be a turtle, and five turtles people made — the leatherback has no bony carapace at all. Companion to Everywhere That Turtles Go (No. 35).

12 + 5→
Details

Twelve ways to be a turtle, and five turtles people made — the companion catalogue to Everywhere That Turtles Go (No. 35). Almost every entry is a thing a turtle is supposed to have and doesn't. The leatherback has no bony carapace at all, just hundreds of interlocking ossicles in oily skin — and it dives to 1,280 m and holds its body up to 18 °C above the sea around it, which gives it the largest range of any reptile alive. The mata mata has weak jaw muscles and almost no tongue and is a superb predator, because it never bites: the mouth is a pump, and every "weakness" on the list is a component of it. The Fitzroy River turtle takes about 70% of its oxygen in through two enlarged sacs at the far end of itself. The Chinese softshell excretes urea through the lining of its mouth rather than its kidneys — the head-dipping that looks like drinking is the opposite of drinking. Painted turtle hatchlings spend their first winter in a nest below −10 °C by supercooling, and what decides whether they live is not how tough they are but what touches them. And the Arrau was filed as a silent animal until somebody brought a hydrophone: 2,122 recorded sounds in eleven types, hatchlings calling from inside the egg, and females coming and answering — the first evidence of post-hatching parental care in any turtle. The silence was ours. The last entry is the bill: Rafetus swinhoei, two or three animals left, the last known female dead in April 2023. The five turtles people made — Turtle Island, ao, Kurma (a pivot for churning, not a world-bearer), Great A'Tuin, and a Larrakia Star Dreaming we cite rather than retell — get a separate section and a deliberately different card, because they are not five drafts of one story. There is no standard turtle.

An arc that runs from the Moon to a graveyard to a beginning.

Twelve land tortoises. It opens on 15 September 1968, when Zond 5 carried two Soviet steppe tortoises around the far side of the Moon and brought them back alive.

12 tortoises→
Details

Twelve land tortoises — Testudinidae — and an arc that runs from the Moon to a graveyard to a beginning. It opens where this collection has to open: on 15 September 1968, Zond 5 carried two Soviet steppe tortoises around the far side of the Moon and brought them back alive, two months before Apollo 8 carried any human. They lost about a tenth of their body weight and were otherwise fine. Then the ordinary astonishments of a slow land animal: the gopher tortoise digs burrows over fourteen metres long that more than 350 other species live in, which is the argument for building rooms that fit whoever needs them most, stated in reptile. The pancake tortoise has a shell whose bone is full of holes and is the fastest and best-climbing tortoise there is — and the beloved story that it inflates itself inside a rock crevice is now believed to be wrong, so the guide says so instead of repeating it. In July 2020 an Aldabra giant tortoise was filmed hunting and swallowing a tern chick, the first deliberate hunt ever recorded in a wild tortoise, which turned "tortoises are herbivores" from a law back into a summary of what we had happened to watch. Jonathan hatched around 1832 and is still on the lawn. The speckled padloper is six centimetres and complete. And then four entries marked as unfinished, because their endings are decisions rather than facts: the star tortoise, most trafficked tortoise on Earth and trafficked because it is beautiful; the ploughshare, whose shell conservationists engrave to spoil it for the market, and it may not be working; Lonesome George, who died in 2012 and took Pinta with him; and Fernanda, found alive in 2019 after 113 years of "extinct." It ends on Île aux Aigrettes, where an Aldabra tortoise is doing an extinct Mauritian tortoise's job — 529 hatchlings in eleven years. Not restoring what was lost. Finding another way for the function to happen.

Field Guide No. 14
A Field Guide to the Ways of Being Alone
Field Guide · 12 + 1 · companion to No. 54

A lone wolf is not an outcast. It is a disperser, on its way to founding a pack.

Twelve ways of keeping your own company among our Terran kin.

Twelve ways of keeping your own company among our Terran kin, grown from Betsy Selvam's Stimpunks essay Lone Wolfing: The Joys of Autistic Solitude.

12 ways of being alone→
Details

Twelve ways of keeping your own company among our Terran kin, grown from Betsy Selvam's Stimpunks essay Lone Wolfing: The Joys of Autistic Solitude. A lone wolf is not an outcast. It is a disperser — typically nine to thirty-six months old, one of the roughly 15–20% of any wolf population currently between territories, crossing the neutral buffer ground to find a mate and found a pack. The animal we named the loner after was always on its way somewhere. The guide is sorted by four conditions it did not invent: writing in The Atlantic in 2017, Brent Crane quotes the developmental psychologist Kenneth Rubin — solitude is productive only if it is voluntary, if emotion can be regulated within it, if one can rejoin when one wants to, and if relationships outside it are maintained. Crane's own gloss is the hinge: the difference between rejuvenation and suffering is the quality of reflection available, and the ability to come back. So: social bees are fewer than 10% of all bee species (Danforth, Minckley & Neff, 2019) — we built the entire hive metaphor on the outlier. One sweat bee nests solitary in some populations and eusocial in others, and reared side by side each keeps its own way. Giant pandas aim over 80% of their scent marks at animal trails, going out of their way to be found by animals they will never meet — asynchronous is not the fallback channel, it is the majority channel. Territorial neighbours become dear enemies (Fisher, 1954), and sometimes nasty ones (Temeles, 1994), which is kept because both are true. Fission–fusion parties change membership hourly and nobody calls it instability. Cicadas spend seventeen years alone underground to be loud together for a fortnight. Pumas share kills with non-kin and are 7.7 times more likely to share with someone who once shared with them (Elbroch et al., Science Advances, 2017). Orangutan party size tracks fruit availability rather than species — you change the field, not the animal. A wandering albatross takes a sabbatical year at sea and divorces at 0.3%. Then the thirteenth card, which gets no reframe table on purpose: the octopus settlements at Octopolis and Octlantis are not a secret society but dens crowded onto the only hard substrate for miles, with evictions at the edges. Proximity is not company; proximity plus scarcity is a fight. Four gates, a “Not:” refusals set that declines the syntaxin 1a bait outright. There is no standard amount of company.

A Field Guide to Nests
Field Guide · 13 + 1 · companion to No. 55

Most nests are not built. The famous termite tower is uninhabited, and a bower is not a nest at all.

Sorted by where the labour actually goes — and the catalogue disagrees with the word.

Thirteen homes among our Terran kin. We began meaning to sort by material, and the material sort kept failing: a hermit crab supplies none, and neither does a gall wasp.

13 ways of having a home→
Details

Thirteen homes among our Terran kin, sorted by where the labour actually goes — and the catalogue disagrees with the word. We began meaning to sort by material, and the material sort kept failing, because a hermit crab supplies none and a gall wasp supplies none either. What varies is who does the work. The companion to What Looks Like Chaos (Zine No. 55), which asks how a nest gets built; this one asks what gets to count as one, and the answer is much wider. Built: a ten-centimetre pufferfish making a two-metre geometric court on the seabed that divers photographed for years before anyone believed the builder was that small (Kawase, Okata & Ito, Scientific Reports, 2013) — the pattern is not decorative, it sorts fine sand into the centre, which is the part the female inspects. Then the correction that reorganised the whole guide: a termite mound is uninhabited. That is the paper's own word. Fungus-farming termites build massive empty towers above their subterranean nests, and the daily swing of solar heating drives convection through the tunnel network (Ocko, King, Andreen, Bardunias, Turner, Soar & Mahadevan, Journal of Experimental Biology, 2017). The most-photographed animal architecture on Earth is routinely called the wrong kind of thing: it is not shelter, it is infrastructure — closer to a lung than a house. Borrowed: a hermit crab contributes no material to any home it will ever occupy, and when a single shell arrives the crabs gather and queue beside it in descending size order, so that when the largest moves in the whole line moves up at once. Lewis and Rotjan tested the prediction that matters and found it held — vacancy chains distribute benefits across many participants rather than to the first taker. The crab that waits is not being passive; it is standing in the only position from which the chain works. The largest bird nest on record, 2.9 m wide, 6 m deep and over two tonnes, was accumulated across decades until it was more than the tree could hold, which is inheritance and its cost in one object. And a woodpecker's ecological job is not its own nest but the vacancies it leaves behind for everything that cannot excavate. Induced: a gall is oak tissue that the insect made the oak build — the inducer injects effectors including phytohormones that redirect the plant's development, and different wasp species induce distinctively different galls on the same host, which is why the literature calls a gall an extended phenotype of the insect. And not building at all evolved independently at least seven separate times in birds: three times in the cuckoos, once in honeyguides, once in a duck, twice in songbirds (Sorenson & Payne, 2002). A thing discovered seven times over is not a mistake, and that is the sentence the guide would most like a reader to carry. Secreted, or worn: a swiftlet builds from nothing but its own hardened saliva, and the entry states plainly that this is why the species is farmed and harvested at industrial scale rather than leaving it as a footnote. An emperor penguin has no structure at all — an egg on two feet under a fold of belly skin, with the shelter being the huddle, whose walls are other penguins and which moves. If somewhere-to-be requires a structure, the emperor has nowhere and manifestly succeeds, so the definition has to give: some of us have been housed by other people rather than by rooms, and it counted. And a trapdoor spider known as Number 16 held one silk-lined burrow for 43 years, the longest life recorded for any spider — a record that is really about Barbara York Main, who began following that population in 1974 and kept returning for four decades. A long quiet life in one small place is invisible unless somebody decides it is worth watching. Not a nest: the bower, which every list of animal architecture files as the finest nest alive. No egg is ever laid in it and nothing lives there; the female builds the actual nest elsewhere, alone. It is a theatre with deliberate forced perspective — objects graded outward so they subtend a more even visual angle from where she stands (Endler, Endler & Doerr, Current Biology, 2010). We have spent a century calling a stage set a house. And the fourteenth card gets no reframe table, which is a fourth distinct reason for withholding the apparatus. The rooms people build are not graded here. Running a sounds-like/actually over somebody's own room would make us the observer in the doorway with the clipboard — the exact figure No. 55 spends ten spreads indicting — and No. 55 is also explicit that not every mess is a nest, since burnout collapses the architecture and from the doorway the ruins look identical to the curated version. Nobody can sort those two on sight, and the people most certain they can are the reason the guide has a refusals list. The weaver appears in one card that hands off to No. 55 rather than re-running it; the beaver is deliberately absent, already spent in Minor Worlds. Thirteen lineages, thirteen incompatible methods, none ranked, several of them the ones a tidy observer would call failures. The ninth guide to arrive independently at the same sentence: there is no standard nest.

Field Guide No. 16
A Field Guide to Eggs
Field Guide · 15 + 1 · companion to No. 57

The murre's pointed egg does not roll in a safe arc. That is the textbook explanation, and it is wrong.

The egg you are imagining occupies one band out of five.

Fifteen beginnings among our Terran kin, sorted by how far the egg gets from the body — because a material sort was tried first and collapsed.

15 ways of beginning→
Details

Fifteen beginnings among our Terran kin, sorted by how far the egg gets from the body — and the catalogue disagrees with the picture in your head. The egg you are imagining occupies one band out of five. A material sort was tried first and collapsed, because a hen's egg and a common murre's are the same object by every material measure. So: eggs that never leave, eggs grown over by skin, eggs carried, eggs set down and guarded, eggs left. In the first band, live birth turns out to have evolved at least 115 separate times in lizards and snakes alone — against roughly 140 origins in all vertebrates — and about a fifth of squamate species lay no egg at all; whether the transition ever runs backwards is genuinely contested and is left open here. A sand tiger shark shows an egg working as food: the mother goes on ovulating after mating, up to ten embryos begin, and two are born. Syngnathid fishes are the only vertebrates with male pregnancy, and it is literal — a placenta-like pouch, embryos joined to the father's circulation, a measured patrotrophy index of 1, and immune suppression to tolerate them. A Surinam toad's eggs are pressed into her back and her own skin grows over each one and builds it a lid; there is no tadpole stage, and the toadlets push the lids off after three to five months. Giant water bug eggs are cemented to the male's dorsum, and the carrying is plumbing rather than devotion: unattended eggs die every time, in air or underwater. A Banggai cardinalfish father holds the clutch in his mouth for a month and eats nothing, because a mouth cannot be a foregut and a nursery at once. Then the band everybody pictures, where four of five are strange: a kiwi egg is 15 to 22 per cent of the female (an ostrich's is 2 to 3); a monotreme egg is leathery and porous enough to be fed through its own shell, with ten days of egg followed by four to seven months of milk; and a deep-sea octopus in Monterey Canyon was revisited eighteen times over fifty-three months, guarding one clutch, never seen to feed. The murre is the entry that indicts us rather than an animal: the pointed egg was explained for decades by rolling in a tight arc, Ingold found little support in 1980, and Birkhead's objection is embarrassingly simple — the arc is wider than the ledge. Stability on a slope and shell strength do the work instead. In the last band, walking away turns out not to be the failure state: a megapode chick hatches under 40 to 150 cm of compost, digs out unaided in a median forty hours, and can fly the same day; a brine shrimp cyst waits up to eighteen dry years and resumes where it stopped; and a cuckoo's shell has a second job no other egg here has, since it must survive being looked at — matched to host retinal function rather than to our eyes, by host-specific female lineages, with the arms-race framing now under live challenge. Then the sixteenth card, dashed, with no reframe table: the egg-crack accounts, borrowed from trans communities and credited as unowned, because there is no mistaken verdict about the morning your own life rearranged itself behind you. One thing the biology does supply is a limit rather than a licence — across birds, squamates, turtles, crocodilians and the echidna, the tool that opens an egg is on the inside. You cannot do it for anyone. You can only be audible. The tenth guide to arrive independently at the same sentence: there is no standard egg.

Field Guide No. 17
A Field Guide to Resting
Field Guide · 15 + 2 · companion to No. 59

there is no standard way to rest — not an amount, not a posture, not an organ

Fifteen ways of resting among our Terran kin, sorted by what is doing the resting. An animal with no brain and no clock. A bird ten months airborne. A squirrel below freezing. And the famous twenty-hour bat, which is one measurement at one temperature.

15 entries→
Details

Companion to No. 59, and it asks the question the zine assumes an answer to: what does resting actually look like, across kin who share almost no anatomy? The bands sort by what carries the rest rather than by taxon or by amount. Hydra and Cassiopea have a nerve net and no brain and meet every behavioural criterion for sleep, and Hydra has no circadian clock either — a roughly four-hour cycle instead (Kanaya et al. 2020). C. elegans rests for two to three hours before each of its four moults (Raizen et al. 2008). Great frigatebirds sleep 42 minutes a day aloft, in the soar and never while flapping; common swifts spend over 99 per cent of a ten-month phase airborne, and the record is not uniform — some individuals roosted in midwinter, others never landed (Hedenström et al. 2016). Sperm whales hang vertically and go silent, unresponsive to a boat until it touched them (Miller et al. 2008). Bears keep their skeleton through six months of disuse; arctic ground squirrels hold a core temperature of −2.9 °C by supercooling, with no antifreeze in the plasma (Barnes 1989); lungfish seal themselves in a cocoon of living cells for years. Emperor penguins reach 37.5 °C inside a huddle while it is −19 °C outside, and two thirds of the saving is geometry rather than warmth (Gilbert et al. 2008). Parrotfish build a mucus tent for 2.5 per cent of a day's energy and cut parasite attacks from nearly all to about one in ten (Grutter et al. 2011). Northern fur seals suppress REM for weeks and never pay it back. And the card the guide would keep if it could keep one: the sleepiest-mammal figure of 19.9 hours is a single 1960s measurement at 33 °C on an unknown number of animals, which becomes 11.0 hours at 26 °C — and about one per cent of mammal species have had their sleep measured at all (Harding et al. 2022). Two cards carry no reframe table: sponges, because nobody knows yet and writing actually over an open question is bluff; and plants, because nyctinasty was named by Linnaeus and Darwin himself called the daytime version “improperly” sleep, read here from the 1880 primary. Seventeen cards, fifteen entries.

Field Guide No. 18
A Field Guide to Unfinished Animals
Field Guide · 16 + 2 · companion to No. 61

there is no final draft — and what varies here is not the animals, it is us

Sixteen animals we reconstructed wrong, sorted by the kind of mistake and tagged for whether the argument is over. Drawn upside down, then back-to-front. Three animals in three groups. A skull on a tail. Two arms for forty-four years.

16 entries→
Details

Companion to No. 61, and it asks the question that zine assumes an answer to: how often does the record actually move? Sixteen animals we reconstructed wrong, sorted by the kind of mistake, and — this guide’s one addition to the house form, borrowed from How We Got Here — every card carries a status: revised (ten cards), contested (four), or name stuck (two). A catalogue of corrections that will not say which corrections are finished would read as a list of settled triumphs, which is the smoothing it is complaining about. Assembled wrong: Hallucigenia was published in 1977 walking on its spines, inverted by Ramsköld and Hou in 1991, and turned end-for-end by Smith and Caron in 2015 when they found the eyes, mouth ring and tooth-lined throat at the other end — wrong on one axis for fourteen years and on the other for twenty-four more. Anomalocaris spent most of a century as three animals in three groups: a shrimp-like appendage, the “jellyfish” Peytoia, and the “sponge” Laggania, united by Whittington and Briggs in 1985 — and then the correction was itself corrected, when Daley and Bergström found a three-plate oral cone in 2012 and Peytoia became valid again. Cope put the skull of Elasmosaurus on the end of its neck in 1868, reading a neck as a tail; and the famous story about who caught him is wrong — it was Joseph Leidy, on 8 March 1870, with Marsh in the room, and Marsh’s claim survives only in a newspaper piece two decades later. Mantell put the Iguanodon thumb spike on its nose; it took thirty-odd whole skeletons at Bernissart for Dollo to move it to the hand. Known from almost nothing: Deinocheirus was two arms found on 9 July 1965 and named in 1970 by Halszka Osmólska and Ewa Roniewicz, and it stayed honestly unplaced for forty-four years until two near-complete skeletons showed a humpbacked, duck-billed omnivore. The Spinosaurus holotype burned in the Munich bombing of 24–25 April 1944, after Stromer’s requests to move the collection were refused — so every reconstruction since argues from photographs, and the aquatic reading is contested. Helicoprion’s tooth whorl was placed on the snout, the upper jaw, the back and the tail before a 2013 CT scan put it in the lower jaw — of a specimen that had been in a drawer since 1950. The line moved: Brontosaurus (the short form of the zine). Torosaurus, proposed as merely a mature Triceratops in 2010 and rejected in 2012, still unresolved — the mirror image of the Brontosaurus case, a merge rather than a split, with the opposite fate. Nanotyrannus, dismissed for forty years as a juvenile T. rex and reinstated in 2025 by Zanno and Napoli on a near-adult skeleton from the Duelling Dinosaurs block, with a second species nobody had separated out. And Tanystropheus, one species until a CT scan made it two, both size classes fully grown. Three of those four errors were the same error: explaining a difference by age. The name outlived it: Oviraptor means egg seizer and it was brooding its own clutch — and the card is careful that the 1995 brooding specimen is an oviraptorid later named Citipati, not Osborn’s animal, because the tidy version of that story is the one that travels. Basilosaurus means king lizard and Owen showed it was a mammal within five years; he proposed Zeuglodon and priority refused it. Both names are permanent, which is the band the zine cares about: a label outlives its refutation by rule or by repetition, and only one of those admits what it is doing. Tidier than the evidence: the fish-crawls-out story has a date problem — Devonian trackways about eighteen million years older than the earliest tetrapod bodies — and the retelling of that correction is smoothed too, because those trackways are themselves disputed. The film raptor is not the fossil one. And the tail-dragging dinosaur was corrected by a whole field changing its mind, from one 1969 monograph. Two cards are dashed and carry no reframe table. Mary Anning found, prepared and drew the first complete plesiosaur in 1823 and Conybeare presented it without naming her; women could not join the Geological Society until 1904, and on her only London trip no label carried her name. She is not a specimen, and running sounds like / actually down the side of a person would file her with the misreconstructed animals, which is the injury. And the last card has nothing to correct yet: on the guide’s own argument some entries above are currently wrong, and it says where to expect it — the age explanation, the fragment that fits every reading, and the instrument that arrives before the answer. Arrival line: there is no final draft, and the guide says plainly why it departs from the house formula: what varies here is not the animals but us. Eighteen cards, sixteen entries.

Field Guide No. 19
A Field Guide to the Ways of Being Family
Field Guide · 13 + 1 · companion to No. 64

there is no standard family — and what varies is not how related anybody is, it is what the care runs on

Thirteen ways of being family among our Terran kin, sorted by what the care actually flows through. A ledger. A coalition of strangers. A boundary crossed. A pile of pups nobody can claim.

13 entries→
Details

Companion to No. 64, and it asks the question that zine leaves open: if care is not keyed to relatedness, what is it keyed to? The sort is by mechanism, because the obvious sort — a line from kin to non-kin — collapsed on the first entry that had to go at both ends of it. In superb fairy-wrens, 76% of offspring (138 of 181) were sired by extra-group males who contribute no care, and 95% of broods contained such young; every male in the group feeds the brood anyway (Mulder, Dunn, Cockburn, Lazenby-Cohen & Howell, 1994). Greater anis — Riehl's own study species — share one clutch between unrelated pairs and show no kin discrimination at all. Pied kingfishers admit an unrelated helper only when conditions are poor enough that the pair cannot feed the brood alone, which is hospitality that began in need and is kept in the entry rather than trimmed. Two bonobo females at Wamba adopted infants from a different social group, nursing them, with no aggression from the group — the first such record in any great ape (Tokuyama et al., 2021). A bottlenose dolphin at Rangiroa reared a presumed melon-headed whale calf alongside her own for about three years (Carzon et al., 2019). Banded mongooses give birth 64% on the same night into one communal litter, and escorts show no assortment by parentage — the literature's own phrase for the effect is a veil of ignorance. Section five exists because 55% of cooperatively breeding birds really do nest primarily with kin: long-tailed tits find their relatives by call similarity, and post-reproductive killer whale grandmothers help more than breeding ones, because a grandmother with her own calf has less to give. And section six cuts against the guide's own grain — Komdeur's translocated Seychelles warblers stay as helpers on good territories rather than breed on poor ones, so constraint is arithmetic and not a wall; and the Damaraland mole-rat, the textbook eusocial mammal, turns out not to be an obligate cooperative breeder at all. An overclaim in our own favour is still an overclaim. The families people make get the fourteenth card and no reframe table, because chosen family is already the most adjudicated arrangement in the catalogue and does not need another verdict from us.

Field Guide No. 20
A Field Guide to Rainbows
Field Guide · 16 + 3 · companion to Nos. 69–70

there is no standard rainbow — and rarity, it turns out, is a fact about where the observer was standing

Sixteen rainbows sorted by what physically changed — the drop, the path inside it, the light arriving, the surface the cone meets, the instrument at its point. Not a rarity countdown.

16 entries→
Details

The catalogue for No. 69 and No. 70, and the guide that takes on a whole genre: every listicle of rainbow kinds sorts by appearance and then ranks by rarity, and both sorts fail. Appearance fails twice, in opposite directions — a fogbow looks nothing like a rainbow and differs only in drop size, its colours all present and diffracted so wide that they overlap into white; a sea spray bow looks identical to a rainbow and is genuinely a different bow, because salt raises the refractive index and shrinks the radius by about 0.8°, so the two stand visibly misaligned in one sky. Rarity fails because it is not a property of any bow: a circumhorizontal arc needs the sun above 58°, which is impossible north of 55°N and happens for roughly 140 hours a year in London against 670 in Los Angeles. Supernumerary arcs cannot be produced by geometric optics at all — Thomas Young explained them by interference in 1804 and they became evidence for the wave theory of light; Airy dismissed him for predicting the wrong angles, and Young had merely missed a 90° phase shift. Third- and fourth-order bows stand around the sun and waited until 2011 for a photograph, found by people who knew exactly where to look. A moonbow carries every colour and a long exposure records them: it is the rods, not the bow, that cannot report them. A dewbow is a hyperbola because a cone met a plane, and a full-circle bow is the only kind there is, seen from somewhere the ground is not in the way. Rainbow light is about 96% polarised, so a lens can switch it off while the sky is unchanged. And the glory is carded as openly unfinished — the surface-wave consensus holds and the precise mechanism is still called obscure. The section on what the light did inside the drop is led by the double rainbow, carded with its mechanism: one bounce leaves at about 42.7°, two at about 50° with the colour order reversed, the outer bow is 43% as bright because a longer path loses more light, and Alexander’s dark band between them is a direction into which neither bow returns any light at all. The mechanism is the catalogue’s; the argument that “secondary” is a rank the physics does not contain stays No. 70’s. Three cards get no reframe table: the ice halos, because they are not rainbows; the antisolar cone — that the bow is centred on your own shadow and no two people see the same one — which is the whole of No. 69; and the flag and the song, which live at the Sound rack. Unique to each observer is not the same as unreal — the guide refuses the word illusion on its own page.

Field Guide No. 21
A Field Guide to Third Nature
Field Guide · 12 + 1 · companion to No. 74

a support nobody named is a support nobody defends

Thirteen habitats we made by accident — and, on every card, the improvement now removing it. Insulation. Cleaner seed. Remediation. Regeneration. None of them should be reversed.

12 entries→
Details

The catalogue for No. 74, built out of that chain’s tenth link: matsutake became rare across Japan by abandonment rather than destruction, because fossil fuel replaced firewood and the woodlands nobody was cutting grew too shady for pine. That turned out not to be a special case. Anna Tsing’s third nature is what manages to live despite capitalism; this asks what it is actually living on. The sort is by what made the habitat — the work, the structure, the damage — because the sort we tried first, by how damaged the place is, collapsed: a hay meadow and a lead-poisoned spoil heap sit at opposite ends of any damage scale and are the same kind of object here. Corncockle did not tolerate farming, it was farming — harvested, stored and re-sown with the grain until better seed cleaning screened its large seeds out. The corncrake needed a late, slow cut, and when the requirement was finally written into a scheme the population rose from 480 to 589 calling males in five years, reached 1,289 by 2014, and slipped again when the prescriptions changed. The high brown fritillary is the entry that embarrasses an instinct: leave the woodland alone is the mechanism of its loss, because it needed the coppice rotation to keep opening clearings. Black redstarts, a bird of Alpine scree, bred on Blitz rubble and declined city by city with regeneration; 27–74 pairs remain and they now breed on land defined as available for development. Swifts nest in gaps that insulation closes. Chimney swifts we assumed were chimney-dependent — recent work finds many still nesting in big hollow trees, which changes what to do, and a guide about unnamed supports should be the last place to invent a dependence that is not there. East of the Rockies every documented purple martin nests in a box a person put up, an arrangement recorded in Choctaw and Chickasaw gourd housing in the 1700s; nothing needs to be improved to end it, only ignored. Calaminarian grassland is Annex I habitat 6130 — a legally protected community held open by lead and zinc poisoning, whose spring sandwort miners called leadwort because it showed them where the galena was; the North Pennines survey records a lichen abundant at Nenthead smeltmill “until the restoration of Nenthead Mines.” Oil platforms off California are among the most productive fish habitat per unit area measured, and decommissioning law says remove them. The London Underground mosquito is the card where we had it wrong: a 2025 genomic study puts molestus’ origin in ancient Mediterranean agriculture, not the Tube — the accidental habitat was real and three orders of magnitude older, and nobody was careless. The peregrine goes the other way and is here so the guide is not a mood. The Chernobyl Exclusion Zone gets a dashed card and no reframe table, because Deryabina et al. and Møller & Mousseau genuinely disagree and filling in an actually column would be picking a winner in a live dispute. Refuses damage-as-good, ruin as an aesthetic, “nature is resilient,” and any suggestion that the improvements should be undone.

The limit the form found on itself

The best thing this collection has produced is a place where the format stops, and it was the tenth guide that found it.

Being a Turtle holds twelve animals and then, in a separate section behind a different card, five turtles that people made: Turtle Island, ao, Kurma, Great A'Tuin, and a Larrakia Star Dreaming it cites rather than retells. They are not entries. They have a dashed border instead of a solid one, and they do not get a reframe table — because the reframe table is a machine for correcting a mistaken verdict, and running “sounds like / actually” over somebody's cosmology would be obscene.

The guide says so on its own face, and then makes the point structurally rather than only in prose: the format difference is the argument. A catalogue that had quietly filed five cosmologies as specimens would have been a tidier page and a much worse one. This is the sharpest thing the form has learned about itself, and it is now the standing rule — if a subject cannot take the apparatus, it gets a different card, not a smaller one.

The drift

Four of the first five guides were cosmological. Guides six through eleven are all biological. Constellations, elements, worlds and stars gave way to symbioses, nervous systems, migration, sharks, turtles and tortoises — and nobody decided that.

It is easy to see why it happened: animals come with better-documented individual cases, and the deficit vocabulary this form exists to dismantle is thicker in biology than in astronomy. It is also worth naming, because a collection that drifts without noticing has stopped choosing. The sky has not run out of catalogues.

Naming it worked, which is the only reason this paragraph is worth keeping. This section previously ended by saying the honest lead was “a guide that goes back to the sky, or to something that is neither animal nor star.” My Monotropic Galaxy is both at once — cosmic vocabulary throughout, and a subject that is neither animal nor star but a person's inner life. The drift is broken at twelve. Writing the gap down on a public page is what made it get filled.

And then the thirteenth went straight back to animals, which is worth admitting in the same breath. The Ways of Being Alone is wolves, bees, pandas, pumas and apes — the drift's exact shape again, one guide after it was named as broken. It arrived that way because it came from Betsy Selvam's essay on Autistic solitude and went looking for what our kin do about company, and biology is where that question is answered. That is a reason, not an excuse. One guide out of the pattern does not make a habit; the sky still has not run out of catalogues.

Not

Not a cabinet of curiosities. These are not weird creatures assembled to be marvelled at. Every entry is an ordinary member of its group doing an ordinary thing, and the surprise is only ever about how narrowly we had drawn the category.

Not diagnosis by analogy. The octopus is not autistic. The slime mold is not a metaphor for anybody's cognition. Where a guide reaches for a human parallel it marks it as a reach, and the entries themselves stay about the animals.

Not proof. Biology does not settle questions about human belonging, and the naturalistic fallacy is a trap this form walks straight past if nobody holds it. A rhyme, not a proof is in these guides because it has to be.

Not settled where it isn't. The symbioses guide keeps root-to-fungus mycorrhizal trade, which is settled, and hedges the forest-wide “wood wide web” story, which Karst, Jones & Hoeksema found insufficiently supported — “Positive citation bias and overinterpreted results lead to misinformation on common mycorrhizal networks in forests,” Nature Ecology & Evolution 7, 2023. It rhymes beautifully with mutual aid, which is exactly why it slipped past us in three pieces before anyone checked.

Not a ranking, however it is read. Numbered guides and numbered entries are an index, not a league table. The number says when a guide was made; nothing in it says which entry won.

What is not here yet

Nothing is scoped and waiting, which matches Sound and differs from Print, where a sheet sits deliberately unbuilt.

The lead this section used to carry has been taken up. It read: a guide that goes back to the sky, or to something that is neither animal nor star. My Monotropic Galaxy answered it on both counts, and it is left recorded here rather than quietly deleted, because a gap that gets filled is the evidence that naming gaps in public does something.

What is open now is narrower and comes from the twelfth guide rather than from the drift. It ends by asking readers to draw their own constellations, and the site gave them nothing to draw on — so The Constellation Sheet was built next and sits in Print: eighty stars, nothing joined, and a blank index on the reverse. It was held back one pass on purpose, so the guest guide could ship as Helen wrote it before we started adding things around it. Naming it here rather than keeping it on a private list is the same practice as everywhere else on this site — the list of what we have not done is part of the record.
