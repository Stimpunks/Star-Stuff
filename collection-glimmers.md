# *Glimmers*

A Star Stuff collection · 5 pieces · started 31 August 2026

Hopeful science news, told with a Star Stuff angle. News that stokes our senses of wonder, awe, interdependence and belonging — written close to the event, dated on its face, and held to exactly the same sourcing standard as the slow work.

---

Good news, written while it is still news. A launch works, an instrument sees something, a number moves the right way — and we write it up that week.

Entries are dated instead of numbered, and each carries two dates: when the thing happened, and when we wrote about it. Anything written this quickly was written with less than the full picture, and the gap is yours to see.

Quick is not loose. Where the name comes from — including the part that argues against us — is below the cards.

### [Which Distortion](https://starstuff.earth/which-distortion.html)

5 September 2026 · Glimmers · Ryan Boren

*Nobody at any step became more accurate. They got better at choosing, and better at saying so.*

The General Assembly adopted the “Correct the map” resolution, 164 votes to 1. Three equal-area projections, computed from their own equations, all enclose exactly 4π and all disagree about shape.

**Details**

**Every headline called Equal Earth the more accurate map, and that is the one thing the mathematics forbids — but the resolution never says it.** A/80/L.104 passed 164–1–6 on 4 September 2026, sponsored by 55 African states and the Bahamas, introduced by Togo for the African Union; the United States voted against and called it “superfluous”. Its preamble states that *“no single projection can perfectly represent the curved surface of the Earth on a plane, and… each projection is therefore suited to particular purposes”* — the [Theorema Egregium](https://starstuff.earth/cannot-flatten-a-sphere-zine.html) in the voice of a UN resolution — and operative paragraph 3 claims only “a more accurate representation of the **sizes**” from “a relevant option”, urged “where their sizes are critical”. **Three equal-area projections were computed here from their published equations** rather than drawn by eye: Gall–Peters, Eckert IV and Equal Earth each enclose exactly 4π square units, each has a local area scale of 1.000000 at every sampled point, and each pays a different price in shape — 2.00×, 1.40× and 1.35× at the equator. **Gall–Peters, the map most people mean by “the fair map”, is worst at exactly the latitude the campaign is about**, which is why three cartographers built Equal Earth in 2018 after Boston Public Schools adopted Peters in 2017 — their own stated motivation. And hours before the vote France announced it was adopting **Eckert IV instead**, a different equal-area map: two supporters, one afternoon, two answers, which is the consensus working rather than failing. **The news nobody reported is paragraph 10**, which urges states to explain projections’ “characteristics, limitations and appropriate applications” and to put the “critical teaching of cartography” in schools. That is Tissot’s 1859 move — you cannot remove the distortion, so draw it — made policy. Publish the indicatrix.

### [Until There Were Ten](https://starstuff.earth/until-there-were-ten.html)

2 September 2026 · Glimmers · Ryan Boren

*A thing that never changes and has no counterexample is very hard to tell apart from a rule.*

Saturn's hexagon was the only one of its kind for forty-four years. There is now a decagon at the south pole — drawn unevenly, wobbling on a 32.5-day cycle, and possibly temporary.

**Details**

**The tidy version of this story is *two poles, two polygons, how balanced*, and the paper refuses to let you have it.** Saturn's hexagon sits near 78.5°N, was found by Voyager in 1980–81, and has been in every image since — a lifetime exceeding 44 years with no counterexample anywhere on the planet. The south pole was tipped out of view from mid-2012 until 2023; when it came back there was a ten-sided wave at 58°S to 63°S, wavelength 16,782 ± 1,100 km. **But it is not the hexagon mirrored.** Where the hexagon's sides and corners are equally contrasted, the decagon is *“not uniform in longitude”*: three corners very prominent, four less so, three barely defined. Its corners oscillate on a mean 32.5-day period with amplitudes of 4.6° to 8.4°, and the wobble travels — each corner peaking about 2.7 days after its neighbour — which the authors call *“a complex dynamical behavior, never observed in the hexagon.”* It did not exist in Saturn's previous year, and it *“could be a transient evolving phenomenon, whereas the hexagon is a robust wave.”* **Two candidate causes and no way yet to choose:** a jet instability, or a 4,000-km red anticyclone at 55°S that resembles one Voyager saw in 1981. Three shallow-water runs, none reproducing the observed drift. Also: a shape drifting at 2.5 m/s inside a 116 m/s jet, so the wind goes through the corners and the corners stay put; a jet whose strength has not changed substantially since 1981; and two of the fourteen authors affiliated to a private observatory in the Australian outback and a French astronomy association rather than a university. **The turn is epistemic, not decorative:** one example, held still for forty-four years, had looked exactly like a law.

### [Came Back Clean](https://starstuff.earth/came-back-clean.html)

31 August 2026 · Glimmers · Ryan Boren

*The pair that comes back clean is the one to worry about.*

A thousand people buried two thousand pairs of cotton underpants across Switzerland. Land use mattered more than anything else — and the ground that came back cleanest was the lawn.

**Details**

**The intuition runs the wrong way, which is why the finding is worth having.** Cotton is nearly pure cellulose, so a buried pair of underpants is a standardised meal: how much disappears tells you how much life was down there eating. About a thousand volunteers ran it at a thousand Swiss sites for the University of Zurich and Agroscope, under the excellent project name *Beweisstück Unterhose* — Proof by Underpants — and land use turned out to matter more than soil chemistry, texture, climate or altitude. **Private gardens had the most soil life of any land type studied. Lawns had the least.** The most managed surface most of us own is the deadest, which is [No. 8](https://starstuff.earth/the-universe-runs-on-difference-zine.html)’s argument about monoculture arriving as a measurement rather than a metaphor. **Around 240 of the citizen scientists are listed as co-authors** — not acknowledged, not thanked in a footnote, authors — which is a claim about who counts as a knower and is the methods section rather than a slogan. Also: the Tea Bag Index and the published critique that says macrofauna damage gets counted as decomposition, an objection that applies harder to a whole pair of underpants; the 59%-of-global-biodiversity figure printed with its ±15 and its live argument; and the burial protocol, so you can run it in your own garden. **Fig. 1 is adapted from the paper’s own scale under CC BY 4.0** — the same licence question *Flying Free* asked and could not clear, asked again and answered yes.

### [None of Those Miles](https://starstuff.earth/none-of-those-miles.html)

31 August 2026 · Glimmers · Ryan Boren

*It’s happening. It’s really happening. It’s finally happening.*

Sunlight is the cheapest electricity we have ever made — with a fourteen-word condition the headline keeps dropping. Four diagrams, a learning curve derived from the data, and a supply line with no gate in it.

**Details**

**The good news is real and it is conditional, and the piece keeps both halves.** The IEA’s 2020 finding was that *“for projects with low cost financing that tap high quality resources, solar PV is now the cheapest source of electricity in history”* — and the version that travels drops the fourteen-word condition and swaps *electricity* for *energy*, which silently extends a claim about 22% of the system to all of it. **That is the same fault [Rainbow Serpents](https://starstuff.earth/rainbow-serpents.html) is about, two days later in a different field**, and this page nearly committed it. **The mechanism is a learning curve, and it is derived here rather than quoted:** module prices fell from $128.27 to $0.258 per watt between 1975 and 2024 — a factor of 497 — while cumulative capacity grew 3.4 million times, 21.7 doublings. Fitted on log–log axes that is 23.5% off the price per doubling at R² = 0.979. **It is called Swanson’s law and it is not a law:** prices rose in six of those fifty years, 2022 by 10.2%. Nobody invented cheap solar; thousands of people iterated it into existence in public, and two governments paid for the early expensive units. Also: silicon as the eighth most abundant element in the universe and a product of massive stars, so a panel is star stuff arranged to catch starlight, both halves literal; the 250-year sequence of US energy crossovers, read twice, because *a crossover is not a subtraction*; community solar as a watering hole; and Bill McKibben’s line about ninety-three million miles, quoted from a dated source after finding that its three circulating versions do not match each other.

### [Flying Free](https://starstuff.earth/flying-free.html)

30 August 2026 · Glimmers · Ryan Boren

*I needed to witness something hopeful and wonder-filled, something to make me feel good about humanity.*

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope left Kennedy at 7:26 in the morning, bound for Lagrange Two. On the teamwork that put it there, and why the most famously balanced place in the sky still needs its thrusters.

**Details**

Roman lifted off from Launch Complex 39A on a Falcon Heavy and separated 31 minutes later, beginning a hundred-day cruise to a halo orbit 1.5 million kilometres out. The piece takes the launch broadcast at its word and then checks it. **Two of the three things Roman is built to measure have no agreed explanation at all** — NASA’s own page says dark energy is “just the name that astronomers gave to the mysterious ‘something’” doing it — and the third, the exoplanet census, works only because two detection methods with *opposite* blind spots are running at once. Shawn Domagal-Goldman called the result the ecology of the known universe, which is exact: ecology needs the whole stand of trees, because a range is something you count rather than photograph. **The best fact is the one usually left out of the L2 story.** Nothing parks at Lagrange Two; it is an unstable equilibrium, and an unattended spacecraft drifts off in about three weeks. Roman will fire thrusters on a schedule, forever, with fuel budgeted for it from the start — and nobody at NASA files that under failure to be independent. A rhyme, not a proof. Also: Nancy Grace Roman, told by a physics department that he usually dissuaded women but she *might* make it; a mirror handed on from the National Reconnaissance Office; four budget requests that proposed cancelling the mission and the appropriations that saved it each time; and one word from the broadcast — *superpower* — that stays with the telescope and does not come home with us.

How this collection works

## What holds these together

Everything else on this site is slow on purpose. A zine takes one settled, checkable fact and follows it until the claim about belonging is already inside it — and settled facts can wait, which is why that method works. **News cannot wait, and that is the whole reason this collection exists as a separate place rather than as a looser standard inside an existing one.**

A glimmer here is a piece of science news that made us feel better about being alive among other people: a launch that worked, an instrument that saw something, a finding that widens rather than narrows what counts as ordinary. Written while the feeling is still on, and then checked as carefully as anything else here.

The four registers we care about are the ones in the name of the thing: **wonder**, **awe**, **interdependence** and **belonging**. Interdependence is doing more work in that list than it looks. Most of what lands here will be about a large number of people managing to build something together over decades, which is the least reported and most remarkable part of any launch.

## The axis is occasion, and that is a new one here

Every other collection on this site says on its own face how it sorts, and this one owes the same disclosure. [Star Stuff](https://starstuff.earth/collection-star-stuff.html), [Star Gazing](https://starstuff.earth/collection-star-gazing.html), [More Than Human](https://starstuff.earth/collection-more-than-human.html), [Kin](https://starstuff.earth/collection-kin.html) and [Stars We Grew Up On](https://starstuff.earth/collection-stars-we-grew-up-on.html) sort by **register** — what kind of argument a piece is making. [Field Guides](https://starstuff.earth/collection-field-guides.html) and [How We Got Here](https://starstuff.earth/collection-how-we-got-here.html) sort by **form**. [Print](https://starstuff.earth/collection-print.html) and [Sound](https://starstuff.earth/collection-sound.html) sort by **medium**.

**Glimmers sorts by occasion.** A piece is here because something happened on a particular day and we wanted to say so while it was still happening. That is a fourth axis, and naming it is the price of adding one.

The practical consequences are three, and they are all constraints rather than permissions:

- **Dated on its face.** Every entry carries the date of the event and the date of publication, because a piece written close to an event is a piece written with less than the full picture, and the reader is owed the ability to see how close.
- **Unnumbered.** Zines carry a number that records *when*; that sequence is load-bearing across the changelog, the fact-check ledger and years of prose references. Glimmers entries are dated instead. A date says *when* better than a number does, and the number sequence should not have news inserted into it.
- **Same eight gates, same ledger, same changelog.** Faster to write is not looser. Every claim traces to a primary or agency source, every quotation is graded, and the entry gets its row in the fact-check ledger like anything else. *Timeliness is not a discount.*

## Where the name comes from, including the part that goes against us

**Glimmer is Deb Dana’s word**, coined in 2018 in *The Polyvagal Theory in Therapy* as the counterpart to a *trigger*: the small moment in which a nervous system registers that things are all right. It arrived inside **polyvagal theory, which is influential and scientifically contested**, and we say that every time we use the word.

**Helen Edgar** gives the reading this collection actually works from, in [*Glimmers: Autistic Joy and Monotropism*](https://autisticrealms.com/glimmers-autistic-joy-and-monotropism/): a glimmer need not relate to polyvagal theory at all, being simply the opposite of a trigger — a moment of joy or comfort, sensory or cognitive. [One Word for the Opposite](https://starstuff.earth/one-word-for-the-opposite-zine.html) (No. 80) is the piece that argues the general case, and its finding is an asymmetry anyone can check: trauma work built a dozen words for a nervous system going badly, and the opposite got one, in 2018.

Now the part that goes against us, because leaving it out would make this page a worse version of itself.

**Two days before this collection was named, we took the word *out* of a zine for cause.** [No. 78](https://starstuff.earth/you-cannot-correct-the-sky-zine.html) shipped on 29 August 2026 framed around glimmers, and was repointed within hours. The reason was sound and it is worth stating exactly: a piece in *Star Stuff* has to rest on one settled fact, glimmers has none under it, so the argument was correctly moved onto optics — and then the word was kept as the frame after its foundation had been removed. The piece ended up more rigorous and less alive, pointing at something it could no longer reach.

**So why is it a good name for a collection when it was a bad frame for a zine?** Because those are different jobs, and the failure was specific. A zine’s frame is load-bearing — it is the thing the argument stands on, and it has to hold the weight. **A collection name is not a foundation. It is a label on a shelf**, and what it has to be is *true about the pieces on it*. These pieces are moments of registering that things are all right. That is the word, used for exactly what it means, carrying nothing.

The rest of the argument — that a good word does not stop being good when the theory it arrived in is in trouble, because words and theories are different objects with different failure modes — is already made at length on [No. 80](https://starstuff.earth/one-word-for-the-opposite-zine.html), and we are not going to re-make it here.

## Why this page opened at one member — and closed that gap the same day

This site holds that **collections are outputs, not inputs** — they get made when a form has already been observed in finished work, never as a folder opened in advance. The floor is two members. *Stars We Grew Up On* carried exactly two for a long time; *How We Got Here* was deliberately deferred until a second chain arrived.

**Glimmers opens at one, which breaks that floor, and the break is deliberate rather than an oversight.** The reasoning: a running log is defined by its *cadence*, not by an accumulated form, and cadence is the one property you cannot observe retroactively. Waiting for a second entry would mean holding the first one until it was no longer news — which is to say, destroying the exact property the collection is being made to hold. [Easter Eggs](https://starstuff.earth/collection-easter-eggs.html) broke the same floor at one member, for its own stated reason, and the precedent there is the relevant one: **a break that is written down on a visible page is a better break than a quiet exception inside a tool.**

It is Ryan’s call, made on 31 August 2026, and it is recorded here rather than left to look like drift.

**The gap closed the same day.** *None of Those Miles* arrived hours after *Flying Free*, which puts this collection at the two-member floor after all — the same way [How We Got Here](https://starstuff.earth/collection-how-we-got-here.html) was deferred and then created the day its second chain turned up. **That does not retire the argument above, and the argument is the part worth keeping:** if the second entry had taken three weeks, the first would still have been published here on its own, because a log defined by cadence cannot wait to be sure of itself. The floor was broken on purpose and then met by accident. Both facts stay on the page.

Not

**Not a looser standard.** Written faster, checked the same. Every entry passes the same eight gates, carries the same fact-check ledger row, and grades its own quotations. Where a claim is contested, it is marked contested — including on pages belonging to institutions we are cheering for.

**Not a press-release feed.** An agency announcing something is a lead, not a citation, exactly as an aggregator is. Being glad about a finding is not a reason to stop checking it, and enthusiasm is the condition under which checking is most often skipped.

**Not toxic positivity, and not a rule about how to feel.** Noticing a glimmer fixes nothing, and it is not offered as advice to anyone who is drowning. There is no obligation here to be cheered up. This is a shelf for things that happened to cheer *us* up, published in case that is useful.

**Not a naturalistic fallacy.** Nothing in the sky owes anyone anything, and no value is derived from a fact anywhere in this collection. Where a piece of physics rhymes with something true about how people are treated, the rhyme is marked as a rhyme and not promoted to evidence.

**Not restricted to space.** The first entry is a telescope because a telescope launched. The collection is for any science news that does the work — biology, ecology, materials, anything.
