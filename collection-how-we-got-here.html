How We Got Here

A Star Stuff collection · 13 pieces

One link per spread, from a settled fact in physical science to something that is happening to us now — and the strength of every link printed on its face.

Why we make these

To take science back from the things that wear its clothes. Eugenics and behaviourism did not fail because they were too scientific. They took real mathematics — a curve built for observational error, a law of effect measured honestly in cats — and applied it to human beings without the discipline that made it work in the first place. That is what every chain in this collection turns out to be about.

Three things get mistaken for science and are its opposite. Pseudo-science, which has none of the method. Scientism, which keeps the authority and drops the practice — the p-value and the phrase evidence-based without the pre-registration, the blinding, the reported harms, the published null. And the smoothed popular retelling, which files off every hedge until a contested finding arrives as a fact, because the tidy version travels further.

When those tools get pointed at people, they flatten exactly the variation the science elsewhere insists is real — and they measure the surface, badly. The result is epistemic injustice with a citation attached, which is far harder to answer than a plain insult. So these chains are not arguments against evidence. They are arguments for the parts that got left behind on the way here.

What holds these together

This collection sorts by form, not by register — the same odd axis Field Guides sorts on, and we say so here rather than let it read as an oversight. Everywhere else on this site a piece follows one fact down. A chain follows many facts sideways, across a century or two, and what carries the argument is not any single fact but the handoffs between them.

The shape comes from James Burke's Connections (BBC, 1978), which Cosmic Connections already names as one of the two lineages inside this site's tagline — the other being Carl Sagan's The Cosmic Connection (1973). Burke's argument is that nothing in the modern world can be understood in isolation, because it was assembled out of centuries of unrelated people solving unrelated problems for reasons of their own, none of them aiming at what they produced.

The name is borrowed twice over, and honestly. How did we get here is the title of the paper underneath No. 39 — Randall, Ewing, Marr, Jimenez and Bourouiba's reconstruction of the five-micron myth. We noticed the phrase fit the whole form only after two of these existed.

Why the joints are marked

Here is the problem with the form, stated plainly: a chain is built to feel inevitable. Each link arrives just as the last one lands, the story accelerates, and by the end a reader has been persuaded by momentum rather than by evidence. Burke's chains are wonderful and some of his links are frankly loose. This site's standard is to verify everything and to say what we could not verify. Those two things are in direct tension.

So the form carries the epistemics instead of a footnote doing it. Every link is claimed at one of three strengths, said in the prose and drawn into the running spine at the foot of each spread:

documented There is a source for the causal link itself, not merely for the two things it joins. A supervision, a citation, a person acknowledging the debt in print.
contested The connection is argued, inferred, or actively disputed in current scholarship — including by the historians we are relying on. Where they hedge, we hedge.
leap We are rhyming, and we say so. A resonance worth drawing that licenses nothing. The star-stuff bridge in No. 39 is tagged this way on purpose.

This is not decoration, and the test is whether the mix is real. In No. 38: six documented, three contested, one leap — and the chain's own founding anecdote is among the contested ones, placed first so the notation is taught on the weakest link rather than the strongest. In No. 39 the load-bearing joint is marked contested because the paper reconstructing it will only call the connection likely, having found no explicit statement of it anywhere in the literature they reviewed.

What every chain here ends up saying

Nobody authored the road. Not one of the people in these chains was aiming at where it arrived. An astronomer wanted accurate transit times; a bacteriologist wanted to know how far a sneeze goes; an educator wanted children protected from arbitrary marking; an epidemiologist was preparing students for a real threat. Each solved their own problem competently, and could not have seen what it would join up with a century downstream.

That is not an excuse — the harm is not smaller for being unintended. It is a route. A thing that was accumulated can be dis-accumulated, one competent adjustment at a time, in the other direction. You do not have to defeat a conspiracy. You have to go back and read the citation.

This page was made at two

Collections here are observed, not planned — the argument Cosmic Connections makes about the whole site. Fourteen collections were noticed only after thirty-five zines had accumulated in no folders at all, and Field Guides waited until there were eleven of them.

So this one was deliberately deferred when the first chain shipped, and made when the second arrived with the same shape, the same notation and the same ending — Stars We Grew Up On having carried two numbered zines for months already. Two is the floor here, not a shortcut. The form was found in the work; the folder came after.

Three more followed within the day, which is the part worth recording rather than quietly absorbing. The page did not cause them — but it is fair to note that naming a form makes the next instance easier to see, and that is exactly the risk the deferral was guarding against. Eleven is not proof the category was real; it is what the category has attracted so far.

The tally, so far

Across the thirteen chains: 116 documented joints, 28 contested, 12 leaps. Nos. 38 and 39 carried one leap and two; Nos. 40 and 41 carried none, and said so in their colophons as something to be suspicious of rather than pleased about; No. 42 restored one. No. 56 runs documented for ten rungs and then spends both of its weak joints in a row, at the end, on the same handoff — which is what an airtight rail and a genuinely uncertain transfer look like when neither is disguised as the other. A notation whose weakest tag never fires is measuring the choice of subject, not the care of the argument. These numbers are counted from the pages rather than remembered — an earlier version of this collection got them wrong, and the correction is in the changelog.

The mix is not evenly spread, and the two ends of it are the argument for watching that. No. 52 runs twelve documented, four contested, one leap and carries the most contested joints of any chain here — not because its sourcing is worse but because its subject includes a smoothed retelling, a quarter-century disagreement about what counts as evidence, two elements that turned out not to exist, and an accusation of data selection that took sixty years to answer. A chain with no contested joints is usually a chain that did not look.

No. 50 runs eleven documented, one contested, one leap — the highest documented share of any chain here, level with No. 40, and that is a fact about its subject, not a boast. Its rail is a sequence of published resolutions, a maintained table of leap seconds and a paper in Nature; a chain built out of minutes and votes is supposed to run documented. The honesty is in where the weak tag lands. The single leap is the step from how a standards body handles its own mismatch to how institutions handle ours, it is the joint the whole zine exists for, and putting the entire weight of the argument on one link that is openly marked as the weakest is the shape this notation was invented to make possible.

Not

Not a conspiracy genre. The chains here are about accumulation, not design. If a piece in this collection ever reads as though somebody planned the outcome, it has gone wrong — that reading makes the history smaller, and it lets the accumulation off.

Not a licence to leap. The tags exist to make weak links visible, not permissible. A chain that needed three leap joints to reach its conclusion would not be publishable here; it would be a different, shorter piece.

Not history as inevitability. Every one of these chains contains a moment where somebody could plainly have done otherwise, and often somebody said so at the time and was ignored. Naming those moments is the point.

Not finished. Where a source could not be read at the primary, the piece says so on the page and the open item is logged. See the changelog for what we have got wrong and corrected.

The pieces

In the order they were made. The number records when; the collection records what.

In February 1796 the Astronomer Royal dismissed his assistant David Kinnebrook for marking star transits eight tenths of a second late. Bessel later established that observers differ from one another systematically and irreducibly — and astronomy's answer was the personal equation: publish a constant for each observer, build the variation into the model, keep the observer. This zine follows the measuring apparatus out of the observatory: Wundt's laboratory, Cattell's “mental tests,” Thorndike's puzzle boxes and his 1918 credo that whatever exists at all exists in some amount, Kelly's Kansas Silent Reading Test, Pressey's machine built from typewriter parts, Skinner's teaching machines, and Lovaas at UCLA in 1987 — while a second rail runs alongside from Galton through Kraepelin to the DSM. The two meet at a table with a child at it. The first zine built as a Burke-style chain, and every joint is marked documented, contested or leap — six solid, three dashed, one dotted — because two of them are weaker than the story wants. The turn: school found the same human variation astronomy found, and made it the thing to remove. Accommodation is the older answer. Zine No. 39
Five Microns
Stimpunks Zines

on a number that answered a different question, and the room it left you in

In March 2020 the WHO posted “FACT: COVID-19 is NOT airborne.” Not a lie — doctrine, faithfully applied, resting on a number that had quietly changed questions.

15 spreads→
Details

In March 2020 the WHO posted “FACT: COVID-19 is NOT airborne” and called the alternative misinformation. Not a lie — doctrine, faithfully applied, resting on a number that had quietly changed questions. William Wells put the boundary between droplets that fall and droplets that evaporate into lingering nuclei at 100 micrometres, and never wrote 5; his own table gives a 1 µm particle sixteen hours to fall two metres. The 5 µm figure came from industrial hygiene asking which particles reach the deepest lung — deposition, not suspension — and Alexander Langmuir fused the two in 1951, in a lecture preparing public-health students for biological warfare months after Korea began. Randall, Ewing, Marr, Jimenez and Bourouiba reconstructed it in Interface Focus under the title “How did we get here,” and refuse to solder the load-bearing joint, writing only that the deposition focus “likely” popularised the cut-off — so this zine marks it contested too. Meanwhile Flügge's team in Breslau had grown colonies nine metres from a sneeze in the 1890s, meant “droplets” to cover what we now call aerosols, waited five hours before reading their plates, and recommended ventilation. The 1–2 metre rule has its own separate lineage and turns out to match the width of a 1940s camera's field of view. The second chain zine, every joint marked documented, contested or leap. Fifteen spreads, thirteen diagrams, a running two-rail spine, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 40
Five Sigma
Stimpunks Zines

how much evidence is enough before you do something to a child

Particle physics will not permit the word “discovery” below five sigma — one chance in three and a half million — and wrote down why.

16 spreads→
Details

How much evidence is enough before you do something to a child? Particle physics answered that question carefully and wrote it down. It will not permit the word “discovery” below five sigma — a p-value of 3 × 10⁻⁷, one chance in three and a half million — and the reason is empirical rather than principled: too many 3σ and 4σ results kept evaporating. Louis Lyons, whose paper is the spine of this zine, goes further and argues the bar should not be uniform at all, grading it by degree of surprise, impact, look-elsewhere effect and systematics: single top production 3σ, the Higgs 5σ, supersymmetry 7σ, faster-than-light neutrinos above 8σ. He also prints the statisticians' objection that his own gold standard is unsound. Against all that stands p = 0.05 — about 2σ — chosen by Fisher in 1925 with the word convenient, and unexamined since. The gap is roughly 170,000×, and the asymmetry runs backwards: if physics is wrong it retracts a paper and the particle is unharmed; if we are wrong a childhood already happened and there is no more data to collect. Run Lyons' four dials on an intervention and every one reads high — then the systematics column turns out to be empty, because 150 intervention studies for young Autistic children produced 11 that mentioned adverse events at all. Not an argument against science. An argument against scientism, which keeps the authority and drops the practice. The first chain here with no leap joints. Sixteen spreads, fourteen diagrams, two rails that never meet, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 41
A Conquering Gaze from Nowhere
Stimpunks Zines

parallax, positionality, and the research done on us

For roughly two centuries the strongest objection to a moving Earth was that the stars showed no parallax. The reasoning was valid, and the conclusion wrong.

15 spreads→
Details

For roughly two centuries the strongest objection to a moving Earth was that the stars showed no parallax. The reasoning was valid, the observations careful, and the conclusion wrong — partly because the distances required seemed absurd, and partly because telescopic star discs are an artefact of the aperture rather than a property of stars. Then in 1838 Friedrich Bessel measured 61 Cygni at 0.3136 arcseconds from Königsberg — a two-centimetre coin seen from thirteen kilometres. The method carries the whole argument: the baseline is the observer's own displacement across Earth's orbit, so a viewpoint with no location has no baseline, no angle and no depth. A view from nowhere cannot measure distance even in principle; it is the blindest available position. And it is the same Bessel, at the same observatory, who gave us the personal equation in No. 38 — twice showing that knowledge requires a located, particular observer. Every rung of the cosmic distance ladder above parallax is calibrated by the one beneath, and rung two — the period–luminosity relation — was built by Henrietta Swan Leavitt, employed as a Harvard “computer,” paid by the hour, not permitted to observe, and published under her director's name: “has been prepared by Miss Leavitt.” That is the third woman in three chains to do the work and lose the byline, and the zine stops to name all three. Donna Haraway gave the fantasy its name in 1988 — the god trick, the unmarked category claiming the power to see and not be seen — and her positive claim is the one usually dropped: only partial perspective promises objective vision. She refuses relativism just as flatly, calling it “a way of being nowhere while claiming to be everywhere equally.” Then the numbers: 96% of behavioural-science samples from Western industrialised nations, 68% from the US, reported as facts about humans. Then research done on Autistic people rather than with them, and double empathy as a finding that only exists because somebody stood at the other end of the baseline. The correction is neither removing the observer nor declaring all views equal: publish your coordinates. Fifteen spreads, thirteen diagrams, a single rail with a marked baseline, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 42
Look for the Air
Stimpunks Zines

two thousand years spent deleting character from the explanation, and the industry that put it back

Aristotle explained motion by what a thing is. It is not a foolish theory — go outside and test it and every prediction checks out.

14 spreads→
Details

Aristotle's physics explained motion by what a thing is: heavy bodies fall because it is in their nature to seek the centre, faster in proportion to weight, slower in a denser medium. It is not a foolish theory — go outside and test it and every prediction checks out, because everyday observation happens in air. The error is where the explanation was put. Galileo showed the rule contradicts itself (tie a heavy body to a light one and it predicts both faster and slower), rolled balls down inclined planes, and made the strange move of reasoning about a fall with no medium at all — while the famous Leaning Tower demonstration turns out to come from a biography written decades later and is marked contested here. Newton finished it: acceleration is set by the forces acting plus one scalar property, and disposition disappears. Then, on 2 August 1971, David Scott released a geology hammer and a falcon feather together in the lunar vacuum and they landed together, on film. The difference was never in the feather. It was in the air. This chain argues that mindset marketing runs that two-thousand-year move backwards on children. Behaviourism looks like it made the same move but stopped one ring short — it deleted the interior and still left the child as the only adjustable thing. California's task force commissioned its own research, was told the associations between self-esteem and its expected consequences were “mixed, insignificant, or absent,” and shipped in 1990 regardless. Growth mindset fails the same way across 63 studies and 97,672 participants, with higher-quality studies less likely to show benefit — and Dweck and Duckworth both objected to the deployment while it kept growing. The dashboard completes it: a schema with fields for effort, focus and behaviour points, and no field for the room. Along the way the zine corrects its own source — the famous Eric Hoffer line about a movement becoming a business and degenerating into a racket is a misquotation that invents a moral arc he never wrote. It closes on scarcity and slack, on equity literacy, and on the first leap joint since No. 39, marked because it is the sentence a reader most wants to be true. Fourteen spreads, twelve diagrams, two rails running in opposite directions, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 43
The Elimination of Waste
Stimpunks Zines

on a word that meant one thing in a steam engine and another in a school

In 1824 Sadi Carnot proved a heat engine has a hard, provable ceiling on its efficiency — and that waste heat is not sloppiness, it is the fee.

15 spreads→
Details

In 1824 Sadi Carnot proved something strange: that a heat engine has a hard, provable ceiling on its efficiency, set only by the two temperatures available and beatable by no engineer who ever lives. And with it came the result that matters here — waste heat is not sloppiness, it is the fee. An engine with nowhere to dump it does not run at all. So “eliminate all waste” isn't an ambitious goal not yet reached; in thermodynamics it names the one outcome that is ruled out. (He reached this from caloric theory, which was wrong — Clausius and Kelvin supplied the formula later, so “Carnot derived the efficiency equation” is itself a smoothed retelling, and the joint is marked contested.) Then the word left the engine room, and what it shed in transit was the ceiling. Frederick Taylor's Principles of Scientific Management appeared in 1911, and Raymond Callahan's Education and the Cult of Efficiency (1962) dates what followed to the month: in July 1911, one month after Taylor's American Magazine series concluded, a school board member told the NEA that “scientific management” held a message for every teacher — and that if teachers did not voluntarily increase their efficiency, the business world would force them to. By 1916 Cubberley's textbook was listing what schools must take from the factory: good tools, specialized machinery, continuous measurement of production, and “the elimination of waste in manufacture.” Ninety-two years after Carnot, the phrase arrives in a book about children as a specification. By 1912 an educator was already complaining that teachers were “curiously protected in inefficiency.” And the question nobody asked sits in Callahan's own parenthesis: would Schmidt, the pig-iron handler, have agreed that science had reduced his fatigue? It ends at spoon theory — and then spends an entire spread arguing that this argument should not need physics at all, because you are not a heat engine and deriving rest from thermodynamics is precisely the move the collection condemns. Fifteen spreads, thirteen diagrams, a spine that crosses its own ceiling, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 50
The Sky Was Not Regular Enough
Stimpunks Zines

on cutting a standard loose from the thing it measured, and whether you say so out loud

Until 1960 a second was one 86,400th of a day, and the definition pointed at the sky. Then the instrument became steadier than the thing it measured.

16 spreads→
Details

Until 1960 a second was a piece of a day — one 86,400th of it — and the definition pointed at something. Not a prototype in a vault, but the sky: you could check it against a star. Then quartz clocks arrived and, for the first time in the history of measurement, the instrument was steadier than the Earth. Scheibe and Adelsberger reported the seasonal wobble in 1936; Stoyko put it at about two milliseconds in 1937. The Earth was never irregular to itself — it became irregular the moment we had something steadier to compare it to. So the tie was cut, twice. In 1956 the second became a fraction of the tropical year 1900, a year that had already ended and could never be re-measured; in 1967 it became 9,192,631,770 caesium transitions, and after that the definition points at no day, no year, no sky at all. Every one of those moves was an improvement, and this zine says so — the collection exists to reclaim science, not to indict it. Since 1972 the gap between the atomic standard and the turning planet has been closed in public by leap seconds: 27 of them, first on 30 June 1972, last on 31 December 2016, and none since, because the Earth started spinning faster and the correction now due is a negative leap second that has never been tested. Duncan Agnew showed in Nature in 2024 that melting Greenland and Antarctic ice slowed the spin enough to postpone it from 2026 to 2029 — climate change bought the world's timekeepers three years, and he refuses to call that good news. Resolution 4 of the 27th CGPM decided in 2022 to widen the tolerance by 2035, and the implementing vote is at the 28th CGPM this October; the proposal is a leap hour. The argument: a leap second is not a bug but an honesty mechanism — a standards body saying in public, on a named date, with a number attached, that its standard and the world disagree by exactly this much. Abolishing it is defensible engineering. What it costs is the announcement. And that is the one thing carried across to us, marked leap because caesium licenses nothing about a person: an accommodation is a leap second, and the objections to it arrive in the same order — it's irregular, it breaks the system, everybody handles it differently — with the same proposed remedy, widen the tolerance and stop announcing. Ends at processing speed and crip time: the Earth is not slow, the second left. Eleven documented joints, one contested, one leap. Sixteen spreads, thirteen diagrams, a running spine that is a plot of its own data, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 52
We Learned to Build the Eclipse
Stimpunks Zines

on an element nobody would accept because it could not be put on a bench, and on the day we stopped travelling to the Moon's shadow

A chain about how a whole branch of physics stopped waiting for the Moon — the corona, for most of astronomy's history available only during totality.

21 spreads→
Details

A chain about how a whole branch of physics stopped waiting for the Moon. The Sun’s outer atmosphere is always there and almost never available: the photosphere drowns it, so for most of astronomy’s history the corona and the prominences could only be studied during totality — a few minutes, roughly every eighteen months, along a track a few hundred kilometres wide, usually somewhere difficult, and one cloud ended the expedition. It begins in 1604, where pinhole solar diameters came out systematically too large and nobody could say why until Kepler supplied the theory of how an image forms behind a small hole: the method came first and worked badly; the understanding came second and fixed it. Then Guntur, 18 August 1868, where several observers caught a yellow line near sodium’s — and where what Janssen actually took away was not the line but its brightness. The next morning he observed the prominences again with no eclipse at all, spreading the sky’s glare thin with dispersion while the emission line stayed put. Lockyer, who had wanted this since 1866 and was blocked by glass rather than by ideas, got there on 20 October; both reports reached the Académie within days and it struck a medal for the pair — for the method. Neither report flagged the yellow line, which is the first of four contested joints: the famous sentence “helium was discovered during an eclipse in 1868” is wrong in almost every part, and what the compression deletes is years of argument. Lockyer alone later called it D3 and proposed an element; for a quarter century chemists refused it, not because the spectroscopy was faulty but because nobody could put the gas on a bench — and a thing visible only in light from 150 million kilometres away did not, to them, count as a thing. They were rigorous and they were wrong, and the zine insists on both, because the same method also produced two elements that were never there: coronium and nebulium, dissolved by Bowen in 1927 and Grotrian and Edlén in 1939 and 1942 as forbidden lines of ordinary oxygen and iron — which is also how we learned the corona is millions of degrees hotter than the surface. Ramsay got helium out of cleveite on 26 March 1895, twenty-seven years late, and it had been underfoot the whole time. Then the chain runs downhill, because capability is specific: in 1919 the Moon was needed again to see whether starlight bends, the Sobral astrograph plates were set aside, Earman and Glymour charged Eddington with seeing what he wanted, and Kennefick later established that the call was Dyson’s and that the plates could only agree with Newton if the instrument had also changed magnification — a reason to distrust them other than what they said, which is the whole difference between rejecting bad data and rejecting inconvenient data. Finally Bernard Lyot, chasing stray light up a mountain, photographed the corona with no eclipse on 12 July 1931: an eclipse at a time and place of human choosing, turning minutes per expedition into hours per clear day. We now fly manufactured occulters at other stars. The transfer, marked leap because it is one, refuses the obvious framing first: eclipses were computed years ahead, so the difficulty was never luck but a structural condition — the observation existing only there, only then, with the ocean, the freight, the years and the weather all charged to whoever needed it. An expedition demonstrates what is required and is a catastrophe as a delivery mechanism. So there are three states, not two: reach it, ask for it, or have it — and the middle one is what we named accommodation, which is the word Stimpunks' own page exists to criticise, since the problem is structural and the response is individual. Lyot did not apply for access to the corona; he built an instrument in which it is simply there, and the occulting disc is not a concession but where the precision comes from. Which is a curb cut: Michael Pachovas and friends poured cement into a crude ramp one Berkeley evening and rolled off into the night, and Berkeley cut its first official kerb on Telegraph Avenue in 1972 — while the sledgehammer version of that story is disability lore, not history, which a chain that spends a spread on smoothed retellings could not close on. Angela Glover Blackwell named the curb-cut effect in 2017, and the zine states plainly that it is an observation about what built-in conditions do and never the justification. Twenty-one spreads, twelve documented joints, four contested, one leap, a generated spine, and a “Not:” set that refuses the reading that barriers are valuable for what they reveal. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 56
The Noise Floor
Stimpunks Zines

every receiver has one, no design removes it, and the biggest discovery in modern cosmology arrived as noise two engineers spent ten months trying to get rid of

A chain about a limit nobody beats, and the rooms we build as though it were free. Johnson and Nyquist, printed on facing pages in 1928.

15 spreads→
Details

A chain about a limit nobody beats, and the rooms we build as though it were free. In July 1928 one volume of Physical Review printed the measurement and its explanation on facing pages — J. B. Johnson at 97–109, H. Nyquist at 110–113, whose first footnote is three words long: “See preceding paper.” Every conductor above absolute zero hisses, and Nyquist’s conclusion is that the effect is “a universal function of frequency, resistance and temperature and of these variables only” — not the metal, not the workmanship, since “one may be made of silver and the other of lead.” The available power in a band is kTB, and that is in the primary rather than a modern gloss on it. The part that usually goes missing is why it cannot be engineered away. Nyquist did not measure the floor, he deduced it — from the second law. Two resistors at one temperature must pass equal power both ways in every band, or a resonant circuit between them would move heat between equal-temperature bodies for free. The hiss is not permitted, it is required: a silent resistor would be a perpetual-motion machine, which is the same law that says you cannot un-stir the milk from the tea — and nobody files that under insufficient effort. Twenty years on, Shannon’s Theorem 17 turns the floor into a limit on meaning, with the sentence that closes the door: “It is not possible to transmit at a higher rate by any encoding system without a definite positive frequency of errors.” You cannot filter your way to clarity, and that is a theorem rather than a complaint. So engineering stopped hunting for a quieter resistor and changed the question: cool it, shield it, narrow the band — three moves, none of which asks the receiver to beat physics, all of which change the conditions the listening happens in. Which is exactly the instrument at Holmdel, where a maser and a reference load both sat in liquid helium at 4.2 K — a floor lowered deliberately so a remainder would mean something. A remainder was left: 3.5 ± 1.0 K, and the paper is mostly a list of what it was not, down to taping the seams with aluminium and walking a transmitter to ten positions on the ground. The pigeon story is corrected against its primary, and the correction is better than the version that travels: Wilson wrote that evicting them “obtained only a small reduction in antenna temperature” — the floor survived them, and that clause is the one that gets cut. The famous phrase “white dielectric material” is not in his Nobel lecture, and the birds are not in the 1965 paper at all. What could not be removed was “isotropic, unpolarized, and free from seasonal variations (July, 1964-April, 1965)”, and every property that made it impossible to eliminate is a property that made it the universe. Then the 1928 shape repeats mirrored — theory at 414–419, measurement at 419–421, thirty-seven years on. The most consequential discovery in modern cosmology arrived as noise two engineers spent ten months trying to get rid of. Meanwhile the transfer to rooms turns out not to be ours to make: ANSI/ASA S12.60 caps classroom background at 35 dBA because the profession already calls the quantity signal to noise ratio and wants about 15 dB of it — and its instruction is to lower the floor rather than amplify the teacher. Wilkins halved headaches by removing 100 Hz fluorescent flicker, with his own caveat that the harm fell on “a minority that is particularly affected”; Kim and de Dear found sound privacy the largest source of dissatisfaction in any building, and found open plan bought no more ease of interaction than a private office. The last two joints refuse to overclaim, on the page rather than in a footnote: the step to Autistic auditory experience is contested — there is no k for a nervous system — carried by Remington and Fairnie’s finding of increased auditory capacity, one mechanism producing both the distress and the ability, and by Rosas-Pérez’s 2026 acoustics thesis, in which no Autistic respondent among 311 noise-sensitive people found filtering easy — printed with her caveat that the whole sample was already sensitive. Her other finding is where the zine says the analogy breaks, and the break is worth more than the resemblance: no habituation, but an accumulation effect. Thermal noise is stationary; kTB is the same in the tenth hour as the first. The human pattern is cumulative. A resistor never gets tired of being warm — so the floor in a room is crueller than the floor in a circuit, not merely like it. Which also means “you’ll get used to it” is testable, and the exposure hypothesis under it rests on five participants, none noise-sensitive, and two pure tones. The closing analogy is leap, because physics entails nothing about who deserves a quiet room. What it does is remove an excuse. Fifteen spreads, ten documented joints, one contested, one leap, a spine the page calls schematic rather than letting it pass for a plot, and a coda pointing at the practice already written down. A rhyme, not a proof.

The chain the How We Got Here motive was written for, and the one where it turns on us. Darwin stated the two-level problem in Descent of Man (1871) and finished the paragraph "and this would be natural selection." Wynne-Edwards (1962) got the group-level version wrong and Williams (1966) was right to demolish it — a joint kept because any sympathetic account that skips it is selling something. Hamilton (1964) supplied inclusive fitness, which is good science and stays good science. Price (1970) then partitioned selection into within-group and between-group components, so by 1970 both frameworks sat in one formula, before the argument started. Then the other rail: Tom Maschler tried to talk Dawkins out of the title in 1975, and The Selfish Gene’s own introduction says the word to stress is “gene,” that the book “devotes more attention to altruism,” and — in the next sentence — that “altruism might well be favoured at other levels”, which is multilevel selection in everything but name. Chapter one refuses the naturalistic fallacy outright: “I am not advocating a morality based on evolution.” And Dawkins later retracts his own most-quoted sentence, calling “born selfish” misleading and asking readers to delete it. The correction was printed in the same book as the error and it did not travel. Wilson & Wilson (2007) then revived group selection on a careful formulation, stated “the main exception to this rule” immediately after their famous line — and forty pages later compressed it into a one-foot summary, echoing Rabbi Hillel, that drops both the exception and the word single. That compressed version is the one our own Altruism glossary carries, second-hand, and we found it while writing the spread about the mechanism. Nowak, Tarnita & Wilson (2010) and a reply co-signed by more than a hundred biologists show a field still arguing; and for standard models kin selection and multilevel selection turn out to be the same arithmetic added up in a different order. The turn is tagged leap: account at one level only and every other level’s contribution becomes invisible — not disproved, never entered — which decides what repair is thinkable. Nine documented joints, three contested, one leap. Refuses being read as anti-evidence, as anti-Dawkins, and as “group selection won.”

Oblivious to Encounter
Stimpunks Zines

on what the plantation had to remove before it could scale, and the mushroom that has defeated seventy years of trying

Sameness is not what the world does when you leave it alone. It is the expensive thing, and somebody paid for it.

10 links→
Details

The chain that starts on a grass and ends on a specification. Sugarcane is propagated by pushing a cut piece into the ground, so every plant is the previous plant again — it can be multiplied forever without anything ever meeting anything, which is what Anna Tsing means by “comparatively self-contained, oblivious to encounter.” Portuguese Brazil assembled that cane with land emptied by conquest and with enslaved Africans taken far enough that there were “no established routes for escape” — and this chain withholds its own format on that link, visibly and on purpose, because what was done there is not a mechanism for illustrating an argument. Interchangeability was not discovered in people; it was produced in them, by force, because the mill needed it as an input. Sidney Mintz, read at pp. 47–48, documents Barbados holdings cut into equal ten-acre parcels harvested seriatim “assuring a steady flow of cane to the mill,” mills that “operated unceasingly,” a work week broken only from Saturday night to Monday — and part of the pace driven by a belief about boiling syrup he calls “somewhat misleading,” so the demand had already outrun the chemistry. His verdict is printed here with the hedge he put on it: “I believe it was probably the closest thing to industry that was typical of the seventeenth century.” Industry before the homeland — missed, he says, partly because scholars looking for the factory “began with the artisans and craftsmen of Europe.” Tsing names the resulting idea of progress scalability, the ability to change scale “without any change in project frames” — and states its price: it “banishes meaningful diversity, that is, diversity that might change things.” Then the rails cross. Matsutake has defeated seventy years and millions of yen; the 2020 Mycoscience review still reports cultivation “mostly unsuccessful,” and the only field successes used pine already colonised — so the only way to farm it is to already have the relationship and wait. It exists solely as an exchange with a tree, which is why it will not travel. And in Japan it was lost by abandonment: fossil fuel replaced firewood, the woodlands went shady, the pines died, and by the mid-1970s it was rare — a support removed by improvements aimed at something else. The turn is tagged leap: an accommodation is, by definition, a difference that requires the frame to change, which is exactly what a scalable system is specified to exclude. Nine documented joints, one contested, one leap. Refuses enslaved people as metaphor, ruin as an aesthetic, and “nature says be kind.”

Zine No. 79
Four Decimal Places
Stimpunks Zines · chain

A chain from atmospheric convection to a paperback subtitle — on the ratio at which a human life was said to begin going well, and the part-time student who read the primary source.

2.9013 was never a measurement of anybody. It was a property of the equations, and would have been the same number if every person studied had answered differently.

14 spreads · 8d 1c 1l→
Details

Edward Lorenz published Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow in 1963 — three equations for atmospheric convection whose solutions never repeat, and a real result checked exhaustively for sixty years. What travelled fastest afterwards was not the mathematics but the register: nonlinear dynamics acquired a rigorous-sounding authority outside physics, difficult for a non-specialist to check. In 1999 Marcial Losada fitted Lorenz-type equations to speech coded as positive or negative in business meetings — equations derived for fluid convection, applied to things people say at work, on the justification that both are complicated. Being complicated is not a shared mechanism. In 2005, in American Psychologist, Barbara Fredrickson and Losada joined broaden-and-build to that model and produced a number: people at or above a positive-to-negative ratio of 2.9013 flourish; below it they languish; above 11.6346 flourishing fails again. Four decimal places, on the ratio at which a human life begins to go well. The precision is doing specific work — a number given that precisely claims an instrument was involved. Fredrickson’s 2009 trade book then rounded it away on its own cover: the 3 to 1 ratio that will change your life. The four decimals bought credibility at the journal and were friction at the bookshop. Then a part-time student. Around 2012 Nick Brown, in his fifties, taking an applied positive psychology master’s at the University of East London with no mathematics past school, met the paper on his course and could not find where the data entered the equations. So he went and read Lorenz 1963. With the original in front of him it was visible: the equations had no connection to the emotion data, and would generate the same numbers whatever the participants had said. He wrote to Alan Sokal, of the 1996 Social Text hoax; with Harris Friedman they published in American Psychologist in July 2013. The nonlinear modelling and both ratios were withdrawn as invalid — Losada did not co-sign — while what survives remains contested, Carol Nickerson arguing in 2018 that there is no empirical evidence for critical positivity ratios at all. The chain judges the paper and not the people. Lorenz was right; borrowing across fields is how much good work happens; Fredrickson corrected her own paper, which is the system working, slowly and embarrassingly, which is the only way it works. The failure worth studying is not that somebody was wrong. It is that it took eight years and a stranger. The final joint is marked leap and argues that the precision was the persuasion, and the precision was the empty part — which matters most for people already being scored, since a ratio for flourishing is an instrument for finding those not flourishing correctly. Six refusals, including that this is not an argument against measurement (the method worked), not “do your own research” (Brown read the source the experts cited, which is the opposite move), and not a claim that anyone was a fraud. Spread two draws a boundary with our own shelf: Five Times a Crab borrows attractor from the same 1963 paper, openly and with the source named. Same paper, two borrowings, and the difference is whether you say which one you are doing. Fourteen spreads, ten joints — eight documented, one contested, one leap — and a generated spine. A closing spread that is deliberately not a link asks whether the number reached the classroom, where 4:1 and 5:1 praise-to-correction ratios are repeated throughout positive behaviour support. No citation from that literature to Fredrickson & Losada could be established, and the spread says so rather than inventing one. What is there is a genre with its own ancestry — Gottman’s empirical 5:1, Flora (2000) — and the field’s own verdict, from Sabey, Charlton & Charlton (2019): good face validity, and “a lack of empirical support for any specific ratio.” A chain can be cut at the retraction; a genre reproduces without a source. Positive greetings at the door is the worked example, and the tell is what it measured — academic engaged time and disruptive behaviour, not whether a child felt welcome. With Ehrenreich on positive thinking as an apology for the crueler aspects of the market economy, whose flip side is a harsh insistence on personal responsibility. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 84
There Have Been So Many Earths
Stimpunks Zines · chain

Nine links from Theia to Chicxulub, with the state of the land drawn at each one — and the finding that only three of them were moments.

Oxygenic photosynthesis was invented once and took something like a billion years to change the sky. Pangea was a hundred and forty-five million years of being a continent. The two events everybody can name are the two whose bars cannot be drawn.

15 spreads · 6d 2c 1l · scroll→
Details

The chain is chronological and the sections are as long as the things they describe. This is the collection’s second scroll zine, and the second time the form has been the argument: a section’s height is the duration it covers, so Chicxulub is one screen you fall past and the wait between oxygenic photosynthesis and the sky changing colour is three, most of it empty, with a rail down the side so the emptiness reads as elapsed time. Hank Green asked what the most important thing that ever happened was, and defined important usefully — how much depended on it, how easily it could have gone otherwise, and the sharper move underneath: importance is not a property of the universe, so the question is what had to happen for mattering to exist at all. With Cali Moore (PBS Eons) and Joe Hansen (Be Smart), the candidates were the origin of life, the eukaryotic cell, the Earth–Moon system, Chicxulub, the ribosome and oxygenic photosynthesis. Put them in order and something the list format hides comes out. Only three were moments, and all three are impacts — and even those are not three of a kind: Chicxulub is in the ground with a crater and an iridium layer, Theia is an inference from a moon that has never been found, and the rock that may have carried life is imagined, standing in for a transition nobody can describe. One documented, one inferred, one imagined. Everything else was a duration. Photosystem II learned to pull electrons out of water once, and the sky did not change for something like a billion years, because oxygen was consumed on contact by a planet with an enormous appetite; the invention and the consequence are two different events. Eukaryogenesis was, in Green’s own words, a bunch of things in succession, and the date is a neighbourhood rather than a date. Pangea was over a hundred million years of being a continent — roughly a C curled round the Tethys, with a desert down its equator so effective that one landmass still held two worlds — and its assembly dried out the wetlands where lycopsids grew a hundred feet tall, leaving only the ones that could shrink. Two corrections the source did not carry. Its citation for earliest life is Nutman et al. (2016) on 3.7 Ga Isua stromatolites; Allwood et al. (2018) went back to the same rocks and found deformation structures, some of the bumps pointing downward, which stromatolites do not do — the widely accepted floor is the younger 3.48 Ga Dresser Formation. And the famous thirty seconds later and it would have hit ocean is not a result: it traces to a 2017 BBC documentary, not to Kaiho & Oshima’s ~13% of Earth’s surface or Collins et al.’s 45–60° trajectory, and the piece refuses it rather than using it — a stopwatch is a different kind of claim from a probability across a planet. A globe on every link shows the land at that moment, dashed and faint before 1 Ga and reconstructed after it, because that is where Merdith et al. (2021) can hold a full-plate model together and a crisp coastline older than that is an invention wearing a map’s clothes. The art carries its own epistemics, the way the joint tags do. Nine links: six documented, two contested, one leap, plus a leap named inside Link 9 and declined. The turn is the demand for a moment — when did you know, when were you diagnosed, what happened to you — every version of which assumes an impact. Mostly there is no crater; mostly a difference met an environment with no room in it, over years, and the word arrived long after the thing it named. The diagnosis is not the event; it is the sinks finally filling. Ends on four models of the next supercontinent, drawn all four and chosen none, because Earth has never held an arrangement and is not holding this one. Endosymbiosis itself is No. 7’s argument and is pointed at rather than repeated. A rhyme, not a proof.
