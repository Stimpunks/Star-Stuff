Made for Paper

A Star Stuff collection · nine broadsides, and two that aren't

Everything else here is a web page that will print. These are sheets of paper that happen to have a web page.

Why paper at all

The rest of this collection is light on dark, and the shared stylesheet inverts it for print so that a zine handed to a printer comes back readable. That is a rescue operation, and it works — but it means every other page here was designed for a screen and then made to survive paper.

The broadsides run the other way. Each one is drawn as ink on white in both media, so what you see on this site is not the artifact — it is a preview of the artifact. The sheet is the thing. The web page is how you get one.

That inversion follows from what a broadside is for. A zine is read; a broadside is carried, handed over, and pinned up. It goes into a room where nobody is going to open a laptop — a kitchen table, a clinic waiting area, a staff room, a door. It gets photocopied badly and folded into a pocket. The historical form is the same one, and we are not being clever by borrowing it: a single cheap sheet, posted where people would see it. Proclamations, ballads, manifestos.

The sheets

Nine broadsides, in the order they were made — and two pieces that share the rack without being one. What each is for comes first, because on a broadside that is the load-bearing part.

Details

For the day you can't do what you did last week — the hand-out companion to Zine No. 20. Side A states the physics and then the claim: cool a glass of water and it does not slowly thicken toward solid; it stays liquid, liquid, liquid, and then at a single temperature flips all at once into ice. Same molecules, nothing added and nothing taken away. Capacity is a state, not a trait. The figure plots two lines against worsening conditions — a dashed diagonal for the gradual decline people expect, and the real curve, which holds and then drops. You are not inconsistent; you were near a boundary. Side B covers supercooling, critical slowing down, threshold anxiety, and what to ask for. Sized for A4 or US Letter.

A functioning-labels field card — the sheet you hand across the table when someone is about to say mild, high-functioning, or low support needs. The face draws the spiky profile, grid and baseline and all, and then five phrases with what each one actually does: high-functioning is heard as a compliment and used as a refusal; low support needs renames the rungs without removing the ladder; mild reports how much your difference costs the person describing it. Drawing the profile in order to refuse it is the point — you cannot refuse a picture you have not shown — and the reverse is where the grid gets dropped. There you get what to say instead, the spectroscopy fact (a spectrum is a fingerprint, not a scale), Helen Edgar's constellation of intensities, and the guardrail the whole sheet stands on: refusing the line is not refusing that some of us need far more support. Eighty stars, nothing joined, and a title with a gap in it: My ______ Galaxy. Field Guide No. 12 ends by asking you to draw your own constellations, and the site gave you nothing to draw on — this is that gap, closed. Every other sheet in the print rack compresses an argument made at length somewhere else; this one compresses nothing, because it is a surface. The face is the field, with a rule where Helen Edgar's title reads Monotropic, because that is her word for her own experience and a blank sheet must not assume it of whoever picks it up. The reverse is a blank Constellation Index of ten rows — ten rather than twenty, since twenty is her number and printing hers would quietly turn a personal map into a quota. The stars are SVG circles rather than the site's usual CSS starfield, which matters more here than anywhere else on this site: browsers leave background graphics off by default, so a gradient field would have printed as an empty white box, and a blank sheet with no stars on it is not a blank sheet, it is a mistake. Verified by printing it — two sides on A4 and US Letter, with the dots landing on paper. There is no wrong way to fill it in, and no score at the end.

What earns a sheet

Those nine are the whole rack, and it stays small on purpose. Four rules decide what joins it, applied in order — they exist because “this would make a good broadside” is an easy thing to say about almost anything, and most of the time it is wrong. A proposal that fails rule three is not a weaker broadside; it is a zine, and should be built as one.

  1. It compresses something already argued at length on the site, and links back. A broadside invents no new claim. Every line on it is a compression of text that exists in full somewhere else, with its sources attached, so the sheet inherits that piece's fact-checking rather than needing its own. If a draft starts making an argument its parent never made, it has stopped being a compression and become a new piece — which needs its own primary sources and its own ledger row.
  2. Side A is a face you'd pin up; side B does the work. The face carries one thing, large enough to read from across a room. The reverse is where the habits, the receipts, the full text and the sources go. This division is what separates a broadside from a poster: a poster is finished when you have looked at it, and a broadside has a second half that expects to be read.
  3. You can name the moment someone reaches for it, away from a screen. This is the rule that does the actual filtering. Six Ways is consulted when a claim arrives and something about it feels too neat. L★S is handed to someone you love. The Same Water is for the day you cannot do what you did last week. If you cannot finish the sentence “someone picks this up when…”, you do not have a broadside — you have a summary, and summaries belong on the page they summarise.
  4. Ink on white in both media, 190 × 259 mm, two sides, no third page, two spot inks that clear AA. The physical specification. It is not negotiable per-sheet, because the point of a fixed format is that nine different arguments come off the printer as the same object — a rack, not a pile.

The fourth rule is the physical one, and every number in it was arrived at by printing something and finding out it was wrong — the paper size, the margin that tipped a sheet onto a third page, the inks, and the three gates that measure it. That working lives on its own page: Designing for Paper →

Not

Not a poster. A poster is finished when you have looked at it. Every sheet here has a reverse, and the reverse is where the sources are.

Not merch. Open edition, CC BY-SA 4.0. No numbered prints, no scarcity, nothing to buy — print as many as you want and give them away. If a sheet works, we would rather it circulate without us.

Not a summary standing in for the argument. A broadside compresses a piece that exists in full, and points at it. It is not the short version for people who won't read the long one.

Not screen-first with a print stylesheet bolted on. That approach is exactly how 44 pages came to print blank. Paper is the target, not the fallback.

The one that was held, and why that is worth recording

Until 15 August 2026 this section read What is not here yet, and the thing it named was the functioning-labels field card — the sheet you hand across the table when someone is about to say “mild,” “high-functioning,” or “low support needs.” It had the clearest moment of anything on the list, which by rule three should have made it the strongest candidate in the rack. It was held anyway, and not for want of an argument to compress.

It was held because Zine No. 21 is after Helen Edgar, and constellation of intensities is her phrase. A broadside circulates harder than a zine — it goes on walls, into meetings, into photocopiers — so who gets to move a phrase into that kind of circulation is a decision to ask about rather than assume.

The sheet now exists, and it exists in the order the hold was for: built first, then shown to Helen as a PDF of the actual artifact rather than described, then shipped on her word. She reviewed it and approved it on 15 August 2026. Describing a sheet is not the same as showing someone the thing that will end up on the wall, which is why the asking came after the building rather than instead of it.

The note stays rather than being quietly deleted, because a hold that vanishes on the day it lifts teaches nobody anything. What it records is that the delay was the correct cost, and that the same question gets asked again next time.
