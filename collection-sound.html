A Star Stuff collection · seven racks, 229 cards

Every other collection here is something we made. These are racks pointing at other people's work — the writing is only the way in.

The odd axis, again

Five of the collections on this site sort zines by register — what kind of argument a piece is making. Print broke that pattern by sorting on medium instead, and said so on its face rather than letting it read as an oversight. Sound is the second one on that axis, so the same disclosure applies: this is a shelf, not a claim about kinds of argument. What the seven racks have in common is that you listen to them.

But there is a sharper difference, and it is the thing worth saying here. Everything else on this site is ours — we wrote the zines, drew the diagrams, checked the claims, and if a page is wrong it is wrong in our voice. A rack is not ours. The songs belong to the people who made them; what we contribute is selection, arrangement, and a reason per track. That is a real contribution and also a modest one, and it changes what the work has to be careful about.

Mostly it changes this: the recording is the destination and the writing is the doorway. A card that is more interesting than its song has failed. If you read a note and do not press play, we have wasted both our time.

The racks

Seven, in the order they were made, and they are deliberately different shapes — two themes, one scene, and four single artists who are nothing like each other. That variety was not planned; it turned up, and then it turned out to be the useful thing about the shelf.

The count is 229 cards, not 229 songs: a few pieces appear twice in different rooms, deliberately — Conqueror at a Red Bull session and Conqueror on late-night television are not the same event, and five Bowie songs sit in both the L★ rack and the Bowie rack because each rack wanted them for a different reason. The Dolly rack widened that gap on purpose: its closing section is nineteen covers, so “Jolene” alone holds eight cards and “I Will Always Love You” five — which is the argument of that rack rather than an accident of counting. Counted by distinct title the whole collection is 181, and we would rather print both numbers than round the awkward one away.

36 + essay→
Details

The loud companion to the L★S Playlist, and the one that argues first. In July 1977 Bob Marley recorded Punky Reggae Party with Lee Perry after hearing The Clash cover a Jamaican record — a guest list for an alliance that did not exist until it was read out, and the following April roughly 100,000 people marched six miles through National Front territory to hear The Clash, Steel Pulse and X-Ray Spex on one Rock Against Racism bill. Naming a thing helped it happen, which is what neurodiversity did too. Then the physics, because the physics is unreasonably good: sound needs matter — in a vacuum there is none at all — so every note anyone has heard was carried by nitrogen and oxygen forged in stars. A pure sine wave has a pitch and almost no identity; Helmholtz worked out in 1863 that what tells you who is playing lives entirely in the overtones above the note. And distortion, the sound the genre is named by, is a nonlinearity adding harmonics that were never sent in — difference, deliberately added. It reaches you at the skeleton, too: you hear your own voice partly through skull bone, which is why recordings sound wrong. Thirty-six songs from the Stimpunks PSA — Bikini Kill, Dream Nails, Kae Tempest, Le Tigre, The Linda Lindas, Gossip, pink suits — with the door test punk actually contributed, and the honest part: a loud room is itself an access barrier, and acceptance is not any scene's nature but a practice re-won in every room.

Stimming and Belonging with AURORA
Sound · 28 performances + essay

The shape: one body of work, and one body.

Weighted toward live performances, because the argument is the body.

The companion rack to Warriors and Weirdos (No. 13). It opens on AURORA's own account of seeing herself on video for the first time.

28 + essay→
Details

The companion rack to Warriors and Weirdos (No. 13), weighted toward live performances because the argument is the body. It opens on AURORA's own account of seeing herself on video for the first time — she was shocked, said she couldn't do anything about it, and kept it: I have to use everything I got. Then the physics of the light she shares a name with, which turns out to be about exactly this. The solar wind delivers energy and nothing else; the colour is the atom's own — that famous green is oxygen's 557.7 nm line. And that line is a forbidden transition: it breaks the electric-dipole selection rules, so instead of the nanoseconds an allowed transition takes, the atom waits about 0.7 seconds before letting go, and the red line waits 110. Which is why altitude decides the colour — lower down the air is crowded and a collision takes the energy first. The most famous light in the sky is a slow release the rules call forbidden, and it only completes where nothing crowds it. Twenty-eight performances across six movements, plus stim-listening (Jim Irion's coinage) and the reason Ryan's own playlist is nearly all live footage. The page is careful about whose word is whose: she calls it emotion; we are the ones calling it stimming, and it says so rather than putting the term in her mouth. The sharpest refusal is saved for last — her movement is celebrated because it is on a stage and lovely, while the same body doing the same thing in a classroom gets a behaviour plan.

Rack · One artist
Nobody Got His Eyes Right
Sound · 31 tracks + essay

The shape: one career, chronologically, and two corrections.

The most-looked-at face in pop, and the one fact everybody repeats about it.

Thirty-one songs from Space Oddity to I Can't Give Everything Away — the listening companion to Turn and Face the Strange (No. 12).

31 + essay→
Details

The long version of the five Bowie cards that open the L★S Playlist, and the listening companion to Turn and Face the Strange (No. 12) — thirty-one songs from Space Oddity to I Can't Give Everything Away. It starts from the most-looked-at face in pop and the one fact everybody repeats about it, which is wrong. He did not have different-coloured eyes. Both irises were the same colour; in February 1962 a schoolfriend punched him and a fingernail paralysed the muscles that contract the left iris, so that pupil could never close again — anisocoria, not heterochromia. A pupil stuck wide is a hole full of shadow, which is why the eye reads darker in exactly the bright light where a working pupil contracts, which is to say under every stage light and camera flash he ever stood in. That changes what kind of thing the famous feature was: heterochromia is a variation you're born with; this was an injury, reportedly with lasting effects on his depth perception. Glamour is not an accommodation. Then the second thing everyone knows — the constellation named for him in 2016 is not a constellation; there are 88, the list was fixed in the 1920s, and the observatory that made it said plainly it was an invented asterism. Both errors lean the same way: they round a damaged iris and a small tribute up into something more elegant, and the tidier version travels further. Which is Too Good to Check with a soundtrack — and it has a cost, because while the myth is that beautiful nobody asks what a fixed pupil is like under a follow spot.

Rack · One artist
One Body at a Time
Sound · 25 tracks + essay

The shape: one career, chronologically, and one method repeated at three scales.

Repetition is the mechanism, not the deficit — and the room fills up around you.

Twenty-five songs from Psycho Killer to Everybody Laughs — the listening companion to The Body Understands It First (No. 66).

25 + essay→
Details

David Byrne from the 1977 CBGB audition to Who Is the Sky? in 2025, and the listening companion to The Body Understands It First (No. 66). The essay runs on one settled finding: groove is measurable and agreed upon across listeners (Janata, Tomic & Haberman, 2012), and the feature doing the work is repetition — a figure has to come round again before a body can meet it rather than chase it. Which is how Remain in Light was built: instruments recorded largely one at a time, each part a short figure held without developing, credited openly to Fela Kuti and West African polyrhythm. Then the same method twice more. Byrne wrote out early Talking Heads as a performance style defined by negatives — no solos, no poses, no patter, and an instruction to club lighting people to turn the lights on at the start and off at the end — which is subtraction as an access strategy, whether or not anyone called it that. And Stop Making Sense adds one body at a time until the stage is full and the man who could not relax is dancing; nobody fixed him, the room filled up. The synchrony research behind that is real and, per a 2017 meta-analysis, modest, and the page says so rather than letting a good sentence do a mechanism's work. On his own account of being Autistic the rack is careful: it is self-described and hedged in the original, and we do not upgrade it.

Love Without Cringing
Sound · 43 tracks + essay

The shape: one catalogue in order, with the authorship checked at every card.

The persona was assigned before she chose it, and the copyright stayed in her pocket.

Forty-three tracks — twenty-four of hers, then nineteen covers — the listening companion to No One Had to Qualify (No. 67).

43 + essay→
Details

Dolly Parton 1967–2023, made three days after she died. The essay opens on a fact worth checking before repeating: her first hit, “Dumb Blonde,” was written by Curly Putman — the persona arrived before she had any say in it, and the self-invention story is really about what she did once she was inside it. Then the 1974 refusal, told as she tells it: Colonel Tom Parker wanted half the publishing for Elvis, she said no and cried all night, and Whitney Houston settled the argument eighteen years later. A third section pulls the catalogue’s dark run forward — “Down from Dover,” “The Bridge,” a stillbirth and a suicide — because the woman who built the Imagination Library was never naive about what happens to people; Tressie McMillan Cottom’s reading of that run as gothic motherhood is borrowed with the tag on, as is her argument about blonde as unmarked whiteness. The last section is about what travels: “9 to 5” sells productivity software now, and in 2023 a Wisconsin school barred first-graders from singing “Rainbowland” under a policy on controversial issues — a song about there being no gate, stopped at one. Companion zine: No One Had to Qualify.

Rack · One theme
Skepticism and Wonder
Playlist & essay

The Rainbow Connection — forty songs, and the two uneasily cohabiting modes of thought they carry.

A song told a six-year-old that rainbows have nothing to hide, and he took it as permission to love the real thing more than any story about what it was concealing.

40 songs→
Details

“Rainbow Connection” — Paul Williams and Kenneth Ascher, sung by Jim Henson as Kermit, opening The Muppet Movie in 1979 — grants that rainbows are visions, downgrades them to illusions, and then lands the line that did the work for a science-loving kid: rainbows have nothing to hide. No pot of gold, no other side, nothing withheld. As a closeted queer and atheist child, Ryan found that observational reality better than any story about what a rainbow might be concealing — and the essay is honest that the song gets one word wrong. A rainbow is not an illusion. An illusion is a mistake perception makes; a rainbow is occurring, calculable to a fraction of a degree, and Roger Bacon measured its maximum elevation at 42° around 1267 with an astrolabe and no theory of refraction. What is true is stranger and better: it has no location — a direction rather than a place, centred on the shadow of your own head. Carl Sagan later supplied the vocabulary in The Demon-Haunted World (1995): his parents taught him “the two uneasily cohabiting modes of thought that are central to the scientific method.” Uneasily is the load-bearing word — not reconciled, sharing a house. NeuroTribes’ chapter “Princes of the Air” supplies the lineage, with Asperger forced to walk back his claim that his patients’ interests were remote from real concerns because their imaginations kept anticipating science by decades; and Dorion Sagan — son of Carl Sagan and Lynn Margulis, whose endosymbiosis this collection already runs on — calls detail and breadth “a kind of alembic, an antidote to both.” Then the fact that closes it. In March 2023 the School District of Waukesha, Wisconsin pulled “Rainbowland” from a first-grade concert as potentially controversial — and the song chosen to replace it was “Rainbow Connection,” which was initially banned too, reinstated only after parents pushed back; the teacher who spoke publicly was later terminated. A song whose entire argument is that rainbows have nothing hidden in them was, for a moment, too controversial for six-year-olds. Seven sections: eleven versions of the song; the Garfunkel and Oates joke that reaches the same thesis; eleven of “Over the Rainbow,” which wants the opposite thing; four of “What a Wonderful World,” which wants this one; the colours named as a list and as physics; the rainbow turned inward as identity; and finally Merle Haggard’s sour “Rainbow Stew” against a man on a mountain undone by a double rainbow, the two modes back to back with no attempt to reconcile them. All forty video ids were resolved through oEmbed before anything was written about them — five carried no artist in their link text, and four of those we would have got wrong. No lyric is reproduced anywhere on the page, which is stated rather than worked around.

What earns a rack

Three is few enough that the rules could be tacit, which is exactly when they should be written down instead. These are applied in order, and the first one does most of the filtering.

  1. The music has to be doing the arguing. Not illustrating an argument the prose already finished. If the songs could be swapped for different songs without changing what the page says, this is a zine with a soundtrack and should be built as a zine. The test is whether removing the audio removes the point.
  2. Every track carries a written reason. No vibes-only entries, no filler to reach a round number. Each card says why this recording is on this rack — which is also the honest limit on how long a rack can get, because a hundred and twenty-one reasons is already a lot of reasons and twice that would be padding.
  3. Every embed is resolved before it ships. A card whose embed quietly points at a different recording is invisible to every gate we run: the markup is valid, the contrast is fine, the words are all there. So each video ID goes through the oEmbed endpoint and comes back with its real title and channel before the page is published. 95 of them so far, and that pass has already produced two corrections — an unattributed song turning out to be Le Butcherettes, and a release year that would have shipped wrong from memory.
  4. Describe the song; don't reproduce it. The words belong to the people who wrote them, and a lyric sheet is not what a rack is for. Cards describe, characterise and argue; the recording is where you meet the song. This also keeps the writing honest — it is much harder to fake a reason than to quote a good line.

Rule four arrived with the second rack, and the first one predates it: seven cards on the L★S Playlist quote short lyric fragments. They are brief, attributed and long-standing, and we are naming the inconsistency here rather than quietly conforming the older page or pretending the rule was always in force. If it changes, it will change in the changelog.

Two pages, one piece of physics

This was not planned and is the best thing about the shelf, so it gets its own heading.

The punk rack went looking for what makes an amplifier sound like punk and arrived at overtones: a pure sine wave has a pitch and almost no identity, and what tells you who is playing lives entirely in the harmonics above the note. Distortion is a nonlinearity that adds harmonics which were never in the signal — character as surplus.

The AURORA rack went looking for the physics of the northern lights and arrived at emission lines: the solar wind delivers energy and nothing else, and the famous green is atomic oxygen's own 557.7 nm line. Energy from outside; colour from within.

Those are the same fact wearing different clothes. What comes out is characteristic of the material, whatever put the energy in. Neither page set out to say that, and neither knew the other was going to; the convergence was noticed afterward, reading them side by side — which is the whole method described on Cosmic Connections, arriving without being asked. It is also, as both pages say at length, a rhyme rather than a proof. Spectroscopy has no opinion about anybody's belonging.

Worth adding that the third essay did not join in. The Bowie rack went looking for its own fact and came back with optics and an anatomy correction instead — a paralysed iris, and a constellation that is not one. Three essays, two convergences, one page that went somewhere else entirely. We are recording that rather than stretching the pattern to fit it, because a pattern you have to push into shape is not one you observed.

The gap

A collection built out of audio and video has an access problem, and we have not solved it. Everything else on this site is text: it reflows, it takes a screen reader, it prints, it can be read at whatever pace you need. The shelf is 146 embedded videos.

What exists: every card carries a written note that stands on its own, so the argument of each rack survives without playing anything, and the essays are text. What does not exist: transcripts, and any guarantee of caption quality. Captions on the embeds are whatever the uploader provided or whatever the platform generated, and auto-generated captions on sung material are frequently poor. We do not control them and we have not audited them.

Naming it is not fixing it. It is here because a site that argues for access every week should say plainly where its own work falls short, and because the next person to build a rack should know this is the outstanding job.

Not

Not a mood board. A rack is an argument you can play. If the only thing holding a set of songs together is that they feel similar, that is a playlist and it belongs on a streaming service, where it will be better recommended than we could manage.

Not ours to own. We select and we write; the music is the artists' work, and we link back to it rather than hosting it. The CC BY-SA on this site covers our writing, not their recordings.

Not a lyric sheet. See rule four. Cards describe songs; they do not reprint them.

Not artists diagnosed from a distance. Where a musician has spoken about being neurodivergent we use their word and no other, and where we recognise something they have not named, we say the recognition is ours. This is stated in full on the AURORA rack, which is where the temptation is strongest.

Not a soundtrack for the reader's suffering. These racks are not sad-playlist infrastructure. Several of the songs are about being unwell and several are about joy, and neither kind is here to be a mood you are supposed to arrive in.

What is not here yet

This section named two candidates when the shelf held three racks, and one of them has since been built — the Bowie rack above was on this list as not started before it existed. We are leaving the trace rather than editing it out, because a to-do list you quietly rewrite once it is done stops being evidence of anything.

What remains: a rack for the happy-flappy and stim-dancing material, which is currently distributed across stimpunks.org rather than collected. It would need to clear rule one on its own terms — the argument there is about bodies moving for pleasure, and it is not obvious yet whether the music carries that or merely accompanies it. Not started.

Named here rather than kept on a private list, same as everywhere else on this site. The list of things we have not done is part of the record.
