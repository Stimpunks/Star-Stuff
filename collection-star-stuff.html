Star Stuff

A Star Stuff collection · 27 pieces

The founding register: one settled, checkable fact, followed honestly, already contains the claim about belonging.

What holds these together

Every piece here works the same way. Take one fact that is settled and checkable, follow it honestly, and find that the fact itself already contains the claim about belonging. The calcium in your bones really was forged in stars. Mitochondria really were free-living bacteria that never left. The early universe really did need tiny unevennesses to make anything at all — a perfectly uniform cosmos would have stayed a dead fog forever.

That is the difference between physics as argument and physics as decoration. In this collection the fact is the argument, not an illustration of a point already decided somewhere else. If the science turned out otherwise, the piece would have to change. That is the test, and it is the reason this register earns the trust it asks for.

Sorted by register, not by date

The first ten zines founded this method, and seven of them are here. But nineteen of these pieces were made much later — The Nearest Body, No Universal Now, The Same Water and Not a Line among them — and still belong here, because they do exactly the same thing. Tidal force really does fall off with the cube of distance, which is why the Moon runs the tide even though the Sun out-masses it some 27 million times. Clocks really do run at different rates by speed and gravity, which is why your phone's GPS has to correct for it. Water really does hold and then flip at a threshold rather than stiffening by degrees.

Membership here is decided by mechanism, not chronology. A piece is in this collection if the fact is doing the work. The numbers still say when each one was made — see Cosmic Connections on why we kept them that way.

Not

Not the naturalistic fallacy. The atoms do not owe us this conclusion. We are noticing a rhyme, not collecting a proof, and The Lines We Drew is in this collection precisely to name the trap: the bell curve was recruited to make “normal” look like physics, and eugenics followed. Dressing a human value as a law of nature is the exact move we refuse.

Not interdisciplinary as tourism — borrowing a physics word for its glamour and leaving the physics behind.

Zine No. 24

9 spreads→
Details

The air we share, read through star stuff and solarpunk — a companion to Bodies of Water. There is only one air: a single, well-mixed body with no walls in it, so thoroughly circulated that your next breath very likely holds atoms once breathed by strangers and by trees across the world. It's a reciprocal breath — we exhale what plants inhale, and our oxygen is the exhaust of ancient cyanobacteria (the Great Oxidation Event, ~2.4 billion years ago), forged first in stars. Every room we share is a shared-air space (a term coined by Helen Edgar & Ryan Boren): in it we trade breath and co-regulate, nervous systems settling into a shared rhythm. The neurodiversity turn: many of us are the canaries of that room. Miners carried canaries because the birds felt the bad air first — not because they were weak, but because they were sensitive, and their alarm was information. Our sensitivity to fragrance, stale air, and fluorescent hum is an early-warning system, not a flaw: don't blame the bird, change the air — build it breathable for the canaries and it's breathable for all. Which raises the harder question: habitable, and for whom? The burden of shared air falls unevenly, so breathable air is a commons, not a private accommodation one person quietly manages. The response is solarpunk: clean air as infrastructure as resistance — open windows, plants, masks, DIY Corsi–Rosenthal boxes — mutual aid you can build in an afternoon. Move quietly and plant things. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 44
You Cannot Flatten a Sphere
Stimpunks Zines

on curvature, map projections, and what a single number has to throw away

Gauss proved in 1827 that no flat map can preserve everything. An IQ score is literally a projection — so it must destroy information.

12 spreads→
Details

Try wrapping a sheet of paper smoothly around an orange. It creases, always, and that is not clumsiness — it is forbidden. Gauss proved it in 1827 and liked the result enough to call it the Theorema Egregium, the remarkable theorem: a surface’s curvature is intrinsic, so bending that doesn’t stretch can never change it. Paper rolls into a tube or a cone because those are still flat in the sense that matters; a sphere is not, and no care gets you there. You have used this today without noticing — fold a pizza slice and the tip stops drooping, because curving it one way forces it to stay straight the other. The consequence: no flat map of the world can preserve everything, and every projection must sacrifice area, angle, distance or direction. Mercator chose angle, in 1569, so a constant compass bearing would be a straight line — superb for sailors, and it swells Greenland to the apparent size of an Africa roughly fourteen times larger. The map never says what it chose, and that silence is the scandal rather than the distortion. Then Tissot’s answer from 1859, which is the honest one: you cannot remove the distortion, so draw it — a grid of ellipses showing exactly what this map did to this place. And then the turn. An IQ score, a DSM-5 support level, a functioning label: each takes a many-axis person and returns one value, which is not a metaphor but literally a projection — so it must destroy information, the choice of what to destroy is somebody’s, and the number never records it. Worse than a map, in fact: a Mercator keeps every place badly, while a single number doesn’t distort the other dimensions, it deletes them. Ask what a summary was optimised for and you learn whose journey it serves — Mercator for sailors, a support level for a budget, a functioning label for a gate. If you must summarise, publish the indicatrix: the axes it came from, the range it moves across, what it was built for, reviewed by the person measured. Closes on the gentlest thing the theorem gives — a globe has no edges and no corners, so being at the margin was never information about you, only about where somebody cut. Companion to No. 21 Not a Line, which asks what shape the thing is; this asks what a reduction costs. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 45
The Cloud Phase
Stimpunks Zines

on the last stage of a supernova remnant, and how long the in-between actually lasts

The story we get told about a dead star ends about one step past the explosion. The physics runs further than the sentence does.

11 spreads→
Details

The story we get told about a dead star ends about one step past the explosion: the shell expands, thins, cools, and disperses into the interstellar medium. Fade out. But the physics runs further than the sentence does, and there is another word waiting at the end of it. Romano, Behrendt & Burkert (2024) followed remnants through their long cold decline and found the radiative phase divides into four stages — and the last one they call the cloud phase. Not the end phase. Not the dispersal phase. The cloud phase: once nothing is driving the shell and the pressure inside has fallen to match the pressure outside, nothing holds the cavity open, so the remnant falls back into itself and settles as a chemically enriched cloud. That is a 2024 simulation and it is held here as proposed, flagged in place — because the argument's weight sits somewhere firmer. Murray (2011) measured what clouds actually do: in the Milky Way's most luminous star-forming complexes, the star formation efficiency averages 0.08, so ninety-two percent of the cloud never becomes a star — and that is the flattering number, since the Galactic average of 0.005 puts it nearer ninety-nine and a half. A giant molecular cloud lasts 27 ± 12 million years, and the bright episode inside it is short, late, and the thing that tears the cloud apart. Which turns the story the other way round: the in-between is not the wait between the real states. It is the majority state, by mass and by duration — and that is a measurement, offered as fact rather than as comfort. Along the way, SN 1987A: at least 0.2 solar masses of dust condensed inside its own wreckage, grains that did not exist anywhere before the star died. The debris field is a foundry. Written after Helen Edgar, whose Supernova Remnant and Emergence Point map the same stage from the inside — burnout as “remnants of my old self entangled in new ways of being,” recovery as re-assembly rather than return, and deterritorialisation as “a soft loosening of pressure.” A cloud never decides to collapse; it is collapsed upon, by gravity and radiation and its neighbours, so the honest question when one of us reorganises is what the pressure is and where it comes from. Held with care throughout: gestation is not a promise of a star, most of the mass never lights, and the claim is only that the cloud is a real state, not a waiting room. Closes on aluminium-26 in the Allende meteorite — something nearby and freshly dead was in our own birth cloud, and which star is openly contested fifty years on, so we name the contest instead of picking a winner. Eleven spreads, nine diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 63
Even Fallen Light Glows
Stimpunks Zines · after Helen Edgar

On Neruda’s well, a rewrite made in burnout, six words that answered it — and the fact that fallen wood glows in the dark for no reason anyone has been able to establish.

Every known bioluminescent fungus is a white-rot decomposer, so the thing that glows is the thing that eats fallen wood. After 160 million years, nobody can say what the glow is for.

12 spreads→
Details

Three texts and one fungus. Neruda’s untitled poem in El mar y las campanas (1973) says that if each day falls inside each night there is a well “where clarity is imprisoned,” and instructs you to sit on the rim of the well of darkness and fish for fallen light with patience (William O’Daly’s translation, The Sea and the Bells, Copper Canyon, 1988, pp. 94–95 — read at the printed page, because every online copy is an aggregator). Read the grammar and the poem has quietly handed out two roles: the one who fishes, and the light that fell. Ryan Boren’s rewrite, made in deep Autistic burnout, moves the speaker into the well — the rim is distant, no casting can reach, patience broke — and the discovery inside it is structural rather than emotional: sitting on a rim with patience is work you can do, being the light at the bottom is not work at all, and once you are the light every instruction in the poem is addressed to somebody else. Helen Edgar answered in six words: even fallen light glows. What makes the reply hold is what it refuses — it does not say the rim is closer, that patience returns, or that you will be reached. It is a claim about the light, not about the rescue, and it pries apart two things the poem had welded together. Then the fact. Foxfire. Perry, Desjardin & Stevani (J. Fungi 11(1):19, 2024) recognise 132 taxa across five lineages — Mycenaceae 96, Omphalotaceae 18, Physalacriaceae 14, Lucentipes 3, Cyphellopsidaceae 1 — and every one is an agaric saprotrophic white-rot decomposer, or rarely a plant pathogen. There is no glowing fungus that lives some other way, which makes the thing that glows is the thing that eats fallen wood a statement of taxonomy rather than a figure of speech. The glow is built out of the log: the caffeic acid cycle runs caffeic acid to hispidin to 3-hydroxyhispidin, the luciferin, which luciferase oxidises with oxygen to caffeylpyruvate and a photon near 530 nm — and then caffeylpyruvate hydrolase turns the spent molecule back into caffeic acid. The end of the reaction is the beginning of the reaction. And in the Armillaria lineage the part that glows is the mycelium and rhizomorphs, threads inside the wood, while the mushrooms are consistently non-luminescent: the showy reproductive part is the dark one. Emission runs continuously, on a temperature-compensated circadian clock (Oliveira et al., Curr. Biol. 25:964–968, 2015), so nothing about it is conditional on a receiver — it does not brighten for company and does not stop when the forest is empty, which is nearly always. The honest centre is spread nine: nobody knows what it is for. Lit mushroom models draw more beetles, flies, bugs and wasps than unlit ones, which is real evidence for spore dispersal — and it explains a cap, not the mycelium and lower stipe, which make no spores at all. A live rival says the luciferin is an antioxidant and the light is a byproduct of handling reactive oxygen while dismantling lignin. The review states outright that a non-adaptive, accidental origin cannot yet be excluded. Spread ten is the guardrail: bioluminescence arose once, about 160 mya, and has been lost or inactivated in lineage after lineage since — so keeping the glow is not an achievement and losing it is the commonest thing that ever happened to it. A zine that says even fallen light glows is one sentence away from so you had better be glowing, and the phylogeny is what blocks it. The turn: if you are the fallen light, glowing is not a task you are behind on, and the casting is somebody else’s work — which is where solidarity stops being a warm feeling and becomes a division of labour. Helen’s constellation is what sits at the rim. Refusals on the face: not that the log is glad it fell; not that burnout is secretly productive, because decay is not a chrysalis and burnout is damage somebody’s conditions caused; not that you must glow to be worth keeping; not that the glow is a signal so try harder to be seen; not a promise that you will be reached; not nature proving anything about a nervous system; and not the rewrite corrected — both poems are on the page, unedited, in order. The consolation is deliberately thin, because a thicker one would be a lie to somebody whose patience has broken. Twelve spreads, one diagram. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 46
Companion Stars
Stimpunks Zines

on gravitational binding, the three-body problem, and why no one's path can be solved alone

A star that shares a system with another star already has a name, and it is astronomy's own working word: a companion star.

9 spreads→
Details

A star that shares a system with another star already has a name, and it is astronomy’s own working word: a companion star. Helen Edgar’s thirteenth constellation carries it — “the people who orbit close … who don’t need me to perform and who just ‘get it’.” But before any of that, a correction, because this collection exists partly to take science back from the version that travels furthest. You have met the line most stars are in binaries — the Sun is the odd one out. It isn’t so. Charles Lada put the binary statistics beside the stellar mass function in 2006 and published the answer as his title: most stars are single — two-thirds of main-sequence systems in the Galactic disk. Multiplicity climbs steeply with mass (~26% of M dwarfs, 44 ± 2% of Sun-like stars, near-total for the most massive), and the galaxy is overwhelmingly made of the small end. So this zine will not tell you that everybody has their people. The claim it makes instead is smaller and holds all the way down: where the binding is real, here is exactly what it is. Sirius A and its white-dwarf companion trace a 50.13-year orbit about a barycentre lying in neither star — and equal binding is not equal motion, since the heavier body is displaced less, which is worth saying out loud for anyone who has been told they’re imagining who does more of the moving. Then the hardest spread: bring two stars close and they do deform each other — stretched to ellipsoids, drawn to teardrops, finally sharing one dumbbell envelope with matter crossing between them. Proximity is not automatically good. It is the wide pairs — bound out to a parsec, one orbit longer than the whole history of flowering plants — where both keep their shape. And then the turn, which is theorems rather than reading: with two bodies you can write the solution down, but Bruns (1887) proved no further algebraic integrals exist, Poincaré (1890) closed off the single-valued analytic ones, and Sundman’s 1912 series converges far too slowly to use. So these systems are stepped forward numerically, together, and no one body’s path can be solved on its own and the others added afterward. That same work is where chaos was found — and the part the popular word destroys is that nothing is random: every rule is exact and known, and the system is still unpredictable far out. Read beside the language this community built first — David Gray-Hammond’s Chaotic Self (“you can’t unqueer a queer mind”) and Autistic Rhizome, with Helen’s Rhizome Array and Mycelial Stars — and holding the tension honestly rather than smoothing it: the rhizome describes resilience, no node depending on another to exist; the three-body physics describes non-separability while bodies are bound. Both true, neither cancelling. Closes on what actually holds a bound system: no agreement, no shared origin, no similarity, no upkeep, no performance — mutual gravity and nothing else, which means there is no version of a companion star that has to earn it. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 48
The Shadow Is Bigger Than the Thing
Stimpunks Zines

on the black hole nobody photographed, and what can only be known by its effect on everything else

Nobody has photographed a black hole, and nobody will — the ordinary way of knowing a thing is simply unavailable, and no better lens fixes it.

12 spreads→
Details

Nobody has photographed a black hole, and nobody will. A camera works by catching light that left the subject, and the event horizon is the surface where every outward direction has stopped existing — so the ordinary way of knowing a thing is simply unavailable, and no better lens fixes it. What the Event Horizon Telescope released in April 2019 and May 2022 is a shadow: the set of directions in which light that might have reached you went into the hole instead, ringed by the light that passed far enough out to be bent around and escape. The ruler that would not travel one clade sideways gets less latitude than the one we hand a five-year-old for life — and there is no Case 3700, nowhere to file, no numbered Opinion. The paperwork is not always kind either, and spread eight says so: Oviraptor means egg seizer, named in 1924 for a clutch it was brooding, corrected by Norell and colleagues in 1994 and 1995, and stuck with the libel forever under nomenclatural priority. Two ways a label outlives its refutation — priority, a rule written down, and repetition, which needs no rule at all. Only one admits it. Then the turn: Velociraptor was feathered, on the evidence of quill knobs on the ulna, and it is smaller and stranger than the movie and closer to a bad-tempered rooster, because birds are dinosaurs. Not one of these corrections made an animal less interesting. Every one made it stranger. The animals were never wrong about themselves; we were wrong about them, and being wrong out loud is what let them get better. And 298 pages is not what growing out of it looks like — it is what it looks like when nobody does. What get serious asks for is never depth, which monotropic attention already has; it is convertibility. Not that we stop being absorbed, but that we stop being absorbed for free. Bettin’s inversion holds: retained neoteny is not developmental lag but a distributed adaptive capacity that keeps a group from setting into whatever the highest-ranking person already believes. The close earns the collection. Apatosaurus louisae is estimated at about 41 tonnes of calcium phosphate, and calcium and phosphorus are made in stars — so a sauropod walked the Late Jurassic on a skeleton of dead stars, every atom second-hand and every atom still in circulation. Bone is piezoelectric, as Yasuda showed, so those animals were writing their own skeletons by walking on them; that this was true of sauropod bone is marked as an inference, not a measurement. And Sander and sixteen co-authors still report “no direct evidence on sauropod food and food processing” — the biggest animals ever to walk, and we do not know how they ate, which never made them less real. Refusals on the face: not an argument against evidence or diagnosis, since published provenance is what we are asking for; not “categories are meaningless,” because thirteen is a real bar doing real work; not a claim that taxonomy is like being labelled; not “the scientists were wrong and the kid was right,” because being corrected was never the injury and not being correctable is; not a demand that every label be revoked, since some of us want ours; and not the naturalistic fallacy, because down to is not because of. Thirteen spreads, eleven diagrams. A method, not a proof.

Zine No. 62
The Light Arrived Before the Name
Stimpunks Zines

On light-travel time, the seventy-one years it took one description of us to reach English, and the difference between what a thing is and what it ends up called.

Light leaves with nothing written on it, so every name in astronomy is an arrival record and never an origin record. Seven measured delays, and what they say about the observer.

11 spreads→
Details

The physics is one sentence and it does all the work: light travels at a finite speed and leaves with nothing written on it. Sunlight is eight minutes and twenty seconds old when it reaches your face; Proxima Centauri’s is 4.2 years old; Andromeda’s is 2.5 million. A photon carries a wavelength and a direction and not a discoverer — everything a star will say about itself is complete at emission, and the name gets attached at the far end, by whoever happened to be standing there with an instrument. Astronomy is honest about this and has a word for the landing: first light is the moment a new telescope opens its eye, and nobody has ever thought the telescope invented the galaxy in the frame. So a name in astronomy is an arrival record, never an origin record — and almost everything in this zine is those two things being confused, with a person in place of a star. Priority is the version written down: credit goes to whoever publishes first, and a comet takes the name of whoever files. Halley earned his fairly — he worked out that the comets of 1531, 1607 and 1682 were one object and in 1705 predicted its 1758 return — and its passages had been recorded for more than two thousand years before him. The rule never claimed otherwise. The trouble starts when the register stops saying what it is a register of. Then 1925, twice. In Cambridge, Massachusetts, Cecilia Payne submitted the thesis showing the stars are overwhelmingly hydrogen and helium; Henry Norris Russell told her it was clearly impossible, and she published with a line saying the abundance was “almost certainly not real.” Russell restated the conclusion in 1929. In Moscow — not Kiev, and the zine keeps its own corrected error on the page rather than fixing it quietly — Grunya Sukhareva finished a clinical description of six boys at the hospital-school she had founded in 1921, recording the aloneness, the flattened expression, the narrow blazing interests and the giftedness underneath. Russian in 1925, German in 1926, English in 1996. Seventy-one years, and half a century after Kanner’s and Asperger’s names were fixed to what she had described. Spread five is the structural spine: a distance is a delay, and the same quantity exists for a finding. Seven crossings, each an interval between two dated events. 168 years from Caroline Herschel’s Royal Astronomical Society Gold Medal in 1828 to the next woman’s, Vera Rubin’s, in 1996. 123 years from Matilda Joslyn Gage mapping the pattern in Woman as Inventor (1870) to Margaret Rossiter naming it the Matilda effect (1993) — the name for late naming was itself a hundred and twenty-three years late, and Rossiter did the one thing the pattern does not do by itself: she looked back down the beam and put the earlier name on it. 71 years to English. 42 years from Annie Jump Cannon joining the Harvard College Observatory in 1896 to Harvard giving her a regular appointment in 1938. 7 years from the first pulsar to a Nobel that went to Hewish and Ryle. 4 years from Payne to Russell. And Mittag-Leffler’s 1925 letter intending to nominate Henrietta Leavitt, who had died in 1921 — there was never a nomination. None of those is a claim about intent; each measures a gap, the way you measure a parallax, and the delay tells you about the observer, not the object. The Bell Burnell spread is deliberately precise where the popular telling is not: she read roughly 96 feet of chart paper a day, found a signal too regular to be noise, and is the second author on Hewish, Bell, Pilkington, Scott & Collins, Nature 217, 709 (1968), period 1.3372795 seconds. She was not written out of the paper. She was written out of the prize. And the pulses had been arriving the whole time — what 1967 changed was the listening. Refusals on the face, because six women is six situations and flattening them would be the second erasure: not that gender explains Sukhareva, since Sher & Gibson give her Jewish identity, her Russian nationality and her Russian- and German-language publication alongside it, and raise the possibility that Asperger omitted her deliberately under Nazi-era antisemitism; not that a missed prize and Stalinist violence are the same harm, since she was attacked at the 1951 Pavlovian Sessions and colleagues around her lost their lives; not that every woman here was silenced alike, since Cannon took the Henry Draper Medal in 1931 and funded the award whose first recipient in 1934 was Payne-Gaposchkin — honoured from outside, unpromoted at home, and she is the exception and forty-two years of the rule; not that recognition and justice are the same, since Herschel took the medal in 1828 and stayed her brother’s assistant on the payroll; not that these men stole, since Russell cited Payne and reported “very gratifying agreement” — the mechanism varies every time and the direction does not; not an argument against priority or citation; and not the naturalistic fallacy. Then the turn that earns the collection. The six children Sukhareva wrote down were Autistic, and so were Autistic children in 1895 and 1795 and every year nobody was taking notes. 1925 is not when we started; it is when somebody was standing there with an instrument. A diagnosis is an arrival record, and the criteria say so out loud — the DSM-5 requires symptoms “present in the early developmental period” while conceding they “may not become fully manifest until social demands exceed limited capacities, or may be masked by learned strategies in later life.” The manual is describing light-travel time. Somebody diagnosed at forty-seven has not been Autistic since they were forty-seven; the paperwork is forty-seven years old and the light is not. You can’t have been, you’d have been picked up is that confusion said out loud, and it made the receiver the origin for girls, for Black and brown children, for anyone whose flavour of it stayed indoors. There is a version of this history in which the first thing anybody read about us was written by a woman who noticed the giftedness in the same paragraph as the difficulty — because she did, and it did not arrive in time to set the tone. The close: everything heavier than hydrogen and helium was cooked inside stars and scattered when they died, and the carbon, oxygen and calcium in you crossed several billion years to arrive with no name attached and no signature required. The calcium works whether or not Payne was believed in 1925. Publishing Sukhareva’s name does not reach her — she died in 1981 — it fixes our instrument, the one we are using on the next person whose account arrives in the wrong language or the wrong decade. Down to is not because of. Eleven spreads, five diagrams. The light was never wrong. The paperwork was late.
