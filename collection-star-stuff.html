Star Stuff

A Star Stuff collection · 15 pieces

The founding register: one settled, checkable fact, followed honestly, already contains the claim about belonging.

What holds these together

Every piece here works the same way. Take one fact that is settled and checkable, follow it honestly, and find that the fact itself already contains the claim about belonging. The calcium in your bones really was forged in stars. Mitochondria really were free-living bacteria that never left. The early universe really did need tiny unevennesses to make anything at all — a perfectly uniform cosmos would have stayed a dead fog forever.

That is the difference between physics as argument and physics as decoration. In this collection the fact is the argument, not an illustration of a point already decided somewhere else. If the science turned out otherwise, the piece would have to change. That is the test, and it is the reason this register earns the trust it asks for.

Sorted by register, not by date

The first ten zines founded this method, and most of this collection is them. But five of these pieces — The Nearest Body, No Universal Now, The Same Water, Not a Line, Shared Signal — were made much later and still belong here, because they do exactly the same thing. Tidal force really does fall off with the cube of distance, which is why the Moon runs the tide even though the Sun out-masses it some 27 million times. Clocks really do run at different rates by speed and gravity, which is why your phone's GPS has to correct for it. Water really does hold and then flip at a threshold rather than stiffening by degrees.

Membership here is decided by mechanism, not chronology. A piece is in this collection if the fact is doing the work. The numbers still say when each one was made — see Cosmic Connections on why we kept them that way.

Not

Not the naturalistic fallacy. The atoms do not owe us this conclusion. We are noticing a rhyme, not collecting a proof, and The Lines We Drew is in this collection precisely to name the trap: the bell curve was recruited to make “normal” look like physics, and eugenics followed. Dressing a human value as a law of nature is the exact move we refuse.

Not interdisciplinary as tourism — borrowing a physics word for its glamour and leaving the physics behind.

Zine No. 17
The Nearest Body
Stimpunks Zines

Tides and co-regulation as star stuff. The Sun out-masses the Moon 27 million times, yet the Moon runs the tide — because tidal force falls off with the cube of distance, and nearness beats mass. So co-regulation is gravity: a safe-enough nervous system nearby pulls yours into rhythm, at a distance, without touching. Spring and neap tides as aligned or crossed pulls, parallel presence as tidal locking, meerkat mode as a sea that lost its moon, and the ecosystemic turn — the storm is in the weather, not the water. Zine No. 19
No Universal Now
Stimpunks Zines

Crip time, read through relativity — with a guest coda by Helen Edgar. Physics says there is no universal “now”: simultaneity is relative and clocks run at different rates by speed and gravity (your phone's GPS corrects for it). So the one true clock is a human invention — railway-and-factory standard time, a convention we mistook for a law and then began grading bodies and minds against. Crip time (Zola & Gill, Kafer, Samuels, Price) bends the clock back to fit us instead of bending us to it — the same move as Minor Worlds, now applied to time. Monotropic spiral time and felt time (Helen Edgar, Bergson, Deleuze) are their own real clock, measured in intensity, not minutes; Samuels' time travel, grief time, and broken time hold the hard edges too. The reading: no clock is master, and being “off the clock” is closer to the truth than fitting it. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set, and a closing coda by Helen Edgar on spiral time.

Zine No. 20
The Same Water
Stimpunks Zines

Fluctuating capacity, read through the physics of phase transitions. Water doesn't get gradually stiffer as it cools — it holds, then flips to ice all at once at a threshold. Same H₂O, different state, and the conditions (not the substance) decide which. So the exhausting question — why could you do it yesterday and not today? — has a clean answer: capacity is a state, not a trait, and you were near a boundary. The hardest part is the crossing itself (threshold anxiety is sensitivity to a real edge — broken thresholds, not broken people); you can be supercooled, held past your limit until one small thing tips everything at once; and recovery slows near the edge, because that's how systems behave. Nothing is fixed or broken — fluid adaptation and weather-bodies, re-adapting to a changed climate. Extends Minor Worlds: change the conditions, not the substance. Grounded in phase-transition physics, Cynthia Kim, Helen Edgar, and Stimpunks' own threshold anxiety. Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 21
Not a Line
Stimpunks Zines

The spectrum was never a severity scale — after Helen Edgar & Patrick Dwyer. “Spectrum” is the most misread word in our vocabulary: people hear a line from mild to severe. But a real spectrum isn't a ladder — split a star's light and every element signs its own pattern of lines, a fingerprint, which is the only way we know what stars are made of. And the sky's figure was never a line: it's a constellation. The zine follows the escalation from Helen Edgar's brand-new essay — the line of neuronormativity, to the spiky profile (better, but still a grid measured from a baseline), to a constellation of intensities: no rank, no norm, no line. Patrick Dwyer's autism constellation anchors it; the functioning-labels critique shows how the line gatekeeps support (“not disabled enough” vs “too disabled”). Held with care: refusing the line doesn't erase that some of us need far more support — need is not a rank; name it instead. Cashes in the rainbow motif on every page and hands off to the Constellation Field Guide. Eight spreads, spectrum-forward diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 23
Bodies of Water
Stimpunks Zines

Hydrofeminism as belonging, read through star stuff — after Astrida Neimanis. You are not a container of water; you are water, roughly 60% of you, briefly gathered into a shape. And none of it is only yours: it was rain, river, cloud, and the inside of other creatures first. Neimanis's gestationality (we each began inside another body's water) and Stacy Alaimo's transcorporeality dissolve the clean edge — we are all downstream of one another, permeable and borrowed. The crucial move, held with care: watery belonging is not dissolution into sameness. Where two rivers meet, the confluence runs braided for miles — one channel, two waters, still distinct. That's the neurodiversity paradigm in an image: the spiky profile survives the merge. A dedicated spread carries shared/collective flow — monotropism's deep channels can align into shared flow (Autistic Realms, Helen Edgar); a watering hole is where currents pool. So belonging is a current, not a border — build watering holes, keep the water moving. Much of Earth's water is older than the Sun. Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 24
Shared Air
Stimpunks Zines

The air we share, read through star stuff and solarpunk — a companion to Bodies of Water. There is only one air: a single, well-mixed body with no walls in it, so thoroughly circulated that your next breath very likely holds atoms once breathed by strangers and by trees across the world. It's a reciprocal breath — we exhale what plants inhale, and our oxygen is the exhaust of ancient cyanobacteria (the Great Oxidation Event, ~2.4 billion years ago), forged first in stars. Every room we share is a shared-air space (a term coined by Helen Edgar & Ryan Boren): in it we trade breath and co-regulate, nervous systems settling into a shared rhythm. The neurodiversity turn: many of us are the canaries of that room. Miners carried canaries because the birds felt the bad air first — not because they were weak, but because they were sensitive, and their alarm was information. Our sensitivity to fragrance, stale air, and fluorescent hum is an early-warning system, not a flaw: don't blame the bird, change the air — build it breathable for the canaries and it's breathable for all. Which raises the harder question: habitable, and for whom? The burden of shared air falls unevenly, so breathable air is a commons, not a private accommodation one person quietly manages. The response is solarpunk: clean air as infrastructure as resistance — open windows, plants, masks, DIY Corsi–Rosenthal boxes — mutual aid you can build in an afternoon. Move quietly and plant things. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 25
Shared Signal
Stimpunks Zines

Connection carried across distance, read through star stuff — a companion to Shared Air. The electromagnetic spectrum is one continuous field: radio waves and starlight are the same phenomenon at different wavelengths, and the same physics that carries a voice call carries a star's light. Shared-signal space — a term coined by Helen Edgar — is the counterpart to shared-air space: a call, a message, a voice note, a synthesised voice, carrying the rhythm on a signal instead of the air of a room. A voice note is breath, timestamped and sent; co-regulation crosses a screen too — thinner, but real (Porges; Streuber et al.). For many of us, text and AAC aren't a fallback from speech but the clearer channel — thinking time, structure, fewer sensory demands, a record to return to (Howard & Sedgewick) — and AAC is the real communication, not a workaround. Aversion to the phone is information, not fragility. Reachable, and for whom? Communication access is a commons; we build our own watering holes — the Autistic Rhizome (Edgar), Discords, AAC — sideways and underground, where lurking counts. The oldest signal of all is a dead star's light still reaching your eye: signal lets connection cross a gap touch can't, so no channel is the master channel (Milton). Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 44
You Cannot Flatten a Sphere
Stimpunks Zines

Gauss's Theorema Egregium (1827): curvature is intrinsic, so paper bends into a tube or a cone but never onto a sphere. Wrapping an orange is not a skill problem — it is forbidden, and the folded pizza slice is the same proof in your hand. The consequence is that no flat map preserves everything: every projection sacrifices area, angle, distance or direction, and Mercator chose angle in 1569 so sailors could hold a bearing — swelling Greenland to the apparent size of an Africa roughly fourteen times larger, without ever saying what it chose. Tissot's answer, from 1859, is the honest one: you cannot remove the distortion, but you can draw it. Then the turn — an IQ, a support level, a functioning label are projections of a many-dimensional person onto one axis, and worse than a map, because a Mercator keeps every place badly while a single number deletes the other dimensions outright. Ask what a summary was optimised for and you learn who it was made for. And if you must summarise: publish the indicatrix. Closes on a globe having no edges — being at the margin was always a fact about where somebody cut. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 45
The Cloud Phase
Stimpunks Zines

Astronomy already named the last stage of a supernova remnant, and the name is not the end — Romano, Behrendt & Burkert (2024) subdivide the radiative phase into four, and the fourth is the cloud phase: the shell stops being driven, the pressure inside falls to match the pressure outside, and the remnant falls back into itself, settling as a chemically enriched cloud. Held as proposed, not settled, and flagged in place. The weight sits on an observation instead. Murray (2011) measured star formation efficiency in the Milky Way's most luminous star-forming complexes at 0.08 — so even in the clouds picked for being the most productive, ninety-two percent never becomes a star, and the Galactic average of 0.005 puts the honest figure nearer ninety-nine and a half. A giant molecular cloud lasts 27 ± 12 million years, and the bright episode inside it is short, late, and the thing that tears the cloud apart. So the in-between is not the gap between the real states: it is the majority state, by mass and by duration, and that is a measurement rather than a consolation. Along the way, SN 1987A condensed at least 0.2 solar masses of new dust inside its own wreckage — grains that did not exist before the star died. Read after Helen Edgar: her Supernova Remnant and Emergence Point from My Monotropic Galaxy, recovery as re-assembly rather than return, and “a soft loosening of pressure” — with the collapse trigger always outside, in the field, never in the cloud. Closes on aluminium-26 in the Allende meteorite: something nearby and freshly dead was in our birth cloud, and which star it was stays contested rather than tidied. Eleven spreads, nine diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. Not a promise that the cloud becomes a star.

Zine No. 46
Companion Stars
Stimpunks Zines

A star bound to another star already has astronomy's own name — a companion star — and Helen Edgar's thirteenth constellation carries it. But the piece opens by correcting itself: most stars are in binaries is the smoothed retelling, and Lada (2006) titled his paper against it. Two-thirds of stellar systems in the Galactic disk are single, because multiplicity climbs steeply with mass — ~26% of M dwarfs, 44 ± 2% of Sun-like stars, near-total at the top — and the galaxy is overwhelmingly M dwarfs. So this zine does not promise anybody their people. What it does instead is describe the binding exactly. Sirius A and B orbit a barycentre lying in neither star, over 50.13 years (Bond et al. 2017), and the pull is equal while the travel is not: the heavier body moves less. Get too close and companions deform each other — ellipsoids, then teardrops, then a shared dumbbell envelope with matter crossing over — so proximity is not automatically good, and it is the wide pairs, out to a parsec with orbits reaching a hundred million years, where both keep their shape. Then the turn, and it is theorems rather than interpretation: at three bodies Bruns (1887) and Poincaré (1890) closed off the shortcuts, and Sundman's 1912 series converges too slowly to use, so the paths are stepped forward together and no one body's path can be solved alone. That is also where chaos was found — exact rules, fully known, and still unpredictable far out. Read with David Gray-Hammond's Chaotic Self and Autistic Rhizome beside Helen's Rhizome Array, holding both halves honestly: the rhizome describes resilience, the physics describes non-separability, and neither cancels the other. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 48
The Shadow Is Bigger Than the Thing
Stimpunks Zines

One settled fact, followed to the place it stops being comfortable. A black hole emits nothing, so there is no photograph and never will be; what the Event Horizon Telescope released is a shadow, and the shadow is not the same size as the thing. For a non-spinning black hole the horizon sits at 2 GM/c² and the shadow’s edge at 3√3 GM/c² — about 2.6 times wider — and that magnification holds near-constant across spins and viewing angles (5 ± 0.2 GM/Dc²), which is the only reason it can be worked backwards into a measurement. The instrument is the planet: eight telescopes, six sites, 3.5 petabytes flown out on hard drives, four imaging teams kept blind to each other because everyone knew what they hoped to see. M87* at 42 µas — an orange, on the Moon. Sagittarius A* harder despite being nearer, its inner orbit turning in thirty minutes against M87*’s thirty days, so the subject moves while the shutter is open. The fact carries the claim from there in two halves: some things are known only by their effect on everything around them, and that effect systematically overstates its cause — what gets observed of an unmet need is the disruption, inflated by years of bending, not the need. Physics has a factor to divide out; a life has none, which is why you ask. Ends on the guardrail rather than the warm note: unseeable is not unreal, and not a blank cheque either. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” set that refuses the black-hole-as-person reading outright. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 51
Almost Shadow
Stimpunks Zines

One settled fact about optics, followed until it turns into a claim about who gets helped. In 1604, drawing eclipse geometry, Kepler found he had umbra for the full shadow and no word at all for the handover between shadow and light — so he made one in a single clause of a diagram: quod est inter KE, NM penumbra dicatur, let what lies between be called penumbra. Paene plus umbra: almost-shadow. The named thing is the zone in between. Astronomy now keeps three — umbra, penumbra, and the antumbra, where the Moon covers the Sun completely and it is still not a total eclipse, a case two words cannot describe. Then the two facts that will not be separated: the penumbra is the enormous zone, thousands of kilometres against an umbral track of a hundred-odd, so almost everyone who has seen an eclipse saw a partial one — and 99 percent obscured is still daylight, because the surviving sliver of photosphere outshines the corona by orders of magnitude. Gradient and threshold, both true, neither alone. Two fields later borrowed Kepler’s word for exactly the cases where a binary costs lives: the ischaemic penumbra of Astrup, Siesjö and Symon (1981), tissue silent but salvageable, which is the entire reason acute stroke has a treatment window; and Justice Douglas in Griswold (1965), whose critics get their say on the page. The turn: partial support is not proportional support, ninety percent of a ramp is a wall, and impaired is not lost. Twelve spreads, five diagrams, a “Not:” set that refuses the severity-spectrum reading outright. A rhyme, not a proof.
