Star Stuff

A Star Stuff collection · 23 pieces

The founding register: one settled, checkable fact, followed honestly, already contains the claim about belonging.

What holds these together

Every piece here works the same way. Take one fact that is settled and checkable, follow it honestly, and find that the fact itself already contains the claim about belonging. The calcium in your bones really was forged in stars. Mitochondria really were free-living bacteria that never left. The early universe really did need tiny unevennesses to make anything at all — a perfectly uniform cosmos would have stayed a dead fog forever.

That is the difference between physics as argument and physics as decoration. In this collection the fact is the argument, not an illustration of a point already decided somewhere else. If the science turned out otherwise, the piece would have to change. That is the test, and it is the reason this register earns the trust it asks for.

Sorted by register, not by date

The first ten zines founded this method, and seven of them are here. But sixteen of these pieces were made much later — The Nearest Body, No Universal Now, The Same Water and Not a Line among them — and still belong here, because they do exactly the same thing. Tidal force really does fall off with the cube of distance, which is why the Moon runs the tide even though the Sun out-masses it some 27 million times. Clocks really do run at different rates by speed and gravity, which is why your phone's GPS has to correct for it. Water really does hold and then flip at a threshold rather than stiffening by degrees.

Membership here is decided by mechanism, not chronology. A piece is in this collection if the fact is doing the work. The numbers still say when each one was made — see Cosmic Connections on why we kept them that way.

Not

Not the naturalistic fallacy. The atoms do not owe us this conclusion. We are noticing a rhyme, not collecting a proof, and The Lines We Drew is in this collection precisely to name the trap: the bell curve was recruited to make “normal” look like physics, and eugenics followed. Dressing a human value as a law of nature is the exact move we refuse.

Not interdisciplinary as tourism — borrowing a physics word for its glamour and leaving the physics behind.

Zine No. 19
No Universal Now
Stimpunks Zines

Crip time, read through relativity — with a guest coda by Helen Edgar. Physics says there is no universal “now”: simultaneity is relative and clocks run at different rates by speed and gravity (your phone's GPS corrects for it). So the one true clock is a human invention — railway-and-factory standard time, a convention we mistook for a law and then began grading bodies and minds against. Crip time (Zola & Gill, Kafer, Samuels, Price) bends the clock back to fit us instead of bending us to it — the same move as Minor Worlds, now applied to time. Monotropic spiral time and felt time (Helen Edgar, Bergson, Deleuze) are their own real clock, measured in intensity, not minutes; Samuels' time travel, grief time, and broken time hold the hard edges too. The reading: no clock is master, and being “off the clock” is closer to the truth than fitting it. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set, and a closing coda by Helen Edgar on spiral time.

Zine No. 20
The Same Water
Stimpunks Zines

Fluctuating capacity, read through the physics of phase transitions. Water doesn't get gradually stiffer as it cools — it holds, then flips to ice all at once at a threshold. Same H₂O, different state, and the conditions (not the substance) decide which. So the exhausting question — why could you do it yesterday and not today? — has a clean answer: capacity is a state, not a trait, and you were near a boundary. The hardest part is the crossing itself (threshold anxiety is sensitivity to a real edge — broken thresholds, not broken people); you can be supercooled, held past your limit until one small thing tips everything at once; and recovery slows near the edge, because that's how systems behave. Nothing is fixed or broken — fluid adaptation and weather-bodies, re-adapting to a changed climate. Extends Minor Worlds: change the conditions, not the substance. Grounded in phase-transition physics, Cynthia Kim, Helen Edgar, and Stimpunks' own threshold anxiety. Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Not a Line
Stimpunks Zines

The spectrum was never a severity scale — after Helen Edgar & Patrick Dwyer. “Spectrum” is the most misread word in our vocabulary: people hear a line from mild to severe. But a real spectrum isn't a ladder — split a star's light and every element signs its own pattern of lines, a fingerprint, which is the only way we know what stars are made of. The zine follows the escalation from Helen Edgar's brand-new essay — the line of neuronormativity, to the spiky profile (better, but still a grid measured from a baseline), to a constellation of intensities: no rank, no norm, no line. Patrick Dwyer's autism constellation anchors it; the functioning-labels critique shows how the line gatekeeps support (“not disabled enough” vs “too disabled”). Held with care: refusing the line doesn't erase that some of us need far more support — need is not a rank; name it instead. Zine No. 24
Shared Air
Stimpunks Zines

The air we share, read through star stuff and solarpunk — a companion to Bodies of Water. There is only one air: a single, well-mixed body with no walls in it, so thoroughly circulated that your next breath very likely holds atoms once breathed by strangers and by trees across the world. It's a reciprocal breath — we exhale what plants inhale, and our oxygen is the exhaust of ancient cyanobacteria (the Great Oxidation Event, ~2.4 billion years ago), forged first in stars. Every room we share is a shared-air space (a term coined by Helen Edgar & Ryan Boren): in it we trade breath and co-regulate, nervous systems settling into a shared rhythm. The neurodiversity turn: many of us are the canaries of that room. Miners carried canaries because the birds felt the bad air first — not because they were weak, but because they were sensitive, and their alarm was information. Our sensitivity to fragrance, stale air, and fluorescent hum is an early-warning system, not a flaw: don't blame the bird, change the air — build it breathable for the canaries and it's breathable for all. Which raises the harder question: habitable, and for whom? The burden of shared air falls unevenly, so breathable air is a commons, not a private accommodation one person quietly manages. The response is solarpunk: clean air as infrastructure as resistance — open windows, plants, masks, DIY Corsi–Rosenthal boxes — mutual aid you can build in an afternoon. Move quietly and plant things. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Shared Signal

Connection carried across distance, read through star stuff — a companion to Shared Air. Zine No. 44
You Cannot Flatten a Sphere
Stimpunks Zines

Gauss's Theorema Egregium (1827): curvature is intrinsic, so paper bends into a tube or a cone but never onto a sphere. Wrapping an orange is not a skill problem — it is forbidden, and the folded pizza slice is the same proof in your hand. The consequence is that no flat map preserves everything: every projection sacrifices area, angle, distance or direction, and Mercator chose angle in 1569 so sailors could hold a bearing — swelling Greenland to the apparent size of an Africa roughly fourteen times larger, without ever saying what it chose. Tissot's answer, from 1859, is the honest one: you cannot remove the distortion, but you can draw it. Then the turn — an IQ, a support level, a functioning label are projections of a many-dimensional person onto one axis, and worse than a map, because a Mercator keeps every place badly while a single number deletes the other dimensions outright. Ask what a summary was optimised for and you learn who it was made for. And if you must summarise: publish the indicatrix. Closes on a globe having no edges — being at the margin was always a fact about where somebody cut. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 45
The Cloud Phase
Stimpunks Zines

Astronomy already named the last stage of a supernova remnant, and the name is not the end — Romano, Behrendt & Burkert (2024) subdivide the radiative phase into four, and the fourth is the cloud phase: the shell stops being driven, the pressure inside falls to match the pressure outside, and the remnant falls back into itself, settling as a chemically enriched cloud. Held as proposed, not settled, and flagged in place. The weight sits on an observation instead. Murray (2011) measured star formation efficiency in the Milky Way's most luminous star-forming complexes at 0.08 — so even in the clouds picked for being the most productive, ninety-two percent never becomes a star, and the Galactic average of 0.005 puts the honest figure nearer ninety-nine and a half. A giant molecular cloud lasts 27 ± 12 million years, and the bright episode inside it is short, late, and the thing that tears the cloud apart. So the in-between is not the gap between the real states: it is the majority state, by mass and by duration, and that is a measurement rather than a consolation. Along the way, SN 1987A condensed at least 0.2 solar masses of new dust inside its own wreckage — grains that did not exist before the star died. Read after Helen Edgar: her Supernova Remnant and Emergence Point from My Monotropic Galaxy, recovery as re-assembly rather than return, and “a soft loosening of pressure” — with the collapse trigger always outside, in the field, never in the cloud. Closes on aluminium-26 in the Allende meteorite: something nearby and freshly dead was in our birth cloud, and which star it was stays contested rather than tidied. Eleven spreads, nine diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. Not a promise that the cloud becomes a star.

Zine No. 46
Companion Stars
Stimpunks Zines

A star bound to another star already has astronomy's own name — a companion star — and Helen Edgar's thirteenth constellation carries it. But the piece opens by correcting itself: most stars are in binaries is the smoothed retelling, and Lada (2006) titled his paper against it. Two-thirds of stellar systems in the Galactic disk are single, because multiplicity climbs steeply with mass — ~26% of M dwarfs, 44 ± 2% of Sun-like stars, near-total at the top — and the galaxy is overwhelmingly M dwarfs. So this zine does not promise anybody their people. What it does instead is describe the binding exactly. Sirius A and B orbit a barycentre lying in neither star, over 50.13 years (Bond et al. 2017), and the pull is equal while the travel is not: the heavier body moves less. Get too close and companions deform each other — ellipsoids, then teardrops, then a shared dumbbell envelope with matter crossing over — so proximity is not automatically good, and it is the wide pairs, out to a parsec with orbits reaching a hundred million years, where both keep their shape. Then the turn, and it is theorems rather than interpretation: at three bodies Bruns (1887) and Poincaré (1890) closed off the shortcuts, and Sundman's 1912 series converges too slowly to use, so the paths are stepped forward together and no one body's path can be solved alone. That is also where chaos was found — exact rules, fully known, and still unpredictable far out. Read with David Gray-Hammond's Chaotic Self and Autistic Rhizome beside Helen's Rhizome Array, holding both halves honestly: the rhizome describes resilience, the physics describes non-separability, and neither cancels the other. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 48
The Shadow Is Bigger Than the Thing
Stimpunks Zines

One settled fact, followed to the place it stops being comfortable. A black hole emits nothing, so there is no photograph and never will be; what the Event Horizon Telescope released is a shadow, and the shadow is not the same size as the thing. For a non-spinning black hole the horizon sits at 2 GM/c² and the shadow’s edge at 3√3 GM/c² — about 2.6 times wider — and that magnification holds near-constant across spins and viewing angles (5 ± 0.2 GM/Dc²), which is the only reason it can be worked backwards into a measurement. The instrument is the planet: eight telescopes, six sites, 3.5 petabytes flown out on hard drives, four imaging teams kept blind to each other because everyone knew what they hoped to see. M87* at 42 µas — an orange, on the Moon. Sagittarius A* harder despite being nearer, its inner orbit turning in thirty minutes against M87*’s thirty days, so the subject moves while the shutter is open. The fact carries the claim from there in two halves: some things are known only by their effect on everything around them, and that effect systematically overstates its cause — what gets observed of an unmet need is the disruption, inflated by years of bending, not the need. Physics has a factor to divide out; a life has none, which is why you ask. Ends on the guardrail rather than the warm note: unseeable is not unreal, and not a blank cheque either. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” set that refuses the black-hole-as-person reading outright. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 51
Almost Shadow
Stimpunks Zines

One settled fact about optics, followed until it turns into a claim about who gets helped. In 1604, drawing eclipse geometry, Kepler found he had umbra for the full shadow and no word at all for the handover between shadow and light — so he made one in a single clause of a diagram: quod est inter KE, NM penumbra dicatur, let what lies between be called penumbra. Paene plus umbra: almost-shadow. The named thing is the zone in between. Astronomy now keeps three — umbra, penumbra, and the antumbra, where the Moon covers the Sun completely and it is still not a total eclipse, a case two words cannot describe. Then the two facts that will not be separated: the penumbra is the enormous zone, thousands of kilometres against an umbral track of a hundred-odd, so almost everyone who has seen an eclipse saw a partial one — and 99 percent obscured is still daylight, because the surviving sliver of photosphere outshines the corona by orders of magnitude. Gradient and threshold, both true, neither alone. Two fields later borrowed Kepler’s word for exactly the cases where a binary costs lives: the ischaemic penumbra of Astrup, Siesjö and Symon (1981), tissue silent but salvageable, which is the entire reason acute stroke has a treatment window; and Justice Douglas in Griswold (1965), whose critics get their say on the page. The turn: partial support is not proportional support, ninety percent of a ramp is a wall, and impaired is not lost. And the distinction the piece owes, which is the sharpest thing in it: an umbra is a relation between a source, an obstruction and a place — never a property of the ground it falls on. The pavement does not have an umbra; it is in one. So a total blockage of support is real and is a fact about an arrangement somebody built and can unbuild, while an umbra located inside a person is a category error. Functioning labels perform exactly that relocation, and “profound autism” — proposed in the Lancet Commission (2021) for people held to be “not able to advocate for themselves” — is the umbra version of it. Steven Kapp’s 2023 review finds that minimally speaking Autistic people can communicate with responsive partners and can self-advocate, which makes that criterion partly a statement about the listener. Refusing the label is not a denial of anyone’s support needs, and the zine says so plainly, crediting Helen Edgar. Thirteen spreads, five diagrams, a “Not:” set that now refuses the bodymind umbra first. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 53
The Moon Is Always Half Lit
Stimpunks Zines

One settled fact about geometry, followed until it turns into a claim about how people get read. The Sun lights one hemisphere of the Moon — not usually, continuously — so a crescent is not a Moon with less light falling on it. It is a fully half-lit Moon seen nearly edge-on to its own daylight, and the lit half is exactly as large at new Moon as at full. Nothing dims. Nothing is withheld. What changes is the angle between the Sun, the Moon and you, which means a phase needs three bodies and one of them is the observer. The US Naval Observatory is precise about the consequence: New Moon, First Quarter, Full Moon and Last Quarter are defined to occur at 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° of longitude difference, which makes them instants with no duration, while waxing crescent, waxing gibbous, waning gibbous and waning crescent are the week-long stretches between. The familiar chart is four knife-edges and four long weeks drawn as eight equal boxes, over a fraction that changes without a single step. Because the observer is one arm of the angle, the same crescent at the same instant is lit on the right from Edinburgh, on the left from Melbourne, and underneath near the equator — three drawings, three correct answers, one Moon. There is a far side and there is no dark side: at new Moon the far side is in full daylight, and Luna 3 photographed it on 7 October 1959, developing the film in an onboard darkroom and scanning it home. Nor is the lock rigid — libration shows us 59 percent, not half. The phases are complements, so our new Moon is a full Earth there, and the ashen glow on a crescent's dark limb is our own light returned, explained by Leonardo around 1506 and never published. The synodic month runs 29.53 days against a 27.32-day orbit, because the reference keeps moving: you can finish the circle and still not count as returned. The turn: waxing and waning are words about the geometry, not about the body — a functioning label is a phase recorded as a substance, and “but she seems fine with me” is a report of one angle offered as the object. And the turn is landed in a life rather than left abstract: Ryan Boren’s own account of being “both r-word and genius” across one school career. Hyperlexic and Autistic, he would not read aloud — situational mutism and exposure anxiety “locked my lips” — until one day, in a quiet corner with a semblance of psychological safety, he read a book cover to cover in a whisper and asked, “Can I go now?” He describes going “from being a full time r-word to a part time one, depending on who was in the room with me and if I felt safe enough speak” — a phase, recorded in the vocabulary of proportion, with the observer’s position stated plainly by the person being measured. And his sentence “Autistic people are psychological safety barometers” inverts the whole measurement, because a barometer’s reading is a fact about the surrounding air: the instrument was reading the room, and the room wrote the result down as a fact about the child. The guardrail gets its own spread, because the easy version of this argument is cruel: a crescent really does deliver less light to you. Measurably. The correction is not that the difference is imaginary but that it is located in the arrangement — and the refusals set turns down “just make them feel safe” as a new flattening, since the point is that the variables are in the arrangement, not that there is only one of them. Fourteen spreads, four diagrams. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 54
How Anything Gets Built
Stimpunks Zines

The Five Neurodivergent Love Locutions set beside five mechanisms by which matter is actually assembled — on the argument that these are construction methods, not compensations. Infodumping: a star cannot stop telling you what it is made of. Every photon leaving carries its full composition, whether or not anyone reads it — Fraunhofer catalogued the dark lines in 1814, Kirchhoff and Bunsen showed in 1859–60 that each is an element’s fingerprint, and Cecilia Payne read hydrogen out of them in 1925. We know we are star stuff because stars will not shut up about themselves, which makes the infodump the evidentiary basis of this entire collection. Parallel play: El-Badry, Rix and Heintz catalogue 1.3 million wide binaries in Gaia eDR3, bound out to a full parsec, neither star perturbing the other, orbits no observer will see completed — not failed close binaries but the commonest stable outcome the galaxy makes. Support swapping: W Ursae Majoris contact binaries share one convective envelope, and energy crossing it from the larger star to the smaller holds both within about 200 K despite a mass ratio near 0.3 — capacity redistributed by the configuration itself. And the honest complication: in Algol systems the donor gave away so much mass it became the less massive, apparently less-developed star, a contradiction Crawford resolved in 1955 — the one that looks behind is the one that gave. Deep pressure: nothing fuses unsqueezed. The solar core sits near 2.4 × 1016 Pa, over 200 billion atmospheres, and the crushing is not a condition the star endures on the way to making elements — the pressure is the manufacturing process. Penguin pebbling: planetesimal accretion was too slow to finish a giant-planet core before the gas disk dissipated; pebble accretion, named by Lambrechts and Johansen in 2012, does it in 10⁴–10⁶ years. The small offering is not the lesser route. For those cores it is the route — and it stops itself once the core opens a pressure bump, so even the sky knows when someone has had enough pebbles. Then the turn, on the face of the zine rather than in a footnote: five-for-five is exactly the shape of a claim that circulates because it is tidy, so the refusal is stated outright. The physics does not justify the love, and a universe that could approve could also decline. Down to is not because of. Amythest Schaber originated the five; Stimpunks put them into currency; penguin pebbling came later through Lindsey; Helen Edgar has taken the framework furthest. Eight spreads, five diagrams. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 55
What Looks Like Chaos
Stimpunks Zines

Nest building is the textbook example of pure instinct — a bird constructs a thing it has never seen, without instruction, and gets it right the first time, so the nest must be something the bird contains rather than something it works out. That claim is falsifiable, because a template predicts one specific thing: sameness, between individuals and within one individual across attempts. Somebody went and measured whether the sameness is there. Southern Botswana, seven colour-ringed male Southern Masked weavers, territories searched daily so a nest was found at the moment it was started; camcorders square to the branch, three nests filmed per bird — twenty-one nests — and the footage coded by a researcher blind to which bird and which date. During the first phase of construction not one measured behaviour was repeatable within a male: not the direction he carried the grass, not how long he wove, not which quadrant he favoured, not how often he lost hold. Then the individuality: two birds consistently pushed the first end of a blade into the right side of the nest, two consistently into the left, three had no side at all — and pooled across all seven, the bias vanished (G = 0.06, p = 0.81). Personal handedness with no species handedness underneath it, which is precisely what a template cannot produce. And they improved: across a season each male dropped grass less often as he built more nests (p < 0.01), while the birds with the most consistent personal style finished the ring phase fastest (R² = 0.25) — a way of one's own was not an eccentricity to be corrected, it was the thing that made him quick. Thirteen years later the harder finding: forty-three white-browed sparrow weaver family groups build architecturally distinct roosts, some with long entrance tubes and some with almost none, stable across years, differing between groups whose territories sit metres apart in the same trees, and not explained by weather, body size, tree height or kinship — then the closer, that the styles are robust to immigration. Not a template and not the environment: a tradition. Set beside all of this is David Gray-Hammond's account of his own room — the diffuser, the books read several at once, the nothing-tidied-away because some days end in a crash rather than a decision — and his sentence about it: to most, it appears that I live in a state of chaos… I know where every item I need resides. The refusals are printed on the zine's face rather than left implied. Not permission from nature, because a universe that could approve could also decline, and down to is not because of. Not instinct versus learning. Not a clever-animal story, since the authors themselves attribute the improvement to increasing dexterity and say any role for cognition still needs demonstrating. And the honest one: not every mess is a nest — burnout collapses the architecture, and the ruins look from the doorway exactly like the curated version. What the science kills is narrower and sturdier for being narrow: the inference that an order you cannot read is not an order. Two centuries of confident observers looked at a nest and saw a machine executing, and were describing the limits of watching while calling it a fact about the bird. Ten spreads, two diagrams. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 57
The Shell Was Your Calcium
Stimpunks Zines

In the trans community, getting your egg cracked is the moment somebody knows and accepts that they are trans; the therapist Essy Knopf extended the term to Autistic and ADHD experience in 2025, and credited its origin himself. The word is borrowed and this zine says so — no individual coined it, and Evelyn Bauer's history for Them traces the earliest documented use to an Urban Dictionary entry in August 2016 and finds even Susan Stryker unable to date her own first encounter with it. What the zine argues with is not Knopf but what happens to the metaphor afterwards, when repetition flattens it into a door: one threshold, crossed once, a settled person on the far side. So we checked it against some eggs. The shell does not get thinner. From about day eleven the chorioallantoic membrane dissolves calcium out of the wall, and Halgrain and colleagues measured the result — thickness between day 0 and day 15 not significantly different (p = 0.110), breaking strength between day 11 and day 15 significantly reduced (p = 0.007). Measure it and nothing is happening. Weigh what it can bear and everything is. Nobody could see it; there was nothing to see. Then where the calcium goes: the shell supplies more than eighty per cent of all the calcium a chick embryo gets, roughly 2.2 grams, and at hatch the skeleton holds about three quarters of the calcium in the whole animal. The container becomes the frame, by mass, and there is no moment where one ends and the other begins — and since collagen is piezoelectric, as Iwao Yasuda showed in the 1950s, the wall you broke is now the part of you that turns pressure into charge. Third, the messy middle is a documented stage: at internal pipping the beak enters the air cell and the lungs begin working while the shell is still whole, a staged handover Margaret Vince traced in three parts. From outside, an unmarked egg. Fourth, quail embryos click, and an egg set in a clutch a day late speeds up and hatches with the others — while a slower click rate retards the ones ahead. Synchronisation runs both ways: the ones ahead slow down and wait. And the close, from Mair and colleagues' 2026 study of 225 posts: not a door but an oscillating Grief, Relief, and Belief Cycle, whose fourth theme is post-diagnosis burnout, with the authors asking that identification be treated as a first step rather than a terminal component of a service. The refusals are on the zine's face. Not cracking somebody else's egg — the egg tooth is on the inside of the shell, and you can only click. Not the person before was fake. Not a graduation. Not permission from nature, because chick embryos have no identities and down to is not because of. And not the egg as the enemy, which is the uncomfortable one, because masking cost us enormously and also got some of us here alive. Ten spreads, four diagrams. A rhyme, not a proof.
