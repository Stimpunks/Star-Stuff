### [You Cannot Flatten a Sphere](https://starstuff.earth/cannot-flatten-a-sphere-zine.html)

Zine No. 44 · Stimpunks Zines

*on curvature, map projections, and what a single number has to throw away*

Gauss proved in 1827 that no flat map can preserve everything. An IQ score is literally a projection — so it must destroy information.

**Details**

Try wrapping a sheet of paper smoothly around an orange. It creases, always, and that is not clumsiness — it is **forbidden**. Gauss proved it in 1827 and liked the result enough to call it the *Theorema Egregium*, the remarkable theorem: a surface’s curvature is intrinsic, so bending that doesn’t stretch can never change it. Paper rolls into a tube or a cone because those are still flat in the sense that matters; a sphere is not, and no care gets you there. You have used this today without noticing — fold a pizza slice and the tip stops drooping, because curving it one way forces it to stay straight the other. The consequence: **no flat map of the world can preserve everything**, and every projection must sacrifice area, angle, distance or direction. Mercator chose angle, in 1569, so a constant compass bearing would be a straight line — superb for sailors, and it swells Greenland to the apparent size of an Africa roughly fourteen times larger. The map never says what it chose, and that silence is the scandal rather than the distortion. Then Tissot’s answer from 1859, which is the honest one: you cannot remove the distortion, so *draw* it — a grid of ellipses showing exactly what this map did to this place. And then the turn. An IQ score, a DSM-5 support level, a functioning label: each takes a many-axis person and returns one value, which is not a metaphor but literally a projection — so it must destroy information, the choice of what to destroy is somebody’s, and the number never records it. Worse than a map, in fact: a Mercator keeps every place badly, while a single number doesn’t distort the other dimensions, it **deletes** them. Ask what a summary was optimised for and you learn whose journey it serves — Mercator for sailors, a support level for a budget, a functioning label for a gate. If you must summarise, **publish the indicatrix**: the axes it came from, the range it moves across, what it was built for, reviewed by the person measured. Closes on the gentlest thing the theorem gives — a globe has no edges and no corners, so being at the margin was never information about you, only about where somebody cut. Companion to No. 21 *Not a Line*, which asks what shape the thing is; this asks what a reduction costs. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

### [The Cloud Phase](https://starstuff.earth/the-cloud-phase-zine.html)

Zine No. 45 · Stimpunks Zines

*on the last stage of a supernova remnant, and how long the in-between actually lasts*

The story we get told about a dead star ends about one step past the explosion. The physics runs further than the sentence does.

**Details**

The story we get told about a dead star ends about one step past the explosion: the shell expands, thins, cools, and *disperses into the interstellar medium*. Fade out. But the physics runs further than the sentence does, and there is another word waiting at the end of it. Romano, Behrendt & Burkert (2024) followed remnants through their long cold decline and found the radiative phase divides into four stages — and the last one they call **the cloud phase**. Not the end phase. Not the dispersal phase. The *cloud* phase: once nothing is driving the shell and the pressure inside has fallen to match the pressure outside, nothing holds the cavity open, so the remnant **falls back into itself** and settles as a chemically enriched cloud. That is a 2024 simulation and it is held here as proposed, flagged in place — because the argument's weight sits somewhere firmer. Murray (2011) measured what clouds actually do: in the Milky Way's most luminous star-forming complexes, the star formation efficiency averages 0.08, so **ninety-two percent of the cloud never becomes a star** — and that is the flattering number, since the Galactic average of 0.005 puts it nearer ninety-nine and a half. A giant molecular cloud lasts 27 ± 12 million years, and the bright episode inside it is short, late, and the thing that tears the cloud apart. Which turns the story the other way round: **the in-between is not the wait between the real states. It is the majority state, by mass and by duration** — and that is a measurement, offered as fact rather than as comfort. Along the way, SN 1987A: at least 0.2 solar masses of dust condensed *inside* its own wreckage, grains that did not exist anywhere before the star died. The debris field is a foundry. Written after Helen Edgar, whose *Supernova Remnant* and *Emergence Point* map the same stage from the inside — burnout as “remnants of my old self entangled in new ways of being,” recovery as re-assembly rather than return, and deterritorialisation as “a soft loosening of pressure.” A cloud never decides to collapse; it is collapsed upon, by gravity and radiation and its neighbours, so the honest question when one of us reorganises is what the pressure is and where it comes from. Held with care throughout: gestation is not a promise of a star, most of the mass never lights, and the claim is only that the cloud is a real state, not a waiting room. Closes on aluminium-26 in the Allende meteorite — something nearby and freshly dead was in our own birth cloud, and *which* star is openly contested fifty years on, so we name the contest instead of picking a winner. Eleven spreads, nine diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

### [Companion Stars](https://starstuff.earth/companion-stars-zine.html)

Zine No. 46 · Stimpunks Zines

*on gravitational binding, the three-body problem, and why no one's path can be solved alone*

A star that shares a system with another star already has a name, and it is astronomy's own working word: a companion star.

**Details**

A star that shares a system with another star already has a name, and it is astronomy’s own working word: a **companion star**. Helen Edgar’s thirteenth constellation carries it — “the people who orbit close … who don’t need me to perform and who just ‘get it’.” But before any of that, a correction, because this collection exists partly to take science back from the version that travels furthest. You have met the line *most stars are in binaries — the Sun is the odd one out.* It isn’t so. Charles Lada put the binary statistics beside the stellar mass function in 2006 and published the answer as his title: **most stars are single** — two-thirds of main-sequence systems in the Galactic disk. Multiplicity climbs steeply with mass (~26% of M dwarfs, 44 ± 2% of Sun-like stars, near-total for the most massive), and the galaxy is overwhelmingly made of the small end. **So this zine will not tell you that everybody has their people.** The claim it makes instead is smaller and holds all the way down: where the binding is real, here is exactly what it is. Sirius A and its white-dwarf companion trace a 50.13-year orbit about a barycentre lying in neither star — and equal binding is not equal motion, since the heavier body is displaced less, which is worth saying out loud for anyone who has been told they’re imagining who does more of the moving. Then the hardest spread: bring two stars close and they *do* deform each other — stretched to ellipsoids, drawn to teardrops, finally sharing one dumbbell envelope with matter crossing between them. Proximity is not automatically good. It is the wide pairs — bound out to a parsec, one orbit longer than the whole history of flowering plants — where both keep their shape. And then the turn, which is theorems rather than reading: with two bodies you can write the solution down, but Bruns (1887) proved no further algebraic integrals exist, Poincaré (1890) closed off the single-valued analytic ones, and Sundman’s 1912 series converges far too slowly to use. So these systems are stepped forward numerically, together, and **no one body’s path can be solved on its own and the others added afterward**. That same work is where chaos was found — and the part the popular word destroys is that *nothing is random*: every rule is exact and known, and the system is still unpredictable far out. Read beside the language this community built first — David Gray-Hammond’s *Chaotic Self* (“you can’t unqueer a queer mind”) and *Autistic Rhizome*, with Helen’s *Rhizome Array* and *Mycelial Stars* — and holding the tension honestly rather than smoothing it: the rhizome describes resilience, no node depending on another to exist; the three-body physics describes non-separability while bodies are bound. Both true, neither cancelling. Closes on what actually holds a bound system: no agreement, no shared origin, no similarity, no upkeep, no performance — mutual gravity and nothing else, which means there is no version of a companion star that has to earn it. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

### [The Shadow Is Bigger Than the Thing](https://starstuff.earth/the-shadow-is-bigger-zine.html)

Zine No. 48 · Stimpunks Zines

*on the black hole nobody photographed, and what can only be known by its effect on everything else*

Nobody has photographed a black hole, and nobody will — the ordinary way of knowing a thing is simply unavailable, and no better lens fixes it.

**Details**

Nobody has photographed a black hole, and nobody will. A camera works by catching light that left the subject, and the event horizon is the surface where every outward direction has stopped existing — so the ordinary way of knowing a thing is simply unavailable, and no better lens fixes it. What the Event Horizon Telescope released in April 2019 and May 2022 is a **shadow**: the set of directions in which light that might have reached you went into the hole instead, ringed by the light that passed far enough out to be bent around and escape. Then the fact this zine is built on, which is exact geometry rather than an impression. For a non-spinning black hole the event horizon sits at 2 *GM/c²* and the edge of the shadow at 3√3 *GM/c²* — so **the dark circle is about 2.6 times wider than the object casting it.** Gravity does not merely hide the thing; it magnifies the hiding. And that magnification is nearly constant across spins and viewing angles (5 ± 0.2 *GM/Dc²*, a spread of about four percent), which is the only reason anyone can work backwards from it. The instrument was the planet: eight radio telescopes at six sites from Spain to the South Pole, observing at 1.3 mm, writing ~3.5 petabytes to helium-filled hard drives that had to be *flown* to two correlators — the Antarctic share stranded until the winter ended. No site saw the ring; no site could have. Four imaging teams then worked **blind to one another**, on two different families of algorithm, because everyone involved knew what they hoped to find. M87\*’s ring came out at 42 ± 3 microarcseconds — an orange, on the Moon. Sagittarius A\* was harder despite being nearer, because its innermost orbit takes about thirty minutes against M87\*’s thirty days: the subject rearranges itself while the shutter is open. Then the turn, in two halves. Some things are known only by their effect on everything around them — monotropism, pain, a sensory environment, an access need — and *the absence of a photograph was never evidence of an absent thing.* But the geometry has a second half, and this piece does not stop before it: **what people see of an unmet need is the disruption, not the need, and the disruption is the larger object**, inflated by every year of bending and masking and quiet accommodation that got folded into it. In physics you can divide the factor out. In a life there is none, which is exactly why you ask. Closes on the guardrail rather than the warm note: unseeable is not unreal, and it is not a blank cheque either — the collaboration pinned Sagittarius A\* to 51.8 ± 2.3 microarcseconds without ever seeing it. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set that turns down the obvious reading first. A rhyme, not a proof.

### [Almost Shadow](https://starstuff.earth/almost-shadow-zine.html)

Zine No. 51 · Stimpunks Zines

*on the zone Kepler had to name, why partial is the enormous one, and the two fields that borrowed the word*

In 1604 Kepler was drawing the geometry of an eclipse and ran out of language — so in the middle of a diagram he made a word: penumbra.

**Details**

In 1604 Johannes Kepler was drawing the geometry of an eclipse and ran out of language. The Sun is not a point of light but a *disc*, so every part of it throws a shadow in a slightly different direction and the result comes out layered: a core where the whole disc is hidden, an outer region where some of it still shines through, and between them no edge at all — just a handover. He had *umbra* for the core. For the handover he had nothing. So in the middle of a diagram in *Ad Vitellionem Paralipomena* he made one: ***quod est inter KE, NM penumbra dicatur*** — let what lies between be called penumbra. The verb is *dicatur*, *let it be called*; that is not a man using a word, it is the word being made because a drawing demanded it. *Paene* plus *umbra*: **almost-shadow**, and what gets named is neither the dark nor the light but the thing between them. Astronomy keeps three zones now, and the third is the one nobody has heard of: the **antumbra**, past the shadow cone’s apex, where the Moon sits *entirely* in front of the Sun — nothing sticking out, nothing off to one side — and it is *still* not a total eclipse, because the Moon is simply too small in your sky to cover what is behind it. Complete obstruction, and the darkness never comes. Two words would have forced that case into one of the other two and both would have been wrong. Then the pair of facts this zine refuses to separate. First, **the partial zone is the enormous one**: the umbral track lands as a stripe a hundred-odd kilometres wide while the penumbra covers thousands, so the overwhelming majority of everyone who has ever seen a solar eclipse saw a partial one. The penumbra is where we live; totality is the exception. Second, and cutting the other way, **99 percent obscured is still daylight.** The corona is only about as bright as a full Moon, and the surviving sliver of photosphere is hundreds to thousands of times brighter than that — so it obliterates the corona and keeps the sky blue. Illuminance falls roughly a hundred-fold in the last two minutes, another order of magnitude in the final *half* minute, more than four orders of magnitude in all. The last increment is not an increment; it is a change of state, and on the far side of it the birds go quiet. Gradient *and* threshold, and any account that can hold only one of them is describing something other than the world — *all gradient* gives you “everyone’s a bit like that” and owes nobody anything; *all threshold* gives you the eligibility form and makes the enormous middle vanish. Two other fields worked this out and reached for Kepler’s word, both times because a binary would cost lives. In 1981 Astrup, Siesjö and Symon found *two* thresholds in ischaemic brain — electrical failure, then ion-pump failure — and named the zone between them the **ischaemic penumbra**: functionally silent, structurally intact, salvageable on reperfusion. That word is the reason acute stroke has a treatment window at all; every intervention is aimed at the penumbra, because the core is past help and the healthy tissue does not need it. And in *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965) Justice Douglas held that guarantees have “penumbras, formed by emanations” — a borrowing the zine presents alongside its critics rather than beneath them, since a shadow with no measurable edge can be stretched to cover whatever the reader wanted covered. The turn: **partial support is not proportional support.** Ninety percent of a ramp is a wall; captions that drop out for the last two minutes did not deliver 98 percent of the talk; almost accessible is a phase change away from accessible. And the most hopeful sentence here is the least sentimental one, carried over from 1981 — in the penumbra, what looks like loss is often deprivation, and deprivation is reversible if you can see the zone exists and get there in time. **Impaired is not lost.** And the distinction the piece owes, which is the sharpest thing in it: **an umbra is a relation between a source, an obstruction and a place — never a property of the ground it falls on.** The pavement does not *have* an umbra; it is *in* one, and nothing about the pavement is different. So a total blockage of *support* is real and is a fact about an arrangement somebody built and can unbuild. **An umbra located inside a person is a category error.** Functioning labels perform exactly that relocation — *high-functioning*, *severe*, *level 3* take a shadow cast by circumstances and write it on a body — and *“profound autism”*, proposed in the *Lancet* Commission (2021) for people held to be “not able to advocate for themselves,” is the umbra version of the move. Steven Kapp’s 2023 review finds that minimally speaking Autistic people *can* communicate, especially with responsive partners, and *can* self-advocate — which makes that criterion partly a statement about the listener, the method and the room. So *profound* reads deprivation as loss, on a person. Refusing the label is **not** a denial of anyone’s support needs, and the zine says so plainly, crediting Helen Edgar: *“Autistic people with profound and multiple learning disabilities and health needs are not ‘Profoundly Autistic,’ they are autistic with specific needs.”* Thirteen spreads, five diagrams, a “Not:” set that now refuses the bodymind umbra first. A rhyme, not a proof.

### [The Moon Is Always Half Lit](https://starstuff.earth/always-half-lit-zine.html)

Zine No. 53 · Stimpunks Zines

**Details**

The Sun lights one hemisphere of the Moon. Not usually, not on average — *continuously*, without a single interruption that was not an eclipse. Which means a crescent Moon is not a Moon that has had most of its light taken away. It is a **fully half-lit Moon seen nearly edge-on to its own daylight**, and the illuminated hemisphere is exactly as large at new Moon as at full. Nothing dims. Nothing is withheld. Nothing is recovering. What changes over a month is not the Moon but the angle between the Sun, the Moon and *you* — a phase needs three bodies, and one of them is the observer, so there is no fact about “what phase the Moon is in” that does not have your position inside it. The US Naval Observatory is precise about where that leads: New Moon, First Quarter, Full Moon and Last Quarter *are defined to occur* when the excess of the Moon’s apparent ecliptic longitude over the Sun’s is 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° — which makes them **instants**, moments with no duration — while waxing crescent, waxing gibbous, waning gibbous and waning crescent are the roughly week-long stretches between them. So the familiar chart of eight equal boxes is really **four knife-edges and four long weeks**, drawn over an illuminated fraction that changes smoothly and without a single step. The boxes are ours; we drew them because a chart needs boxes, and then began reading them back as though we had found them. And because the observer is one arm of the angle, moving *you* changes the answer: at one instant, one lit fraction, one Moon, the crescent is lit along the right edge from Edinburgh, along the left from Melbourne, and underneath near the equator, riding low as a bowl — a *wet moon*, tipped up to hold the rain. Three drawings, three correct answers, nobody seeing an illusion. Luna 3 went and looked on 7 October 1959, returning 29 grainy frames of about seventy percent of it after developing the film in a small automated darkroom it was carrying and scanning the negatives home across the vacuum. What came back was not a shadow but a sunlit landscape, crowded with craters, nearly free of maria. It had been like that the whole time. Nor is the lock rigid: libration in longitude and latitude shows us about **59 percent** of the surface rather than half, so even the thing we describe as fixed turns out to be negotiating. The phases are *complements* — our new Moon is a full Earth seen from there, and at every instant the two sum to one whole, so the light is never missing, only ever somewhere you are not standing. The ashen glow on a crescent’s dark limb is that light returned: earthshine, *the old Moon in the new Moon’s arms*, explained by Leonardo around 1506 in the *Codex Leicester*, never published, and slightly wrong (he credited the oceans; it is mostly clouds). The first *published* explanation is credited to Michael Mästlin, who taught Kepler at Tübingen — teacher and student either side of the same refusal to accept a picture with only two states in it. And the month you live by is longer than the orbit it is made of: 27.32 days to complete a full circuit against the stars, but 29.53 to return to the same phase, because Earth moved roughly 27° meanwhile and the alignment slid forward. **You can finish the circle and still not count as returned, because the thing you are measured against keeps moving.** The turn: **waxing and waning are words about the geometry.** They are not words about the Moon, and nothing in the Moon does them. A functioning label is a phase recorded as a substance — it takes the crescent and files it as the whole Moon there is — and “but she seems fine with me” is not a fact about her, it is a report of one angle offered as though it were the object. And his sentence **“Autistic people are psychological safety barometers”** inverts the whole measurement, because a barometer’s reading is a fact about the surrounding air: the instrument was reading the room, and the room wrote the result down as a fact about the child. The guardrail gets its own spread, because the easy version of this argument is cruel: **a crescent really does deliver less light to you.** Measurably. The correction is not that the difference is imaginary but that it is *located in the arrangement* — and the refusals set turns down “just make them feel safe” as a new flattening, since the point is that the variables are in the arrangement, not that there is only one of them. Fourteen spreads, four diagrams. A rhyme, not a proof.

### [How Anything Gets Built](https://starstuff.earth/how-anything-gets-built-zine.html)

Zine No. 54 · Stimpunks Zines

*the five neurodivergent love locutions, and the mechanisms that actually assemble matter*

**Details**

The Five Neurodivergent Love Locutions — infodumping, parallel play, support swapping, deep pressure, penguin pebbling — set beside five mechanisms by which matter is actually assembled, on the argument that these are *construction methods, not compensations*. The deficit story ranks all five as thin stand-ins for the real thing: real conversation, real play, real gifts. That story needs them to be structurally deficient forms of connection. Structurally, they are how things get built. **Infodumping:** a star cannot stop telling you what it is made of. Every photon leaving passes out through the star’s own atmosphere and each element takes its specific bite out of the spectrum, so the light arrives carrying a complete, unedited account of the composition, whether or not anyone is reading. Fraunhofer catalogued the dark lines in 1814 without knowing what they were; Kirchhoff and Bunsen established in 1859–60 that each is an element’s fingerprint; Cecilia Payne read hydrogen out of them in 1925 and was pressured to soften a conclusion that was right. **We know we are star stuff because stars will not shut up about themselves** — which makes the unasked-for infodump the entire evidentiary basis of this collection. **Parallel play:** filed for decades as a developmental *stage*, something to be passed through. The sky’s version is the wide binary, and El-Badry, Rix and Heintz catalogue *1.3 million* of them in Gaia eDR3, genuinely bound at separations out to a full parsec — 3.26 light years — neither star perturbing the other, orbits so long no observer will see one completed. Nothing is being worked toward; there is no closer configuration this rehearses. **Wide binaries are not failed close binaries**, they are the commonest stable outcome the galaxy produces. **Support swapping:** W Ursae Majoris contact binaries overflow into a single shared convective envelope, and energy crossing it from the larger star to the smaller holds both within about *200 K* of each other despite a mass ratio near 0.3 — the smaller star running far hotter than its own mass could sustain, because capacity is redistributed by the configuration they are in together. And the complication that keeps it honest, because support swapping is not free: in Algol systems mass flows one way, and the star that was originally more massive gave away so much of itself that it ended up the *less* massive, less luminous, apparently less-developed one. Crawford resolved that paradox in 1955 — **the one that looks behind is the one that gave**, its donation written down as apparent deficiency. **Deep pressure:** the locution with the bluntest name, and nothing fuses unsqueezed. A star simply *is* hydrostatic equilibrium, the balance between gravity pulling in and pressure pushing out, held for billions of years; the solar core sits near 2.4 × 10^16^ Pa, over 200 billion atmospheres. Take the crushing away and there is no carbon, no oxygen, no iron, no calcium in the bone. **The pressure is not a condition the star endures on the way to making elements — the pressure is the manufacturing process.** **Penguin pebbling:** the one most readily filed as sweet-but-slight, and the one with the sharpest physics behind it. Classical planetesimal accretion could not finish a giant-planet core before the gas disk dissipated — the big collisions were simply too slow, and without a core in time there is no atmosphere to capture. Pebble accretion, named by Lambrechts and Johansen in 2012, gets there in 10⁴–10⁶ years, because millimetre-scale pebbles are aerodynamically braked and captured across nearly the whole Hill sphere. The small offerings did not supplement the big collisions; the big collisions could not do the job and the small offerings could. **For those cores, the small offering is not a lesser route — it is the route.** It even stops itself: past ten to twenty Earth masses the core opens a pressure bump and the pebbles are halted before arrival, so even the sky knows when someone has had enough pebbles. Then the turn, printed on the face of the zine rather than buried in a footnote, because five-for-five is *exactly* the shape of a claim that circulates for being tidy rather than true. **The physics does not justify the love.** The universe is not signing anything off, and if it were, its approval would be worthless — a universe that could approve could also decline. This is the same defect the site caught in a sentence of its own: *the universe loves you for it* makes the star stuff the **reason**, and a reason can be revoked, where *love you down to your star stuff* measures how far the loving reaches rather than why it is given. *Down to is not because of.* Amythest Schaber originated the five; Stimpunks popularized them and put them into currency from 2022, substituting *locutions* for *languages*; the term *penguin pebbling* reached us later, through Lindsey; and Helen Edgar of Autistic Realms has developed the framework furthest, including the Penguin Pebbling game built with Ryan Boren. Eight spreads, five diagrams. A rhyme, not a proof.

### [What Looks Like Chaos](https://starstuff.earth/what-looks-like-chaos-zine.html)

**Details**

Nest building is the textbook example of pure instinct — a bird constructs a thing it has never seen, without instruction, and gets it right the first time, so the nest must be something the bird *contains* rather than something it works out. That claim is falsifiable, because a template predicts one specific thing: **sameness**, between individuals and within one individual across attempts. Somebody went and measured whether the sameness is there. Southern Botswana, seven colour-ringed male Southern Masked weavers, territories searched daily so a nest was found at the moment it was started; camcorders square to the branch, three nests filmed per bird — *twenty-one nests* — and the footage coded by a researcher blind to which bird and which date. During the first phase of construction **not one measured behaviour was repeatable within a male**: not the direction he carried the grass, not how long he wove, not which quadrant he favoured, not how often he lost hold. Then the individuality: two birds consistently pushed the first end of a blade into the right side of the nest, two consistently into the left, three had no side at all — and **pooled across all seven, the bias vanished** (*G* = 0.06, *p* = 0.81). Personal handedness with no species handedness underneath it, which is precisely what a template cannot produce. And they *improved*: across a season each male dropped grass less often as he built more nests (*p* \< 0.01), while the birds with the most consistent personal style finished the ring phase fastest (*R*² = 0.25) — a way of one's own was not an eccentricity to be corrected, it was the thing that made him quick. Not a template and not the environment: a tradition. Set beside all of this is David Gray-Hammond's account of his own room — the diffuser, the books read several at once, the nothing-tidied-away because some days end in a crash rather than a decision — and his sentence about it: *to most, it appears that I live in a state of chaos… I know where every item I need resides.* The refusals are printed on the zine's face rather than left implied. Not permission from nature, because a universe that could approve could also decline, and *down to is not because of*. Not instinct versus learning. Not a clever-animal story, since the authors themselves attribute the improvement to increasing dexterity and say any role for cognition still needs demonstrating. And the honest one: **not every mess is a nest** — burnout collapses the architecture, and the ruins look from the doorway exactly like the curated version. What the science kills is narrower and sturdier for being narrow: the inference that **an order you cannot read is not an order**. Two centuries of confident observers looked at a nest and saw a machine executing, and were describing the limits of watching while calling it a fact about the bird. **Nesting** is David Gray-Hammond's; **lilipadding** is **Tanya Adkin and David Gray-Hammond**'s, with Adkin's name first on both pieces that set it out — a first draft credited it to him alone, and the colophon says so. ### [Thirteen Changes](https://starstuff.earth/thirteen-changes-zine.html)

**Details**

Every kid who loved **Brontosaurus** got told *actually, it’s Apatosaurus*, and the lesson attached was that the enthusiasm was the amateur part. Here is the paper trail. ### [Even Fallen Light Glows](https://starstuff.earth/even-fallen-light-glows-zine.html)

**Details**

Three texts and one fungus. Neruda’s untitled poem in *El mar y las campanas* (1973) says that if each day falls inside each night there is a well “where clarity is imprisoned,” and instructs you to *sit on the rim of the well of darkness and fish for fallen light with patience* (William O’Daly’s translation, *The Sea and the Bells*, Copper Canyon, 1988, pp. 94–95 — read at the printed page, because every online copy is an aggregator). Read the grammar and the poem has quietly handed out **two roles**: the one who fishes, and the light that fell. **Ryan Boren’s rewrite, made in deep Autistic burnout, moves the speaker into the well** — *the rim is distant, no casting can reach, patience broke* — and the discovery inside it is structural rather than emotional: sitting on a rim with patience is work you can do, being the light at the bottom is not work at all, and once you are the light *every instruction in the poem is addressed to somebody else.* **Helen Edgar answered in six syllables: even fallen light glows.** What makes the reply hold is what it refuses — it does not say the rim is closer, that patience returns, or that you will be reached. The end of the reaction is the beginning of the reaction. And in the *Armillaria* lineage the part that glows is the **mycelium and rhizomorphs**, threads inside the wood, while the mushrooms are consistently non-luminescent: the showy reproductive part is the dark one. Emission runs **continuously, on a temperature-compensated circadian clock** (Oliveira et al., *Curr. Biol.* 25:964–968, 2015), so nothing about it is conditional on a receiver — it does not brighten for company and does not stop when the forest is empty, which is nearly always. **The honest centre is spread nine: nobody knows what it is for.** Lit mushroom models draw more beetles, flies, bugs and wasps than unlit ones, which is real evidence for spore dispersal — and it explains a cap, not the mycelium and lower stipe, which make no spores at all. A live rival says the luciferin is an antioxidant and the light is a byproduct of handling reactive oxygen while dismantling lignin. The review states outright that a non-adaptive, accidental origin **cannot yet be excluded**. Spread ten is the guardrail: bioluminescence arose **once**, about 160 mya, and has been *lost or inactivated* in lineage after lineage since — so keeping the glow is not an achievement and losing it is the commonest thing that ever happened to it. A zine that says *even fallen light glows* is one sentence away from *so you had better be glowing*, and the phylogeny is what blocks it. The turn: if you are the fallen light, glowing is not a task you are behind on, and the casting is somebody else’s work — which is where solidarity stops being a warm feeling and becomes a **division of labour**. Helen’s constellation is what sits at the rim. Refusals on the face: **not** that the log is glad it fell; **not** that burnout is secretly productive, because decay is not a chrysalis and burnout is damage somebody’s conditions caused; **not** that you must glow to be worth keeping; **not** that the glow is a signal so try harder to be seen; **not** a promise that you will be reached; **not** nature proving anything about a nervous system; and **not** the rewrite corrected — both poems are on the page, unedited, in order. The consolation is deliberately thin, because a thicker one would be a lie to somebody whose patience has broken. Twelve spreads, one diagram. A rhyme, not a proof.

### [Your Shadow Is the Centre](https://starstuff.earth/your-shadow-is-the-centre-zine.html)

Zine No. 69 · Stimpunks Zines

*On the bow that no two people can share, the man who proved it in 1267 with an astrolabe and no theory at all, and why being unable to show someone a real thing was never a reason to doubt it.*

A rainbow is a circle centred on the shadow of your own head, so there are as many bows as there are observers — and Roger Bacon used that un-shareability as his *evidence*, not his problem.

**Details**

The oldest correct thing anybody wrote down about a rainbow is not about colour and not about water. **It is about where you are standing.** Around 1267, in the experimental-science part of the *Opus Majus*, Roger Bacon took an astrolabe outside, measured the height of the sun against the height of a bow, and reported that a rainbow can never stand more than **42 degrees** above the horizon. He then wrote something far stranger, and wrote it as a finding: *“From these facts we learn by experiment that there are as many bows as there are observers.”* He gave the test, too — two people look north at the bow, one walks west and the bow goes west with him, the other walks east and *his* bow goes east. Hence *“it is impossible for two observers to see one and the same bow, although an ignorant person does not grasp this fact”*; and the reason, which is this zine’s title: *“the shadow of each observer divides the arc of the rainbow into two equal parts.”* He put the apex of his cone *at the eye* and ran its axis to *“the nadir of the sun”* — the antisolar point — and **he did all of this with no theory of refraction whatsoever**, forty years before anyone described what happens inside a drop and three hundred and seventy before Descartes could calculate it. Roland Stull’s *Practical Meteorology* says the same things now: rainbows are *“centered on the antisolar point, which is the point corresponding to the shadow of your head or camera,”* and *“different observers see different rainbows caused by different light rays interacting with different raindrops”* — red edge 42.7°, violet ~40°, n = 1.3315. But the spread that earns the piece is the **inversion**: Bacon did not treat the privacy of the bow as a puzzle to explain away, he treated it as data, and *ran controls* in 1267. A lit cloud takes a colour that *“appears the same to different observers, nor does it follow the motion of the observer”*; a fish or a stick seen through water is displaced but *“does not follow the observer if he recedes”*; the bow follows you at the speed of *“a man running or riding as fast as possible.”* Three phenomena, one question, one odd answer — and from the odd answer he concluded that the bow must be made of rays that bounce. He landed on reflection alone and was wrong, or badly incomplete; the method was right and the geometry survived into a textbook you can download today. **The feature that makes a rainbow impossible to hand to anybody is the feature that told us what a rainbow is.** Then the harder claims. Les Cowley: *“Rainbows possess neither distance nor linear dimension”* — it behaves as though at infinity and *“cannot be touched, tickled, walked around or driven through”*; a direction, not a place. Your two pupils sit 63 mm apart, each the apex of its own cone collecting its own drops, so **you are not looking at one rainbow with two eyes** — which closes the standard exit from our own umwelt argument in No. 15, because here the perceivers are identical and *position alone is sufficient*. And the conditions: sun behind you, rain ahead, unobstructed, sun under 42° or the whole ring sinks below the horizon — Bacon worked the arithmetic for the latitude of Paris (41°12′ at equinox noon) and for a latitude near the Tropic of Cancer, where *“no rainbow can be formed at any noon of the entire year.”* Rain falling, sun blazing, eyes perfect, and no bow: nothing wrong with the observer, and the arrangement is adjustable. The turn: in the 759 years since Bacon wrote it down, **nobody has ever concluded that rainbows are not real because they cannot be shown to a second person.** No one demands corroboration. That courtesy is not extended evenly, and *if it were real you could show me* has a four-hundred-page textbook counterexample in the sky twice a month. Six refusals on the face, the first being the one that matters: *not* that everybody sees it differently so who’s to say — **different bow, same physics**, 42.7° for everyone, always, and the most private phenomenon here is also one of the most rigidly law-bound. Also not a claim on the Pride rainbow, which already means something specific in our house. Two claims are marked on the page as ours rather than anybody’s measurement. Eleven spreads, six figures, a “Not:” refusals set. **A rhyme, not a proof.**

### [Nothing Is Pushing](https://starstuff.earth/nothing-is-pushing-zine.html)

Zine No. 71 · Stimpunks Zines

*On escape velocity as a coasting number, the places inside a well that cost nothing to sit in, and a telescope built so that it *cannot brake*.*

Escape velocity is defined for a trajectory with no engine running — and it falls as you rise. Two of the five Lagrange points cost nothing at all to occupy, and which two depends on the system, never on the occupant.

**Details**

**Escape velocity is a coasting number, and almost every use of it drops the condition that makes it true.** The 11.186 km/s quoted for Earth is v~esc~ = √(2GM/r), and it is defined for a *ballistic* trajectory — the speed you need if nothing is pushing you afterwards. Two things follow that the motivational reading gets backwards. First, the figure is **local**: it falls as the square root of one over distance, so it is 7.91 km/s at two Earth radii and 5.59 at four. The requirement is not a property of what you are leaving; it is a property of where you are asking from. Second, it says nothing whatever about anything under thrust, which is not a loophole but the scope of the definition. **Dawn is the demonstration.** On 5 September 2012 it left Vesta not by jumping from the floor but by climbing on ion thrust until the bar had come down to meet it — JPL’s own words, *“spiral away as gently as it arrived”*; Marc Rayman’s, *“Thrust is engaged, and we are now climbing away from Vesta atop a blue-green pillar of xenon ions.”* The zine is careful to say on the spread, not only in the colophon, that Dawn *did* eventually cross the local threshold; the claim is that it never had to make the jump from the bottom. That threshold is **μ~G~ = (1 − √(23/27))/2 = 0.0385208965…**, the smaller root of 27μ(1−μ) = 1, and here the zine performs a *precedence correction*: it is usually called Routh’s value after his 1875 paper, and Bruno Sicardy (CMDA 107, 2010) says it *“should actually be called Gascheau’s value in view of the precedence of his work”* — Gabriel Gascheau, professor at Toulouse, published in Paris in **1843**, thirty-plus years earlier. Seven refusals, including that the burst is not available on request (participants describe *“a literal paralysis”*), that *you’re not stuck, you’re just orbiting* is not an accommodation, and that gravity is not the villain — the well is also why there is an atmosphere. One widely-quoted number was hunted, not sourced primarily, and **deliberately left out**. Twelve spreads, seven figures. **A rhyme, not a proof.**

### [Measured Against a Tree](https://starstuff.earth/measured-against-a-tree-zine.html)

Desiccation tolerance is ancestral. The vascular plants gave it up to get tall and kept it only in their seeds, and some went back for it. The deficit list was accurate line by line, and measuring the wrong thing.

**Details**

**Take a tree as the definition of a plant and a moss fails at six points.** No roots — rhizoids anchor and barely absorb. No xylem or phloem. No way to lift water, so no height. No control of its own moisture, because its tissue simply equilibrates with the air. And no photosynthesis at all while dry. Every line is true, and the zine keeps the whole list intact to the last spread. Botany has proper names for the split: a moss is **poikilohydric**, its hydration tracking the surrounding humidity, where a vascular plant is **homoiohydric** and holds an interior against the outside — at the price of roots, xylem, a waxy cuticle and thousands of controllable stomata, with a failure mode that is not a pause but death. *Drying is a state a tree cannot occupy.* **Then the finding that inverts the list.** Proctor, Oliver, Wood, Alpert, Stark, Cleavitt & Mishler, reviewing desiccation tolerance in bryophytes in *The Bryologist* 110(4), 2007, record it as *a primitive character of land plants, lost in the course of evolution of the homoiohydric vascular-plant shoot system, but retained in spores, pollen and seeds, and re-evolved in the vegetative tissues of vascular resurrection plants.* The moss did not fail to acquire the tree’s solution; **the tree discarded the moss’s** — kept it for the one job where nothing else would do, so every seed in a packet is doing what a moss does, and some lineages later evolved their way back to it. **What drying actually is gets stated without comfort.** The cytoplasm enters a glassy state in which molecular mobility is severely reduced; a 2013 *Journal of Experimental Botany* paper on *Syntrichia ruralis* is titled for the result, *“Evidence for the absence of enzymatic reactions in the glassy state.”* It is not idling. Friction stills the air against every surface, and the layer keeps what it holds — heat, water vapour, and over a rotting log up to ten times ambient carbon dioxide. Being small is the entry requirement, not a shortfall. But the layer is not merely found: *friction makes it*, so the long narrow upright leaves, the dense hairs and reflective tips and minuscule spines all deepen it. **The greater the resistance, the deeper the boundary layer — and the larger the boundary layer, the larger the moss can be.** A loop, not a niche: the moss builds the habitat that then permits it to be bigger, and mosses accordingly run from millimetre crusts to ten-centimetre wefts, a range Kimmerer likens to a blueberry bush and a redwood, tracking *the layer* rather than the moss. And layers nest — a rock’s sits under the forest’s umbrella, so the tree that wins every contest for light is also the reason a moss can be large. *That argues against this zine’s own title, which is why it is on the spread and in the closing line.* One more inversion: the green cushion is the **gametophyte**, the generation vascular plants reduced to a few cells in a pollen grain, and the little stalk above it is a periscope whose length correlates with the layer’s depth — the dependent generation is the one built to leave. Closing on *Sphagnum*: peatlands, ~3% of land, holding roughly twice the carbon of all the world’s forest *biomass* — the careful wording, kept because the loose one travels further. Six refusals, including that moss is not secretly tougher, that *small is beautiful* is not on offer, that resilience is never a demand, and that a mechanism recovering in an hour is not a promise made to anyone about burnout. Twelve spreads, three figures. Developed after Helen Edgar’s *Mossy Minds & Monotropism*. **A rhyme, not a proof** — the list was true; the ruler was a tree.

Ice sublimes constantly, and the rate depends on the shape of the surface: a convex grain surface gives up more vapour, a concave hollow gives up less. And the failure is not in the necks: it is in the weak layer underneath, which the bonding above cannot reach or repair. Six refusals, including that this is not a metaphor extracted from someone’s cosmology, that the physics does not validate the reading, and that community does not fix conditions. Twelve spreads, four figures. **A rhyme, not a proof.**

Many of us leave the gathering and go sit with the dogs. The usual explanation is that dogs are more empathetic than people. The evidence will not carry that — and what it will carry is better.

**Details**

**Stress has an outgassing, and a nose can find it through a sample jar.** Wilson, Campbell, Petzel and Reeve ran a double-blind, two-phase, three-alternative forced-choice trial: four dogs — Treo, Winnie, Fingal and Soot — presented with combined breath and sweat taken from thirty-six people before and after three minutes of counting backwards aloud from 9000 in seventeens. Stress was confirmed independently, and eleven of the fifty-three people recruited were *excluded for not having a sufficient stress response at all*. The dogs picked the stress sample in **675 of 720 trials**, and in **34 of 36** on first exposure to a stranger they had no history with. The authors’ own conclusion is about chemistry, not sympathy: an acute stress response changes the volatile organic compounds coming off breath and sweat. A volatile organic compound is metabolic exhaust, not a performance — you do not rehearse it, time it, or get better at it. **Which places the dog on the other channel.** A room grades a narrow authored band: face, gaze, prosody, turn-taking latency. That band is what masking works on, at enormous cost, and it is the band the dogs never had. So the quiet room is not restful because the dog understands us better; it is restful because *the dog is reading the one channel we are not required to manage* — and the signal everybody agreed was missing turns out to be present, legible and reliable at 93.75 per cent as soon as somebody stops looking in the band we spend our lives performing. **The honesty is printed rather than kept back.** The sample is four dogs and the authors call it proof of principle in their own words; the dogs were *trained to alert* and rewarded for it; and discrimination is not interpretation, which is not care. Nagasawa’s famous oxytocin gaze loop is on no spread here, because published commentaries flag confounds in it and a contested result is not a floor. Worse for the warm reading: Parr-Cortes and colleagues found eighteen dogs exposed to an unfamiliar person’s stress odour became significantly *less* likely to approach an ambiguous food location — possible risk-reduction rather than comfort. The dog beside us may be being careful, not soothing. It closes on **Carol Millman**, an Autistic professional dog trainer, who answered “is ABA dog training for children?” by pointing out that the comparison is unfair to the trainers: meet every fundamental need before any conditioning, relieve the fear rather than train away its display, and know that a dog taught not to growl is a *time bomb dog*, because you removed the warning and not the fear. That professional standard is higher than the one applied behaviour analysis has held for us. Thirteen spreads, two figures, five refusals. A rhyme, not a proof.

How this collection works

## What holds these together

Every piece here works the same way. Take one fact that is settled and checkable, follow it honestly, and find that the fact *itself* already contains the claim about belonging. The calcium in your bones really was forged in stars. Mitochondria really were free-living bacteria that never left. The early universe really did need tiny unevennesses to make anything at all — a perfectly uniform cosmos would have stayed a dead fog forever.

That is the difference between physics as argument and physics as decoration. In this collection **the fact is the argument**, not an illustration of a point already decided somewhere else. If the science turned out otherwise, the piece would have to change. That is the test, and it is the reason this register earns the trust it asks for.

## Sorted by register, not by date

The first ten zines founded this method, and seven of them are here. But **thirty-five of these pieces were made much later** — *The Nearest Body*, *No Universal Now*, *The Same Water* and *Not a Line* among them — and still belong here, because they do exactly the same thing. Tidal force really does fall off with the cube of distance, which is why the Moon runs the tide even though the Sun out-masses it some 27 million times. Clocks really do run at different rates by speed and gravity, which is why your phone's GPS has to correct for it. Water really does hold and then flip at a threshold rather than stiffening by degrees.

Membership here is decided by **mechanism, not chronology**. A piece is in this collection if the fact is doing the work. The numbers still say when each one was made — see [Cosmic Connections](https://starstuff.earth/cosmic-connections.html) on why we kept them that way.

Not

**Not the naturalistic fallacy.** The atoms do not owe us this conclusion. We are noticing a rhyme, not collecting a proof, and *The Lines We Drew* is in this collection precisely to name the trap: the bell curve was recruited to make “normal” look like physics, and eugenics followed. Dressing a human value as a law of nature is the exact move we refuse.

**Not interdisciplinary as tourism** — borrowing a physics word for its glamour and leaving the physics behind.
