1543–1838 · the long objection

The objection that was perfectly good

If the Earth really moves around the Sun, then over six months your viewpoint shifts by the whole width of that orbit — and nearby stars should visibly slide against the distant ones.

They don't. Not to the naked eye, not to Tycho Brahe's superb instruments, not to anyone for nearly three hundred years after Copernicus. So the Earth does not move.

That was not stupidity. It was valid reasoning from careful data, and it is the strongest empirical objection the heliocentric model ever faced. The answer — the stars are simply much, much further away than that — sounded like special pleading, because the distances required were, by the standards of the time, absurd.

And there was a second trap underneath, which is the part that should make anyone nervous. Through a telescope, stars show small discs. Take those discs as real and you can compute a physical size — and at the distances parallax would require, stars come out grotesquely, impossibly enormous. Tycho ran that calculation and rejected the conclusion.

The discs were not there. They are an artefact of light passing through a finite aperture — the instrument drawing its own signature on the sky and handing it back as data.the trap under the trap

So the chain opens on a failure with three parts, all of them still with us: absence of evidence read as evidence of absence; a conclusion rejected because its implications felt too large; and an instrument's own artefact mistaken for a property of the thing observed.

What eventually broke it was not a better argument. It was somebody measuring the shift.

Königsberg · 1838

Somebody measured the shift

Friedrich Bessel picked 61 Cygni because it moved. Not much — but its motion across the sky was unusually fast, and fast usually means near.

With a Fraunhofer heliometer, on every clear night he could get, he tracked it against two neighbouring stars. In 1838 he published the result: a parallax of 0.3136 arcseconds.

That is an angle it is worth trying to feel. A two-centimetre coin, seen from thirteen kilometres away. That is the entire wobble on which the first known distance to a star was built. It gave about 10.4 light years; the modern figure is near 11.4.

He was narrowly first. Thomas Henderson had Alpha Centauri and Friedrich Struve had Vega at almost the same moment. Three people, three stars, one method, within months.

Three hundred years of "we looked and saw nothing" ended the moment the instrument got good enough to see a coin at thirteen kilometres. The stars had been moving the whole time.what actually settles an argument

And here is the detail this collection has to stop and mark. Bessel did this at Königsberg — the same man, at the same observatory, who established the personal equation: the finding that observers differ from one another systematically, and that the right response is to publish a constant for each of them and correct the data rather than correct the observer.

Twice, from opposite directions, the same person demonstrated that knowledge requires a located, particular observer. Once by needing their displacement. Once by needing their difference.

The geometry · and what it forbids

No baseline, no depth

Strip parallax down to what it actually is, because the whole zine is in the geometry.

You look at a star. You wait six months, which carries you to the far side of Earth's orbit. You look again. The star appears to have moved a little against the far background, and from that angle and the width of your orbit you get the distance.

The width of your orbit is called the baseline. It is not a nuisance in the measurement, not a source of error to be minimised, not contamination from the observer's situation.

The observer's displacement is the instrument. Take it away and you have not made the measurement purer. You have made it impossible.the sentence this zine turns on

Consider what a genuine view from nowhere would be. No location, therefore no second location, therefore no baseline, no angle, no distance. An observer at no position sees a flat sky and cannot tell a nearby star from a far one. Not "with difficulty." At all. The god's-eye view is, in the one domain where we can check it exactly, the blindest possible position.

And the reverse holds too, which is the constructive half. The more your positions differ, the better your measurement gets. That is why space astrometry beats ground-based: a wider, better-known baseline. Difference between viewpoints is not noise to be averaged out. It is signal, and it is the only signal there is.

Everything above it

Everything rests on the bottom rung

Parallax only reaches so far. Past that, astronomy does something audacious: it builds a ladder, and calibrates each rung against the one below.

Rung one is geometry — parallax, an observer moving, no assumptions required.

Rung two is the period–luminosity relation. Certain variable stars pulse at a rate that tracks their true brightness, so if you can read the period you know the intrinsic luminosity, and comparing that to how bright it looks gives distance. Calibrated by rung one.

Rung three reaches further still with exploding stars of standardisable brightness. Calibrated by rung two. And so on out to the expansion of the universe itself.

Every distance in cosmology — every one — is ultimately anchored to somebody standing in a particular place and moving to another.what the ladder is bolted to

Which means the ladder inherits the bottom rung's fate. Move the calibration and every rung above it moves. This is not hypothetical: recalibrations of the variable-star rung have historically resedimented the entire extragalactic distance scale, and disagreements about that calibration sit near the centre of live arguments about the expansion rate today.

Hold that structure, because the rest of the zine is about a different ladder with the same property. When the bottom rung is a person in a position, and you have pretended there is no person and no position, everything built on top inherits the error and none of it knows.

Harvard College Observatory · 1895–1912

The woman not permitted to observe

Rung two of the ladder — the one everything above it stands on — was built by a person who was not allowed to use the telescope.

Henrietta Swan Leavitt joined Harvard College Observatory in 1895 as one of the women hired as computers: paid by the hour, assigned tasks rather than choosing them, employed to measure photographic plates that men had exposed.

On those plates she found and measured the periods of roughly 1,700 variable stars in the Magellanic Clouds. And she noticed the thing nobody had: because everything in the Small Magellanic Cloud sits at essentially the same distance from us, differences in apparent brightness there are differences in actual brightness. That single observation converts a pattern into a ruler.

The result — the period–luminosity relation, the Leavitt Law — appeared in Harvard College Observatory Circular 173 in 1912, titled "Periods of 25 Variable Stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud."

It was published under the name of the observatory's director, Edward Pickering. It opens by noting that the statement "has been prepared by Miss Leavitt."HCO Circular 173, 1912

Prepared by. The measurement of the universe rests on rung two, and rung two is credited in a subordinate clause.

Now put that next to the argument of the last three spreads. Parallax says knowledge requires an observer in a position. Leavitt was denied the position of observer and made the discovery anyway — from a chair, with a magnifying glass, on somebody else's plates. She had a standpoint. The institution simply declined to record that it was hers.

Two of those come from No. 39; one is on the previous spread. We were not looking for them. They turned up because chains make you read the primary sources, and the primary sources say who was actually in the room.

And this is not a detour from the argument — it is the argument, one turn further in. The god trick does not merely hide the observer. It decides which observers are permitted to exist in the record. The unmarked category gets the byline; the marked body gets the subordinate clause and does the work.

Held carefully. Three cases is a pattern in this collection, not a demonstrated law about science. We are not claiming a mechanism that produced all three, and we have not gone looking for counter-examples — chains that turned up no such erasure would not have prompted a spread. What is documented is each case; the reading across them is ours, and this note is here so the reading cannot be mistaken for the evidence.

1988 · the name for it

The god trick

In 1988 Donna Haraway gave the fantasy its name, in an essay about vision, and about who gets to be looked at.

"I would like to insist on the embodied nature of all vision and so reclaim the sensory system that has been used to signify a leap out of the marked body and into a conquering gaze from nowhere. This is the gaze that mythically inscribes all the marked bodies, that makes the unmarked category claim the power to see and not be seen, to represent while escaping representation."Haraway, "Situated Knowledges," Feminist Studies 14(3), 1988, p. 581

Read the mechanism slowly, because it is precise. There are marked bodies — visible, particular, available for study. And there is an unmarked category, which is not neutral but simply unexamined: it holds the power to see and not be seen, to describe others while never appearing in the description.

Haraway calls the whole manoeuvre a god trick — "the god trick of seeing everything from nowhere" — and is blunt about its status: "that view of infinite vision is an illusion, a god trick."

She is not describing something exotic. She is describing the default voice of a methods section: subjects were observed. Observed by whom, from where, with what stake in the result? The sentence is constructed so the question cannot be asked.

And note where the essay is set, because it is not incidental. Haraway's examples are satellites, space probes, planetary imaging, the “brilliant space platforms of the powerful.” She even quotes a magazine's epigraph on the infinitesimal: “The stuff of stars has come alive.” The critique of the view from nowhere was written, from the start, in the vocabulary of looking at the sky.

The line that usually gets dropped

"Only partial perspective promises objective vision"

Haraway is routinely read as an opponent of objectivity. She is the opposite, and she says so in a sentence she flags as the moral of the essay.

"The moral is simple: only partial perspective promises objective vision. … Feminist objectivity is about limited location and situated knowledge, not about transcendence and splitting of subject and object. It allows us to become answerable for what we learn how to see."Haraway, 1988, p. 583

Not objectivity is impossible. Not all views are equal. Partiality is the precondition of objectivity — and what you gain by having a location is the ability to be answerable, which a view from nowhere structurally cannot be. You cannot check the work of an observer with no address.

Now set that beside the geometry from spread four.

They are the same claim in two vocabularies. In astronomy it is a theorem you can check with trigonometry: the measurement is constituted by the observer's position, and pretending otherwise destroys the measurement. In epistemology it is an argument. The astronomy does not prove the epistemology — but it does demonstrate that the shape of the claim is not mystical, because there is at least one domain where it is exactly, boringly true.

Marked contested, and here is what is contested. Haraway never mentions parallax, and we are not claiming she had it in mind. The parallel is ours. What makes it more than a pun is that both claims have the same structure — position is not a contaminant of the measurement, it is a component of it — and that Haraway independently reaches for astronomical imagery throughout. What it is not is a proof: a theorem in trigonometry cannot settle an argument in epistemology, and if this spread reads as though it does, it has overstepped.

The guard rail · in her own words

Relativism is also a god trick

The obvious misreading of everything so far is therefore all views are equally valid. Haraway blocks that exit herself, in the same essay, and more sharply than her critics do.

"Relativism is a way of being nowhere while claiming to be everywhere equally. The 'equality' of positioning is a denial of responsibility and critical inquiry. Relativism is the perfect mirror twin of totalization in the ideologies of objectivity; both deny the stakes in location, embodiment, and partial perspective; both make it impossible to see well. Relativism and totalization are both 'god tricks' promising vision from everywhere and nowhere equally and fully."Haraway, 1988, p. 584

That is as complete a refusal of "it's all subjective" as anyone has written, and it comes from the person the phrase is most often blamed on.

The geometry says the same thing, and it is worth spelling out because it is not obvious. Two well-known positions give you a distance. Infinitely many positions, none of them recorded, give you nothing at all — you cannot form a baseline out of viewpoints you refuse to specify. Relativism is not many baselines. It is the refusal to state one, which is the same epistemic position as the view from nowhere wearing different clothes.

She is equally careful about the standpoints of the subjugated, which she says are preferred but explicitly not "innocent": they are preferred "because in principle they are least likely to allow denial of the critical and interpretive core of all knowledge," and they remain subject to critical re-examination like any other. And: "not just any partial perspective will do."

The unmarked sample

Here is the god trick with numbers on it.

In 2010, Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan asked a question nobody had bothered to ask systematically: who are the people in the studies? The answer, across the top journals of the behavioural sciences:

They named the sample WEIRD — Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic — and made the finding that turns it from awkward into serious: on many measures these subjects are not merely unrepresentative but frequent outliers compared with the rest of the species.

Notice what the problem actually is, because "unrepresentative sample" undersells it. The results were not published as facts about American undergraduates. They were published as facts about human beings — the population went unstated because the researchers' own position was the unmarked category.

A methods section that does not say where you are standing is not neutral. It is a claim to be standing nowhere, and it is always false.the god trick, operationalised

And this is the ladder problem again, exactly. Rung one was a narrow, unstated sample. Everything built on top inherited it, and none of it knew.

Where it lands on us

Research done on us

Autism research is close to a controlled experiment in what the god trick costs, because for most of its history the observers and the observed were disjoint sets.

Look at what that arrangement produced, using findings this collection has already walked. Outcome measures chosen from outside: the endpoint inherited from 1987 was indistinguishability — how much like everyone else the child seemed — a target nobody standing on the inside would have written down (No. 38). Harms not measured: 150 intervention studies, 11 mentioning adverse events (No. 40). You do not ask about harms you cannot imagine, and you cannot imagine harms you have never been on the receiving end of.

And the finding that only exists because somebody moved. Damian Milton's double empathy problem (2012) reframes the communication breakdown between Autistic and non-Autistic people as mutual and relational — a mismatch between two positions rather than a deficit inside one of them.

That is a parallax result. It is invisible from one position and obvious from two, and getting it required an Autistic researcher — an observer at the other end of the baseline.why the sample includes the analyst

Decades of research described the deficit as a property of the Autistic person, the way Tycho described stellar discs as a property of the stars. In both cases the finding was partly an artefact of the apparatus — and in both cases you could only see that by observing from somewhere else.

Which is what participatory research is for. Not courtesy, and not a legitimacy ritual. A second position on the baseline, which is the only way anyone has ever measured depth.

The correction

Publish your coordinates

Two exits are closed. You cannot remove the observer — that destroys the measurement. You cannot declare all views equal — Haraway refuses it, and so does the geometry.

What is left is the answer Bessel gave twice, and it is embarrassingly practical: state where you are standing, and publish the constant.

In 1838 that meant recording the baseline: which two points the observation was made from, how far apart, how accurately known. In the personal equation it meant recording the observer: this one runs 0.2 seconds late, here is the number, correct the data and keep the person.

The research equivalents are unglamorous and already invented:

Say who the sample was, in the claim and not only in the appendix. Say who the researchers were and what they had at stake. Put people with the experience in the design, not the debrief. Choose outcome measures with the people who will be measured. Report harms. Name the coordinates the work was done from.the baseline, in a methods section

Every one of those is usually filed under ethics or inclusion. They belong under method. A positionality statement is not a confession or a disclaimer — it is a published baseline, and it does the same job Bessel's constants did: it makes the reading correctable by somebody who was not there.

And it is the reason "nothing about us without us" is an epistemic claim before it is a political one. A study of us that contains none of us has one position. Whatever else is wrong with it, it cannot measure depth.

Marked contested. The synthesis is ours: Bessel did not think of the personal equation as a template for research ethics, and Haraway does not cite him. What we are claiming is a shared form — make the observer's position explicit and correctable rather than invisible or irrelevant — not a historical descent. And the practical claim underneath, that participatory methods produce better findings rather than only fairer process, is an empirical question that deserves its own evidence and does not get to lean on the analogy.

The turn

The sky is not flat

There is a real answer to how far away 61 Cygni is. It is about 11.4 light years, and nothing in this zine softens that.

We got it by knowing exactly where somebody was standing, twice, six months apart, and by publishing both positions so anyone could check. The precision came from the situatedness. It did not survive despite it.

So this is not an argument that science is only perspective, and it cannot be turned into one without contradicting both halves of its evidence — the geometry says a position is required, and Haraway says relativism is a god trick. The view from nowhere is not too rigorous. It is not rigorous enough, because it has thrown away the one thing that makes a measurement checkable: a stated place to have made it from.

Objectivity is not the absence of a position. It is a position you have written down, so that someone standing elsewhere can correct you.what the chain arrives at

And nobody built this road on purpose. Tycho was doing his best with an instrument that lied to him. Pickering ran an observatory whose employment practices he had not invented. The researchers in the WEIRD survey were using the subjects who were available. Each did competent work from an unexamined position — which is exactly the failure the whole chain describes, performed by the people describing it.

Which is the hopeful part, and the reason this collection keeps writing chains instead of villains. An unexamined position is not a moral defect. It is a missing line in the methods section. Those can be written.

Not:that all perspectives are equal. Haraway calls that a god trick in the same essay, and the geometry agrees: unstated positions give you nothing. Two known baselines beat infinite unrecorded ones.
Not:that lived experience is automatically right. Haraway is explicit that subjugated standpoints are "not 'innocent' positions" and stay open to critical examination. Preferred is not the same as unquestionable.
Not:that parallax proves any of this. A theorem in trigonometry cannot settle epistemology. It shows the shape of the claim is real in at least one place. We marked that joint contested.
Not:an attack on measurement. The zine's hero is a man who measured a coin at thirteen kilometres and published his baseline.
