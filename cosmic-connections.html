Cosmic Connections

Three sites, one web. Nothing here was arrived at in a straight line, and none of it is anybody's alone. This is the page about the links — what travels between Stimpunks, More Realms, and Star Stuff, and why the connections are the content rather than the packaging.

Cosmic Connections is Helen Edgar's phrase for what this collection is doing, and it landed because it was already carrying two histories.

The first is Connections, James Burke's BBC series, first broadcast in 1978. Ryan Boren grew up on it, and a good deal of how this collection thinks came from watching it young. Its method is the opposite of the tidy march of great men and eureka moments: Burke's argument is that you cannot understand any piece of the modern world in isolation, because the thing you are holding was assembled out of centuries of unrelated people solving unrelated problems for reasons of their own — profit, boredom, war, faith — none of them aiming at the outcome they produced. An episode starts somewhere medieval and arrives, by way of eight things that have no obvious business with each other, at the object on your desk. Burke went on making the argument across three more series, in 1994, 1997, and 2023.

The second is The Cosmic Connection: An Extraterrestrial Perspective, Carl Sagan's 1973 book — the one where he first put the line in print that this whole collection runs on: we are made of star-stuff. Sagan's connection was between our bodies and the interiors of dead stars. Burke's was between a stirrup and a computer. They turn out to be the same move made at different scales: follow the thread, and the thing that looked separate turns out to be continuous with everything around it.

Connection isn't a metaphor we apply to the material. It is how the material got made — and how we make it.

Three sites, one web

There are three places, and they do different work. Calling them a collaboration is accurate but too flat; what actually happens is traffic.

The Symbioses row is the one that shows the shape best. Helen wrote about Monotropa uniflora — the ghost pipe, which grows no chlorophyll and takes all its carbon second-hand from fungi — as a botanical expression of being monotropic. Monotropa means one turn: the same root as monotropism, the Autistic attention style named by Murray, Lesser and Lawson in 2005. A plant name from Linnaean botany, an attention theory from Autistic researchers, and a mutual-aid ethic about receiving without shame, all sitting on one etymology. Nobody planned that. It was found by following a link.

Why links, and not folders

Stimpunks has kept its knowledge as a web of small linked pieces rather than a hierarchy of folders, on the argument that a web of notes with links between them is far more useful than a fixed hierarchical system — the design principle underneath both Tim Berners-Lee's original proposal for the web and Niklas Luhmann's card-index practice. The payoff is not tidiness. It's that connecting notes makes higher-level concepts appear that nobody wrote down, and connecting those makes larger categories appear in turn. The categories are outputs, not inputs.

That is emergence, which Stimpunks glosses as the way complex systems and patterns arise out of a multiplicity of relatively simple interactions. It is the same principle physics states as more is different — that a large enough collection of parts obeys laws you cannot read off the parts themselves — and the same one adrienne maree brown builds organizing practice from in Emergent Strategy: small actions and connections create complex systems.

This is why the collection is built the way it is — every zine cross-linking its siblings, every term linking back out to the glossary, search that indexes down to the individual spread rather than the page. The link density is the argument. A collection where each piece sat in its own folder would make all the same claims and none of the same connections.

Accumulated, not authored

The part of Burke that stuck hardest is the timescale. His chains run for centuries, and no single link in them is a breakthrough. Each is a small competent adjustment by someone who could not have known what it would join up with. Progress in Connections is not a person; it's an accumulation.

Stimpunks calls this iteration and treats it as the working method rather than a phase: as Cornelius Minor puts it, learning is a series of iterations — not linear, and needing deliberate room made for revision and failure. Which is also why this site publishes its own errors. The changelog and the fact-check ledger are the iteration made visible: what we got wrong, what's right, who caught it. A living document that never shows a correction is just a document making a claim about itself.

Everything is a remix

The phrase is Kirby Ferguson's, from his video series Everything Is a Remix (2010–2012, with a fifth part in 2023) — the argument that copying, transforming and combining is not a failure of originality but the mechanism of creativity itself.

Stimpunks' license page puts the same thing in the house register: “Nothing comes from nowhere. Punk understood this. So did bricoleurs, rhizome thinkers, and every neurodivergent kid who built something strange and new out of whatever was at hand.” That is why everything here is CC BY-SA 4.0 and made to be printed, forked and passed on. A collection that argued for interdependence and then locked its own doors would be arguing against itself.

The refusals

Not:
  • Not a genealogy of geniuses. Burke's whole point is that nobody in the chain saw the end of it, and we are not adding ourselves to a lineage of great minds.
  • Not “everything is connected” as mysticism. Specific links, named and checkable, or it doesn't go on the page.
  • Not interdisciplinary as tourism — borrowing a physics word for its glamour and leaving the physics behind.
  • Not connection as extraction. Crediting the source is the connection; taking the idea and dropping the name is the opposite of one.
  • Not a shared masthead standing in for a shared byline. Where a piece is Helen's, it is hers and says so.
Held honestly

Emergence is a real property of complex systems, and the physics of more is different is well established. It does not follow that any particular web of notes will produce insight, that our three sites are a complex adaptive system in the technical sense, or that link-density guarantees anything. Burke's chains are also a storyteller's selection: he picks the path that arrives somewhere, and the unchosen paths that went nowhere don't get an episode.

We are noticing a rhyme between how knowledge accumulates and how we work, and using it as a method. That's a good reason to link generously. It isn't a proof.

So: follow the links. Read a zine, hit a term you don't know, land in the glossary, come back by a different door than you left. That route through the material is not a failure to organize it. It's the thing itself — and it's why Helen's phrase fits. We are made of star stuff, and so is everything we made this out of.
