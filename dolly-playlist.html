Love Without Cringing

Dolly Parton from 1967 to 2023 — twenty-four songs, and the argument that runs through all of them. The persona was assigned before she chose it. The copyright stayed in her pocket. And the songs went out to everybody, with no test at the door.

A Sound rack · companion to Zine No. 67
One

“Dumb Blonde” was not her joke

Her first hit, in 1967, was a song called “Dumb Blonde,” in which a woman informs a man that being blonde is not the same as being stupid. She was twenty-one. She did not write it.

Curly Putman did. Which means the joke, the persona and the terms of engagement all arrived before she had any say in them, and the first thing the public heard from her was a rebuttal to an accusation nobody had put in writing. She spent the next fifty-nine years working inside that costume, and got so good at it that most people came to believe she had designed it from scratch.

That distinction — assigned, then inhabited, then mastered — is worth holding on to, because it is the honest version of a story usually told as pure self-invention. She was extremely deliberate about the wigs and the nails and the laugh. She was also handed a slot marked blonde girl singer by an industry that had exactly one of those, and the alternative on offer was not a different persona. It was no career.

And what the blonde was doing

Tressie McMillan Cottom’s The Dolly Moment (2021) argues that the costume worked as well as it did because it is aggressively white — that “blonde” operates as an unmarked racial identity, filtering for whiteness without anyone having to name it. That reading sits underneath this whole rack and is not resolved by it. Zine No. 67 gives it a spread rather than a footnote.

Two

The Book Lady, and what was on the envelope

In 1995 she started mailing books to children in Sevier County, Tennessee. Not the poor ones. Not the promising ones. Every one, birth to five, a book a month, until they started school.

Thirty-one years later that is more than 332 million books across five countries, going out at something like three and a half million a month. In all that time, the thing that was never added is a test. No income threshold. No application beyond an address. No reading assessment, no referral, no diagnosis, no waiting list, and no annual review to confirm the child still deserves it.

And the parcel does not say to the parent or guardian of. It carries the child’s own name. A two-year-old who cannot yet read gets post.

Why that is rarer than it sounds

Almost everything else a child needs arrives through a gate — an income threshold, an eligibility determination, a severity cut-off, a screening, a place on a list that can be lost. Each is defended the same way: resources are finite, so they must be targeted. That is true, and it is not the whole sentence. A gate does not only ration; it sorts — and sorting requires somebody to rule on who is worth it. Zine No. 67 spends three spreads on what that costs the people who get through it.

She built it for her father. Robert Lee Parton could not read or write, and his daughter called him one of the wisest men she had ever known — both halves of that at once, which is the entire design. She did not conclude that he lacked something. She concluded that something had failed to reach him.

The programme is Dolly Parton’s Imagination Library, and the children called her the Book Lady. In 2017, at seventy-one, she wrote them an album and handed it the proceeds.

It also puts two songs forty-six years apart into a single argument, which is the reason this section sits in a rack rather than only in the zine. “Coat of Many Colors” (1971) is written from inside the laughing — the child in the handmade coat, mocked for it, insisting she was rich and they could not see it. “Makin’ Fun Ain’t Funny” (2017) is the same playground addressed to the other children, the ones doing the laughing. She had been the kid in the coat; when she finally got a room full of six-year-olds listening, she wrote to the ones on the other side of it.

Three

The copyright in her pocket

In 1974 Elvis Presley wanted to record “I Will Always Love You.” The night before the session, Colonel Tom Parker explained the terms.

By Parton’s account, the rule was that Elvis did not record anything without half the publishing going to Presley’s side. She had written the song. She had published it herself. She said no — and, by her own account, cried all night about it.

I wanted to hear Elvis sing it, and it broke my heart. I cried all night.

Dolly Parton, recalling the 1974 decision

Eighteen years later Whitney Houston recorded it for The Bodyguard and it became one of the best-selling singles ever made, with the songwriting income going where Parton had kept it. The story gets told as business acumen, and it was. But the shape of it is a refusal, not a strategy: somebody powerful asked her to hand over half of a thing she had made, as the price of being allowed in the room, and she declined to pay a toll she did not owe.

That is the same instinct as the mailing label, pointed the other way. She would not put a gate in front of anybody else, and she would not walk through one herself.

And then there is what grew on top of that story.

In July 2021, on Watch What Happens Live, Andy Cohen asked her what the best thing was that she had bought with the Houston royalties. She said she had bought property in what was then a Black neighbourhood in Nashville — Sevier Park — and that it was “the perfect place for me to be considering it was Whitney… I’m going to be down here with her people, who are my people as well.”

It went round the world in days as proof that she had been a friend to Black Nashville decades before it was fashionable. It is still going round: it is in her obituaries this week. And in the part that makes it a story, it does not hold up.

What the property records say

NPR’s Amanda Marie Martínez checked it three weeks after the broadcast. Nashville Planning Department records show Parton acquired the two parcels at 12th Avenue South and Elmwood in 1990 and 1991 — before The Bodyguard was released in 1992. The royalties could not have bought the property. The Washington Post had reported the purchase as 1997; NPR, which had run the feel-good version itself, published the correction and named its own error in the first paragraph.

Two historians told NPR the rest of it. Jessica Wilkerson of West Virginia University put it in a pattern — “[Parton] has a pattern of claiming that when she purchases property, she invests in a place that she’s helping people… It gets trickier to do that with a Black community, where she doesn’t live, she’s not from there, and she’s doing it as a rich white person who can buy up real estate.” Learotha Williams Jr. of Tennessee State University declined the reading altogether: “She invested her money in an area that had a rich Black history, but one that was actively being undermined as a result of gentrification.” Sevier Park is now called 12 South, and is one of the whitest, most expensive and most tourist-driven parts of Nashville.

And one detail we are not going to walk past, because the companion zine is built on the absence of exactly this: around the compound, a large gate was constructed.

None of which touches the copyright. She wrote the song, she refused the toll, she kept the money, and every word of that is documented. What the sequel shows is how quickly a verifiable refusal gets promoted into an unverifiable virtue once an audience wants one — which is the subject of this essay’s last section, arriving five years early. Cottom, interviewed for that same NPR piece, put it plainly: “This isn’t about Dolly. Loving Dolly is a stand-in for how we can remediate our love for the nation.”

Four

She wrote the dark ones too

The received Dolly is relentlessly sunny. The catalogue is not.

“Down from Dover” ends with a stillborn baby. “The Bridge” ends, by implication, with a pregnant woman going over one. “In the Good Old Days (When Times Were Bad)” is a title doing an entire argument’s work — a refusal to let anybody get nostalgic about her childhood on her behalf.

Cottom reads this run as gothic motherhood: country music demanded that a woman be a wife or a mother to be taken seriously, and Parton, who never had children, answered the demand by writing an archive of motherhood as painful and complicated as the real thing. That reading is hers and we are borrowing it with the tag on.

What it means for this rack is simpler. The woman who built the most loving institution in American children’s literacy was not naive about what happens to people. She had written the worst outcomes down, in detail, decades earlier. The optimism was a position taken, not a failure to notice.

Five

What travelled, and what got left

Every song here has been carried somewhere by somebody, and carrying changes things.

“Jolene” left home as a plea to another woman and came back as a meme, a metal riff, a slowed-down internet artefact and, in March 2021, four syllables of public-health messaging sung in a hospital chair. “9 to 5” left as a labour song written on acrylic nails on a film set and is now used to sell productivity software. “I Will Always Love You” left as a goodbye to a business partner and came back as the most famous vocal performance of the 1990s, at which point a great many people assumed Whitney Houston had written it.

This is the ordinary fate of anything that travels well, and it is the reason this rack keeps naming what a song was for when it was made. Not to police the covers — she was famously delighted by them — but because the smoothed version always travels further than the specific one, and the specific one is usually where the argument lives.

The clearest case is the newest

In March 2023, administrators at Heyer Elementary in Waukesha, Wisconsin barred a first-grade class from performing “Rainbowland,” her duet with Miley Cyrus, after reviewing it against a district policy on controversial issues in the classroom. The proposed replacement, “Rainbow Connection,” was initially blocked as well. A song about there being no gate, stopped at a gate — and the mechanism was a policy, applied by people following it correctly.

And that is what the title of this rack is pointing at. Near the end of her autobiography, having just spent two pages on the fact that people will sometimes take your love and give nothing back and are still all there is, she catches herself being sincere in public and declines to apologise for it:

I don’t mean to sound like the inside of a Hallmark card. I do think, though, that it’s time we learned to use the word love without cringing.

Dolly Parton, Dolly: My Life and Other Unfinished Business (1994), pp. 297–298

Cottom opens The Dolly Moment by observing that this is exactly the register the culture normally denigrates — that it took a particular political moment to make earnest sentimentality acceptable coming from her. Both things can be true: the moment made room for it, and she had been working in that register for twenty-seven years before the moment arrived, taking the cost of it in reviews the whole time. The songs are where you can hear her not waiting for permission. Twenty-four of them follow.

  • Not a canonisation. This is a rack about a body of work, made three days after she died, by people who are sad. It is not a verdict, and Cottom’s reading is not answered here.
  • Not bootstraps. The rags-to-riches arc is the most extractable thing in the catalogue and gets used to argue that the ladder is fine because one person climbed it.
  • Not claiming her. She was not one of us. The rack is about how her work treated people, not about recruiting her to a community she did not belong to.
One — Underestimated on purpose · 1967–1970

The costume arrives first, written by somebody else. Listen to what she does inside it almost immediately.

The assigned persona
Dumb Blonde
Dolly Parton · 1967

Her first hit, written by Curly Putman — so the joke was handed to her before she could choose it. A twenty-one-year-old answering a charge nobody had formally made, which turned out to be the job description for the next six decades.

The double standard, named
Just Because I'm a Woman
Dolly Parton · 1968

Her own song, and a much harder one: a woman telling a man that her mistakes are no different from his, and that the accounting only runs one way. In 1968, on country radio, from a new artist.

What the sunny one also wrote
The Bridge
Dolly Parton · 1968

Two minutes long, and it stops mid-scene. A pregnant woman, abandoned, standing where the song began. Nothing is stated and there is no possible second reading. Put this next to the received idea of her.

Gothic motherhood
Down from Dover
Dolly Parton · 1970

A pregnant unmarried woman is sent away to wait for a man who is not coming, and the baby is born dead. Tressie McMillan Cottom reads the whole run of these as Parton answering country music's motherhood demand by making it honest.

Two — Poor is not the same as lacking · 1971–1975

The childhood songs, which are about dignity rather than hardship, and which decline to be inspiring.

The one that says it all
Coat of Many Colors
Dolly Parton · 1971

Her mother sews a coat from rags and tells her the Joseph story while she sews. The other children laugh. The song's whole weight is on the last turn: she was not poor, she was rich, and they could not see it because they had never been taught how to look.

Lightness on purpose
Love Is Like a Butterfly
Dolly Parton · 1974

Included precisely because it is slight. She wrote plenty that was not heavy and never treated the light ones as lesser work — which is a position about what art is for, held consistently for sixty years.

Offered as is
The Bargain Store
Dolly Parton · 1975

A woman describes herself as second-hand goods and invites you to look anyway. Banned by some country stations for the implication. The refusal to pretend to be undamaged is the point, and it is the same refusal the whole catalogue keeps making.

Three — The ones she refused to sell · 1973–1980

Four songs that made her rich, and the reason they made her rich rather than somebody else.

Asking, not fighting
Jolene
Dolly Parton · 1973

The most-covered song she wrote, and the strangest: the narrator never threatens her rival, never blames the man, and spends four verses telling another woman how beautiful she is. It is a plea to somebody with all the power, and it is unbearably polite.

The copyright she kept
I Will Always Love You
Dolly Parton · 1974

Written as a goodbye to Porter Wagoner, not a lover — a way of leaving a business partnership without the leaving being a wound. Elvis wanted it; Colonel Parker wanted half the publishing; she said no and cried all night. Whitney Houston, eighteen years later, proved her right.

The crossover
Here You Come Again
Dolly Parton · 1977

Written by Barry Mann and Cynthia Weil, and the record that took her pop. Country purists called it a betrayal. She pointed out, repeatedly and cheerfully, that a bigger audience is just more people — which is the same arithmetic as the mailing label.

A labour song, actually
9 to 5
Dolly Parton · 1980

Written on set, with the percussion track played on her acrylic nails. It is a song about being used, credited to nobody, and promoted past by men — and it is now most often heard as an upbeat number about the working day. That gap is what section four of the essay is about.

Four — Walking out, and the light on the other side · 1977–1991

The songs about leaving, and the two that this rack’s companion zine builds a spread of physics on.

Deliverance
Light of a Clear Blue Morning
Dolly Parton · 1977

Written coming out of seven years with Porter Wagoner, on her first self-produced album. It starts nearly a cappella and builds until the arrangement is doing what the lyric describes. If you play one thing here, play this.

Leaving the room
Two Doors Down
Dolly Parton · 1977

A woman crying alone at a party two doors away decides to go and join it. A very small song about the specific effort of re-entering company when you have been shut in a room with your own bad night.

Pop Dolly, fully committed
You're the Only One
Dolly Parton · 1979

From the stretch when she was chasing the pop charts and getting told off for it. Worth hearing as evidence that the reinvention was not reluctant — she wanted the bigger room and went and got it.

A hymn for women who carried it
Eagle When She Flies
Dolly Parton · 1991

Gentle when she's held, and an eagle when she flies. Written at forty-five about the women she grew up watching, and one of the clearest statements of what she actually thought strength was — nothing to do with never needing help.

Five — Claimed as family, and what it cost · 1987–2017

Four decades of declining to make anybody respectable first, including the year it brought death threats.

Three voices, no lead
Wildflowers
Trio — Dolly Parton, Linda Ronstadt & Emmylou Harris · 1987

She wrote it, and then gave two-thirds of it away. A song about a flower that leaves the garden it was planted in, sung by three women who each had every commercial reason to insist on the top line and did not.

Camp, on purpose
Why'd You Come In Here Lookin' Like That
Dolly Parton · 1989

A number-one country single that is also, unmistakably, a drag number — and was received that way by the audience it was aimed at. She never once framed that audience as a demographic. She called them friends, in print, in 1984.

The one that cost her
Travelin' Thru
Dolly Parton · 2005

Written for Transamerica, drawing on a transgender relative and friend, and nominated for an Academy Award. She received death threats for it and said so plainly. Allyship with a price attached is the only kind that tells you anything.

Stopped at a gate
Rainbowland
Miley Cyrus ft. Dolly Parton · 2017

In March 2023 a Wisconsin elementary school barred its first-graders from singing this, after reviewing it against a policy on controversial issues. The proposed replacement, “Rainbow Connection,” was initially blocked too. A song about there being no gate, stopped at one.

Six — Books, and the kids they went to · 1999–2023

Where the money went, what she made for children, and the last version of the song she wrote when she walked out.

Going home, commercially unwise
The Grass Is Blue
Dolly Parton · 1999

After the pop years she made three bluegrass records that nobody asked for, and this one won a Grammy. A song built entirely from things that are not true, sung by someone insisting on them. She was fifty-three and had nothing to prove and made it anyway.

The inner-light song is a labour song
Shine Like the Sun
Dolly Parton · 2011

Written in 2009 for 9 to 5: The Musical and first sung by three women organising against a boss; she recorded it herself on Better Day two years later. Knowing where it came from changes it from a nice sentiment into a demand.

Written for the Book Lady's kids
I Believe in You
Dolly Parton · 2017

The title track of her first children's album, all fourteen songs written by her, all proceeds to the Imagination Library. A seventy-one-year-old megastar making a record for two-year-olds and routing the money back to the books.

Anti-bullying, from the child who was laughed at
Makin' Fun Ain't Funny
Dolly Parton · 2017

The other side of “Coat of Many Colors,” written forty-six years later and addressed directly to the children doing the laughing. She had been the kid in the coat, and she wrote the song for the ones on the other side of it.

Handed on
Light of a Clear Blue Morning (2023)
Dolly Parton ft. Lainey Wilson, Miley Cyrus, Queen Latifah & Reba McEntire

The 1977 deliverance song, forty-six years on, with four other women taking verses. The right last track: the one she wrote walking out of somewhere, given away to people who will keep singing it after she has gone.

Sources & credits

Authorship, checked rather than assumed. “Dumb Blonde” (1967) was written by Curly Putman, not by Parton — the persona in her first hit was assigned to her. “Here You Come Again” (1977) was written by Barry Mann and Cynthia Weil. Everything else on this rack credited to her, she wrote. “Shine Like the Sun” was written for 9 to 5: The Musical (2009), first recorded by Megan Hilty, Stephanie J. Block and Allison Janney, and cut by Parton herself on Better Day (2011) — a detail worth having, because the song reads differently once you know where it comes from.

Her own words. The “Hallmark card” passage this rack takes its title from is at pp. 297–298 of Dolly: My Life and Other Unfinished Business (HarperCollins, 1994), read in the scan held by the Internet Archive, along with the “people are not perfect… they are still all there is” lines summarised just above it. Quoted briefly for commentary and criticism. An open item, recorded rather than hidden: a widely-shared post attributes the Dickinson poem “If I can stop one Heart from breaking” (J919) to the same book; Ryan searched it and could not find it, so we make no claim about J919. The Dickinson line the book does carry is a paraphrase, and Zine No. 67 closes on it.

The Elvis story. Told by Parton many times over fifty years and consistent across tellings: Colonel Tom Parker’s standing demand for half the publishing, her refusal, and the night she spent crying about it. It is her account of a private conversation and there is no second party still able to confirm it; we present it as she tells it rather than as an established transaction.
