# Love Without Cringing

Dolly Parton from 1967 to 2023 — forty-four tracks — twenty-five of hers, then nineteen covers — and the argument that runs through all of them. The persona was assigned before she chose it. The copyright stayed in her pocket. And the songs went out to everybody, with no test at the door.

A Sound rack · companion to Zine No. 67

One

## “Dumb Blonde” was not her joke

Her first hit, in 1967, was a song called “Dumb Blonde,” in which a woman informs a man that being blonde is not the same as being stupid. She was twenty-one. **She did not write it.**

Curly Putman did. Which means the joke, the persona and the terms of engagement all arrived before she had any say in them, and the first thing the public heard from her was a rebuttal to an accusation nobody had put in writing. She spent the next fifty-nine years working inside that costume, and got so good at it that most people came to believe she had designed it from scratch.

That distinction — *assigned, then inhabited, then mastered* — is worth holding on to, because it is the honest version of a story usually told as pure self-invention. She was extremely deliberate about the wigs and the nails and the laugh. She was also handed a slot marked *blonde girl singer* by an industry that had exactly one of those, and the alternative on offer was not a different persona. It was no career.

And what the blonde was doing

Tressie McMillan Cottom’s [*The Dolly Moment*](https://tressie.substack.com/p/the-dolly-moment) (2021) argues that the costume worked as well as it did because it is **aggressively white** — that “blonde” operates as an unmarked racial identity, filtering for whiteness without anyone having to name it. That reading sits underneath this whole rack and is not resolved by it. [Zine No. 67](https://starstuff.earth/dolly-zine.html) gives it a spread rather than a footnote.

Two

## She wrote the dark ones too

The received Dolly is relentlessly sunny. The catalogue is not.

“Down from Dover” ends with a stillborn baby. “The Bridge” ends, by implication, with a pregnant woman going over one. “In the Good Old Days (When Times Were Bad)” is a title doing an entire argument’s work — a refusal to let anybody get nostalgic about her childhood on her behalf.

Cottom reads this run as *gothic motherhood*: country music demanded that a woman be a wife or a mother to be taken seriously, and Parton, who never had children, answered the demand by writing an archive of motherhood as painful and complicated as the real thing. That reading is hers and we are borrowing it with the tag on.

What it means for this rack is simpler. **The sunniest public figure in America had already written the worst outcomes down, in detail, and gone on singing.** The optimism was a position taken, not a failure to notice — which is worth carrying into the next two sections, because both are about what she then went and did.

Three

## The Book Lady, and what was on the envelope

In 1995 she started mailing books to children in Sevier County, Tennessee. Not the poor ones. Not the promising ones. Every one, birth to five, a book a month, until they started school.

Thirty-one years later that is more than **332 million books** across five countries, going out at something like three and a half million a month. In all that time, the thing that was never added is a test. No income threshold. No application beyond an address. No reading assessment, no referral, no diagnosis, no waiting list, and no annual review to confirm the child still deserves it.

And the parcel does not say *to the parent or guardian of*. It carries the child’s own name. **A two-year-old who cannot yet read gets post.**

Why that is rarer than it sounds

Almost everything else a child needs arrives through a gate — an income threshold, an eligibility determination, a severity cut-off, a screening, a place on a list that can be lost. Each is defended the same way: resources are finite, so they must be targeted. That is true, and it is not the whole sentence. **A gate does not only ration; it sorts** — and sorting requires somebody to rule on who is worth it. [Zine No. 67](https://starstuff.earth/dolly-zine.html) spends three spreads on what that costs the people who get through it.

She built it for her father. Robert Lee Parton could not read or write, and his daughter called him one of the wisest men she had ever known — both halves of that at once, which is the entire design. She did not conclude that he lacked something. She concluded that something had failed to reach him.

The programme is **Dolly Parton’s Imagination Library**, and the children called her the Book Lady. In 2017, at seventy-one, she wrote them an album and handed it the proceeds.

It also puts two songs forty-six years apart into a single argument, which is the reason this section sits in a rack rather than only in the zine. **“Coat of Many Colors” (1971) is written from inside the laughing** — the child in the handmade coat, mocked for it, insisting she was rich and they could not see it. **“Makin’ Fun Ain’t Funny” (2017) is the same playground addressed to the other children**, the ones doing the laughing. She had been the kid in the coat; when she finally got a room full of six-year-olds listening, she wrote to the ones on the other side of it.

Four

## The copyright in her pocket

In 1974 Elvis Presley wanted to record “I Will Always Love You.” The night before the session, Colonel Tom Parker explained the terms.

By Parton’s account, the rule was that Elvis did not record anything without half the publishing going to Presley’s side. She had written the song. She had published it herself. She said no — and, by her own account, cried all night about it.

I wanted to hear Elvis sing it, and it broke my heart. I cried all night.

Dolly Parton, recalling the 1974 decision

Eighteen years later Whitney Houston recorded it for *The Bodyguard* and it became one of the best-selling singles ever made, with the songwriting income going where Parton had kept it. The story gets told as business acumen, and it was. **But the shape of it is a refusal, not a strategy:** somebody powerful asked her to hand over half of a thing she had made, as the price of being allowed in the room, and she declined to pay a toll she did not owe.

That is the same instinct as the mailing label, pointed the other way. She would not put a gate in front of anybody else, and she would not walk through one herself.

**And then there is what grew on top of that story.**

In July 2021, on *Watch What Happens Live*, Andy Cohen asked her what the best thing was that she had bought with the Houston royalties. She said she had bought property in what was then a Black neighbourhood in Nashville — Sevier Park — and that it was *“the perfect place for me to be considering it was Whitney… I’m going to be down here with her people, who are my people as well.”*

It went round the world in days as proof that she had been a friend to Black Nashville decades before it was fashionable. It is still going round: it is in her obituaries this week. And in the part that makes it a story, **it does not hold up.**

What the property records say

NPR’s Amanda Marie Martínez checked it three weeks after the broadcast. Nashville Planning Department records show Parton acquired the two parcels at 12th Avenue South and Elmwood **in 1990 and 1991** — before *The Bodyguard* was released in 1992. **The royalties could not have bought the property.** The *Washington Post* had reported the purchase as 1997; NPR, which had run the feel-good version itself, published the correction and named its own error in the first paragraph.

Two historians told NPR the rest of it. Jessica Wilkerson of West Virginia University put it in a pattern — *“\[Parton\] has a pattern of claiming that when she purchases property, she invests in a place that she’s helping people… It gets trickier to do that with a Black community, where she doesn’t live, she’s not from there, and she’s doing it as a rich white person who can buy up real estate.”* Learotha Williams Jr. of Tennessee State University declined the reading altogether: *“She invested her money in an area that had a rich Black history, but one that was actively being undermined as a result of gentrification.”* Sevier Park is now called 12 South, and is one of the whitest, most expensive and most tourist-driven parts of Nashville.

And one detail we are not going to walk past, because the companion zine is built on the absence of exactly this: around the compound, **a large gate was constructed.**

None of which touches the copyright. She wrote the song, she refused the toll, she kept the money, and every word of that is documented. **What the sequel shows is how quickly a verifiable refusal gets promoted into an unverifiable virtue once an audience wants one** — which is the subject of this essay’s last section, arriving five years early. Cottom, interviewed for that same NPR piece, put it plainly: *“This isn’t about Dolly. Loving Dolly is a stand-in for how we can remediate our love for the nation.”*

Five

## Known and felt and seen

The gate around the compound is a sour note and the last section did not look away from it. This one is the other ledger, and it is longer.

On the day she died, **Rissi Palmer** — who in 2007 became the first Black woman to chart on *Billboard*’s country chart in almost twenty years, and who has spent the years since running *Color Me Country* — spent it telling stories about who Dolly had helped. Two of them are checkable.

**Lenora Ross** was from Cincinnati, sang on Lawrence Welk in 1971, and cut two singles for RCA in 1974 and 1975 with Bob Ferguson producing. The press of the day marketed her as “a female Charley Pride”; she is reported to have been the first Black woman signed to a major country label. Neither single charted. This is her account of a Nashville disc-jockey convention, where each label gathered its artists and let the DJs interview whoever they wanted:

I wasn’t getting interviewed much, and Dolly of course was, and I guess I looked sort of alone. And she started saying, “Hey, everybody, come over here and talk to Lenora — she’s just a little girl getting started like the rest of us did.” Dolly Parton is a beautiful person.

Lenora Ross, c. 1975, on a 1974 convention

Note the mechanism, because it is the one that recurs everywhere in this rack: **she had the attention, and she spent it on somebody who did not.** That is a smaller thing than a foundation and a more difficult one, because it has to be done in the room, in front of the people whose good opinion you also need.

**Donna Summer** is the case usually told slightly wrong, and the true version is better. Summer and her husband Bruce Sudano *wrote* “Starting Over Again”; Dolly recorded it and took it to No. 1 on the country chart on 24 May 1980, her twelfth chart-topper. Summer never recorded it herself. It is reported to have made her the first Black co-writer of a No. 1 country single. **Dolly did not platform her — she cut her song**, which in Nashville is not a gesture but the currency that actually pays, in royalties, for decades.

The pattern held to the end. In 2024 she is the *Dolly P* interlude introducing Beyoncé’s “JOLENE” on *Cowboy Carter*, in the middle of a loud public argument about whether Beyoncé belonged in the genre at all — *“Beyoncé is giving that girl some trouble and she deserves it!”* In 2021, when Lil Nas X sang a gender-flipped “Jolene” in the BBC Radio 1 Live Lounge — an out gay man keeping the song’s address to a woman intact — she wrote: *“Of course, I love him anyway. I was surprised and I’m honored and flattered.”*

And one story we are going to hold at arm’s length, in the same essay that corrects one

You will see it this week: that she spent decades quietly buying band uniforms and instruments for Black high schools across the South and never put her name on it. **It traces to a single observation.** In October 2022 the journalist Michael Harriot noticed that when he asked people who had been in band or choir at majority-Black high schools how they had paid for uniforms, “a surprising number” answered: *Oh, Dolly Parton paid for ours.* Others then said the same about their own schools. That is real testimony and we are not waving it away — but it is testimony, not a documented programme, and no organisation of hers confirms it. **The bind is genuine:** anonymity is the whole of the claim, so a missing paper trail is exactly what the story predicts and cannot settle it either way. We print what it is and let it stand at that height.

It matters that almost all of this was done sideways. Cottom is exact about the method, and it is not flattering:

Ever the artist of misperception, Dolly leaves unsaid what her core conservative country audience needs to never hear her say.

Tressie McMillan Cottom, *The Dolly Moment*, 2021

Both halves of that are true at once, which is the condition this whole rack keeps arriving at. **Except that twice, four years apart, she put it plainly anyway.** In 2016, to Larry King:

We are all God’s children. If you’re gay, you’re gay. If you’re straight, you’re straight. And you should be allowed to be how you are and who you are.

Dolly Parton, [*Larry King Now*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fsq8P9n3hXY), 2016 — in the same answer: “if you’re the fine Christian you think you are, why are you judging people? That’s God’s job.”

And in 2020, to *Billboard*:

I understand people having to make themselves known and felt and seen. And of course Black lives matter. Do we think our little white asses are the only ones that matter? No!

Dolly Parton to *Billboard*, August 2020

**“Known and felt and seen.”** She is describing something Cottom herself has a precise word for, in a different essay entirely:

Legibility is not the same as visibility. We often confuse the two and our discourse is poorer for the conflation… When I am visible, you can see me in the way you can see a dog or a lamppost. I am socially alive, as it were. I am a category and I exist. Being legible requires other people to have the ability to make sense of me. That means I am a thing that exists but that is also *supposed to exist*, in a given space or conversation or exchange.

Tressie McMillan Cottom, [“Why I Hate\* California”](https://tressie.substack.com/p/why-i-hate-california), *essaying*, June 2021

That is the whole difference between being let into a genre and belonging in it. Lenora Ross was visible at that convention — a person in a chair nobody was walking towards. What Dolly did in ten seconds was make her *legible*: somebody who was supposed to be there, like the rest of us.

And here is where the two ledgers meet rather than cancel. **The legibility Dolly was spending was the one the blonde bought her** — the aggressively white, unmarked, instantly-readable persona that is the sharpest thing in Cottom’s critique. That is our reading and not hers, and it does not settle anything: she had that standing because of how the industry sorts people, and she used it to vouch for people the same sorting was keeping out. Both sentences stay.

**And that 2016 sentence is one we already had.** *You should be allowed to be how you are and who you are* is, near enough word for word, the thing this foundation organises around: [authenticity is our purest freedom](https://stimpunks.org/philosophy/authenticity-is-our-purest-freedom/) — a line we take from **Neely J.**, writing in *SITI Girl Miami*, whose very next sentence asks for “the freedom to BE, *fully seen AND heard* in all my glory.” Seen and heard. Known and felt and seen. Two people with nothing else in common arriving at almost the same list.

And the reason it is a *freedom* rather than a preference: for neurodivergent and disabled people, **inauthenticity is usually a survival strategy rather than a choice.** Masking, code-switching, swallowing a stim, flattening an expression, hiding a need — each one costs something, and the bill is paid continuously, all day, for years. *Allowed to be how you are* is not a request to be indulged. It is the removal of a tax.

Which puts her sentence and this rack’s first section into the same argument. She was handed a costume in 1967 that somebody else had written, and she wore it for fifty-nine years — **six decades of practice at being legible on other people’s terms.** What she asked for on television, in 2016, was that nobody else should have to.

She [claimed us as family](https://starstuff.earth/dolly-zine.html#spread-7) with no conditions attached, and a great many of us claimed her back. She helped write people into being supposed to exist.

## What travelled, and what got left

Every song here has been carried somewhere by somebody, and carrying changes things.

“Jolene” left home as a plea to another woman and came back as a meme, a metal riff, a slowed-down internet artefact and, in March 2021, four syllables of public-health messaging sung in a hospital chair. “9 to 5” left as a labour song written on acrylic nails on a film set and is now used to sell productivity software. “I Will Always Love You” left as a goodbye to a business partner and came back as the most famous vocal performance of the 1990s, at which point a great many people assumed Whitney Houston had written it.

This is the ordinary fate of anything that travels well, and it is the reason this rack keeps naming what a song was *for* when it was made. Not to police the covers — she was famously delighted by them — but because **the smoothed version always travels further than the specific one**, and the specific one is usually where the argument lives.

The clearest case is the newest

In March 2023, administrators at Heyer Elementary in Waukesha, Wisconsin barred a first-grade class from performing “Rainbowland,” her duet with Miley Cyrus, after reviewing it against a district policy on *controversial issues in the classroom*. The proposed replacement, “Rainbow Connection,” was initially blocked as well. **A song about there being no gate, stopped at a gate** — and the mechanism was a policy, applied by people following it correctly.

And that is what the title of this rack is pointing at. Near the end of her autobiography, having just spent two pages on the fact that people will sometimes take your love and give nothing back and *are still all there is*, she catches herself being sincere in public and declines to apologise for it:

I don’t mean to sound like the inside of a Hallmark card. I do think, though, that it’s time we learned to use the word *love* without cringing.

Dolly Parton, *Dolly: My Life and Other Unfinished Business* (1994), pp. 297–298

Cottom opens *The Dolly Moment* by observing that this is exactly the register the culture normally denigrates — that it took a particular political moment to make earnest sentimentality acceptable coming from her. Both things can be true: the moment made room for it, **and she had been working in that register for twenty-seven years before the moment arrived**, taking the cost of it in reviews the whole time. The songs are where you can hear her not waiting for permission. Twenty-four of them follow.

- **Not a canonisation.** This is a rack about a body of work, made three days after she died, by people who are sad. It is not a verdict, and Cottom’s reading is not answered here.
- **Not bootstraps.** The rags-to-riches arc is the most extractable thing in the catalogue and gets used to argue that the ladder is fine because one person climbed it.
- **Not claiming her.** She was not one of us. The rack is about how her work treated people, not about recruiting her to a community she did not belong to.

A section of one, because it is a category of one. For sixty years her answer was *I don’t do politics* — and she was explicit about the arithmetic: as many Republican fans as Democrats, and no wish to lose either. Then, at seventy-seven, she opened a rock record by calling somebody a liar.

Sixty years of not saying it

World on Fire

Dolly Parton · 2023

The lead single from *Rockstar*, debuted live closing the 58th ACM Awards on 11 May 2023, a show she was co-hosting. The chorus calls someone a liar, says the world is on fire, and asks what they intend to do when it burns down. Asked on *Today* which lawmakers she meant, she laughed and said **“Any of them!”** — that they worry more about the party than the people, and that she did not think any of them were trying hard enough. **Read it next to section one:** the technique never changed. She landed the punch and still declined to name a side, which is the same manoeuvre that kept her legible to everybody for six decades — used, at the end, to finally say something.

[▶ Dolly Parton — World On Fire (58th ACM Awards)](https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/0QJXsmDBS8k)

### Sources & credits

**Authorship, checked rather than assumed.** “Dumb Blonde” (1967) was written by **Curly Putman**, not by Parton — the persona in her first hit was assigned to her. “Here You Come Again” (1977) was written by **Barry Mann and Cynthia Weil**. Everything else on this rack credited to her, she wrote. “Shine Like the Sun” was written for *9 to 5: The Musical* (2009), first recorded by Megan Hilty, Stephanie J. Block and Allison Janney, and cut by Parton herself on *Better Day* (2011) — a detail worth having, because the song reads differently once you know where it comes from.

**Her own words.** The “Hallmark card” passage this rack takes its title from is at pp. 297–298 of *Dolly: My Life and Other Unfinished Business* (HarperCollins, 1994), read in the scan held by the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/dollymylifeother00part/page/298/mode/2up), along with the “people are not perfect… they are still all there is” lines summarised just above it. Quoted briefly for commentary and criticism. **An open item, recorded rather than hidden:** a widely-shared post attributes the Dickinson poem “If I can stop one Heart from breaking” (J919) to the same book; Ryan searched it and could not find it, so we make no claim about J919. The Dickinson line the book *does* carry is a paraphrase, and [Zine No. 67](https://starstuff.earth/dolly-zine.html) closes on it.

**The Elvis story.** Told by Parton many times over fifty years and consistent across tellings: Colonel Tom Parker’s standing demand for half the publishing, her refusal, and the night she spent crying about it. It is **her account of a private conversation** and there is no second party still able to confirm it; we present it as she tells it rather than as an established transaction.

**The Nashville property.** Amanda Marie Martínez, “Why do we need Dolly Parton to be a saint?”, [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2021/08/20/1029306864/dolly-parton-nashville-sevier-park-real-estate-why-need-be-saint), 20 August 2021 — the Nashville Planning Department records placing the two parcels in 1990 and 1991 (transferred to Parton’s trust in 1997), the *Washington Post*’s incorrect 1997 purchase date, the constructed gate, and the interviews with Jessica Wilkerson and Learotha Williams Jr. Her own remarks are from *Watch What Happens Live With Andy Cohen*, July 2021. **We are printing this because the correction is five years old and still has not caught up with the anecdote**, which ran again in obituaries this week. Nothing here disputes that she owned the property or earned the royalties; what is unsupported is that the second bought the first, and that the purchase constituted an investment in the community already living there.

**Gates opened.** **Lenora Ross**: Cincinnati-born, two RCA singles 1974–75 produced by Bob Ferguson, marketed as “a female Charley Pride,” reported as the first Black woman signed to a major country label; her convention account is from her page at hillbilly-music.com, **which sits behind a bot check we did not bypass, so the quotation rests on Ryan’s reading of that page rather than on our own** — flagged here rather than presented as primary-confirmed. Rissi Palmer raised both artists on the day Parton died. **Donna Summer**: “Starting Over Again,” written by Summer and Bruce Sudano, recorded by Parton, No. 1 Hot Country Songs 24 May 1980 — Summer never recorded it herself, and the “first Black co-writer of a No. 1 country single” superlative is *reported* rather than confirmed at a primary source. **Beyoncé**: the “Dolly P” interlude and Parton’s statement, *Cowboy Carter*, 2024. **Lil Nas X**: BBC Radio 1 Live Lounge, 21 September 2021, and Parton’s reply. **The band uniforms** trace to Michael Harriot, 6 October 2022 — beneficiary testimony aggregated by a journalist, not a documented programme, as the note in section five says on its face. **Legibility**: Cottom, “Why I Hate\* California,” *essaying*, 26 June 2021 — a subscriber post we could not open, supplied by Ryan from his own copy. The reading that Parton spent the legibility her persona bought her is **ours, not Cottom’s**.

**The 2016 interview.** *Larry King Now* (Ora.TV), 2016 — quoted from [the interview itself on Larry King’s own channel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fsq8P9n3hXY), not from the social repost the clip is currently circulating as. **“Authenticity is our purest freedom” is not ours:** it is **Neely J.**’s, from “Collateral Beauty: The Journey of Undoing — an open letter to who needs it,” written for *SITI Girl Miami*. **The piece is no longer online and was never archived** — the only Wayback captures of its URL are 404s — so what survives is the passage quoted here and on stimpunks.org. Her full opening sentence is longer than the part that travels: *“Authenticity is our purest freedom, and I promised a beautiful little Black girl that I would scream her joy and love from the highest rooftops for the rest of my life.”* [Stimpunks adopted it](https://stimpunks.org/philosophy/authenticity-is-our-purest-freedom/) rather than coined it. The masking-as-survival-strategy framing is from that same page. The observation that her 2016 sentence and that philosophy are the same claim is ours.

**The critique.** Tressie McMillan Cottom, “The Dolly Moment: Why We Stan A Post-Racism Queen,” *essaying*, 25 February 2021 — the blonde-as-unmarked-whiteness argument and the gothic-motherhood reading are both hers, borrowed here with attribution and not paraphrased into anonymity. She draws in turn on Tara McPherson, *Reconstructing Dixie* (2003), and on Leigh Edwards’s work on Parton, gender and feminism.

**Rainbowland.** Heyer Elementary School, Waukesha, Wisconsin, March 2023: first-graders barred from performing the song after administrators reviewed it against the district’s Board Policy 2240 on controversial issues in the classroom; “Rainbow Connection” was initially blocked as a replacement and later permitted. Reported by WPR, NPR, TMJ4 and the Waukesha *Freeman*.

**Travelin’ Thru.** Written and performed for *Transamerica* (2005); Academy Award nomination for Best Original Song. Parton has said she drew on a transgender relative and a transgender friend, and told *USA Today* that the hate mail and threats came with the territory of having a big gay following.

**A note on the notes.** Every card describes its song rather than quoting it. The lyrics are the artists’ work and the place to meet them is the recording, which is why the embeds are the point of this page and the writing is only the way in. **Every video id here was resolved through YouTube’s oEmbed endpoint and checked for channel as well as liveness** — the twenty-five of hers are Dolly Parton’s own channel, DollyPartonVEVO, an official “— Topic” channel, or the releasing label (Sugar Hill, Nettwerk), and the nineteen covers are the covering artist’s own channel or VEVO, their “— Topic” channel, or the broadcaster or institution whose session it is (BBC Radio 1, BBC Music, triple j, the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame). **Of twenty-nine covers proposed for section seven, ten were dropped and one was dead:** nine resolved to fan channels and live-audience re-uploads rather than to anyone with a right to the recording, and one — a Beyoncé “music video” that looks entirely official in a search result — turned out to be fan-made, and was replaced with her own VEVO lyric video. Three plausible-looking uploads were dropped from the main list for the same reason.
