A Field Guide to
Eggs

Fifteen beginnings among our Terran kin, sorted by how far the egg gets from the body — and the catalogue turns out to disagree with the picture in your head. The egg you are imagining occupies one band out of five. Hard shell, tidy oval, a parent sitting on it. Most of the others are carried in a father, sunk under the skin of a mother's back, held in a mouth that cannot then be used for eating, or never laid at all. Laying one and sitting on it is an option. It was never the standard.

Where this came from The companion to The Shell Was Your Calcium (No. 57), which borrows the trans community's egg crack and finds that an eggshell hands most of its calcium to the skeleton of the animal it contains. That zine asks what does a shell do. This one asks the prior question — what counts as an egg — and the answer is much wider than one hen could carry.
Why the last entry is dashed Because the apparatus stops there. Fifteen entries get a reframe table because a popular verdict misreads an animal. The egg-crack accounts get none: there is no mistaken verdict about what it felt like when your own life rearranged itself behind you, so there is nothing to correct.

Five distances from the body

The sort is ours, and it came out of the entries rather than ahead of them. We began meaning to sort by what the shell is made of, and that sort collapsed immediately — the chicken and the common murre are the same object by every material measure, and the interesting thing about the murre is not its shell chemistry but a textbook explanation of its shape that turns out to be wrong. We tried sorting by who pays for the embryo, and several animals sat in two gates at once. What actually varies, and varies cleanly, is how far the egg gets from the body that made it. So the sections below run from eggs that never leave, out through eggs carried in skin and in mouths, to eggs guarded at arm's length, to eggs put down in sand and walked away from for good. None of these ranks above another. That is the whole reason it is a catalogue and not a ladder.

One · It never leaves

No egg is ever laid

Three animals that keep the egg inside a body until there is no egg left to speak of — and the first of them is not a curiosity but one of the most-repeated inventions in vertebrate history. Live birth evolved at least 115 separate times in lizards and snakes alone. The third case keeps the egg inside a body that did not make it: the father's.

Two · Under the skin

Laid, then grown over

One animal, and it belongs in a gate of its own because nothing else on this list does what it does. The eggs are laid — properly laid, fertilised outside — and then the mother's skin closes over each one and builds it a lid. She is not carrying them. She has been rebuilt around them.

Three · Carried

Somewhere on a parent, at all times

Two animals for whom the egg is outside the body and still never set down — and in both cases the parent doing it is the male, at a measurable cost to himself. One cannot eat for a month. The other's eggs simply die if he puts them down, which makes the carrying not devotion but plumbing.

Four · Set down and guarded

The band the word actually means

Five animals in the band everybody pictures — and four of them are strange inside it. A bird whose egg is nearly a quarter of her. A mammal whose egg is fed through its own shell. An octopus that guarded one clutch for four and a half years. And the seabird whose famous pointed egg is explained, in book after book, by a mechanism that does not happen.

Five · Left

Put down and walked away from

Four eggs whose parents are gone before anything happens, which sounds like the failure state and is nothing of the kind. One hatchling digs itself out of up to a metre and a half of mound and can fly the same day. One waits eighteen years for the water to come back. And one is left in a stranger's nest, which requires the shell to be a forgery good enough to survive inspection.

Where the apparatus stops

We are not going to reframe your life

This one gets no reframe table, and the absence is the argument. Everywhere above, "sounds like / actually" corrects a verdict that got an animal wrong — a shape misexplained, a father mistaken for a mother, a walked-away-from egg mistaken for neglect. Nobody has reached a mistaken verdict about the morning your own life rearranged itself behind you. There is nothing here to correct, and a column headed actually would be an insult dressed as rigour.

Not: Not permission from nature. Nothing here licenses anything about how we live or who we are. A universe that could approve could also decline, and we would not take the verdict either way. Down to is not because of. Not a ranking. The five sections are five distances, not five rungs. The megapode is not a worse parent than the octopus; it is running a strategy that has worked for millions of years and needs no apology. Not mascots. No animal here is Autistic or trans, and none is a stand-in for us. They are kin with their own business, and the borrowed word in the last entry is credited to the people it belongs to rather than to a lizard. Not a lesson about competition. The sand tiger is here because it shows an egg being used as food, which is a fact about supply. It is not a parable about the strong prevailing, and reading it that way is precisely the misapplication one whole collection here exists to refuse. Not settled. Whether squamate live birth ever reverses to egg-laying is actively contested; the arms-race account of cuckoo egg mimicry has a live challenge against it; and several figures below are ranges rather than numbers, and say so.
Star stuff, beginning

Every animal here is spending calcium, carbon and phosphorus forged in dying stars on the same problem — how to start — and no two of them solved it the same way. The calcium in a kiwi's enormous shell, the keratin in a megapode's egg tooth, the trehalose that holds a brine shrimp intact through eighteen dry years: all of it assembled in stellar interiors and in the deaths that followed.

What the fifteen have in common is not a method. It is that each of them began. The methods do not converge, they do not rank, and the ones a tidy account would call deficient — no shell, no nest, no parent present, no egg at all — are ancient, stable, and in one case reinvented more than a hundred separate times. There is no standard egg.

Figures follow the primary and authoritative sources. Squamate viviparity — live birth has evolved at least 115 times in lizards and snakes, against roughly 140 origins across all vertebrates, and occurs in nearly 20% of squamate species; the early-origin-plus-reversions argument is Pyron, R. A. & Burbrink, F. T., "Early origin of viviparity and multiple reversions to oviparity in squamate reptiles," Ecology Letters 17, 13–21 (2014), and the reversibility of the transition is contested — see Blackburn, D. G., "Evolution of viviparity in squamate reptiles: reversibility reconsidered," Journal of Experimental Zoology B (2015). We print the count, which is not the disputed part, and leave the direction of travel open. Sand tiger shark — oophagy and adelphophagy in lamniform sharks; a female may carry up to ten embryos and gives birth to two, one from each uterus. Given as long-established descriptive natural history rather than from a paper read for this guide; the modern reference on the reproductive cycle is Wyffels, J. T. and colleagues, "Reproductive cycle and periodicity of in situ and aquarium female sand tiger sharks Carcharias taurus," Frontiers in Marine Science 9 (2022). Seahorses — Syngnathidae are the only vertebrates with male pregnancy; the brood pouch supplies protection, osmoregulation and nutrient exchange across placenta-like tissue, with embryos connected to the male's circulatory system. Paternal provisioning is measured: a patrotrophy index of 1 in Hippocampus abdominalis, from Whittington, C. M. and colleagues, "Paternal nutrient provisioning during male pregnancy in the seahorse Hippocampus abdominalis," Journal of Comparative Physiology B 190 (2020). The immunogenetic losses and the tlx1 functional-asplenia finding are from the seahorse genomics literature (2022) and are cited, not read in full. Surinam toad — eggs of roughly 6.5 mm implanted in the female's dorsal epidermis, which grows over each to form a cyst with a horny lid; direct development with no free-living tadpole stage; emergence as toadlets after roughly 12 to 20 weeks; five other Pipa species release young as tadpoles. Follows the descriptive literature on Pipa oviposition, including work written expressly to correct circulating misconceptions about the species; clutch-size figures vary widely between accounts and none is printed. Giant water bugs — back-brooding is Belostomatinae (e.g. Belostoma, Abedus), where females cement eggs to the male's dorsum and every species studied shows exclusive paternal care; brood pumping, brood stroking and surface brooding are the named behaviours, and brooding is obligatory — unattended eggs die whether exposed or submerged. Paternal care measurably costs foraging efficiency, mobility and longevity. The synthesis is R. L. Smith, "Evolution of paternal care in the giant water bugs," in The Evolution of Social Behaviour in Insects and Arachnids (Cambridge). Lethocerus does not back-brood, and the family/subfamily distinction is stated on the card because it is the first thing lost in retelling. Banggai cardinalfish — paternal mouthbrooding for up to about 30 days without feeding; given as well-established field and husbandry natural history, with no clutch figures printed. The inconspicuous-breeding-coloration finding is from work on egg concealment in male cardinalfish (2024); the mixed-parentage result is from genetic work on an Australian mouthbrooding species (2022). Both are cited rather than read in full. Domestic hen — Halgrain, M., Georgeault, S., Bernardet, N., Hincke, M. T. & Réhault-Godbert, S., "Concomitant morphological modifications of the avian eggshell, eggshell membranes and the chorioallantoic membrane during embryonic development," Frontiers in Physiology 13, 838013 (2022), read at source: thickness between EID0 and EID15 not significantly different (p = 0.110), breaking strength between EID11 and EID15 significantly reduced (p = 0.007), the shell supplying more than 80% of the embryo's calcium and the skeleton holding about 75% of it at hatch. The internal-pipping and egg-tooth material is handed to No. 57, whose colophon carries its own limits — including that the commonly quoted 12–24 hour interval between internal and external pipping could not be confirmed against a primary and is therefore not printed. Common murre — and this is the entry to check hardest, because it corrects a textbook. The pyriform egg was widely explained by rolling in a tight arc or spinning like a top; Ingold (1980) found little support, and the account is rejected in Birkhead, T. R. & Thompson, J. E. and colleagues, "Egg shape in the Common Guillemot Uria aalge and Brünnich's Guillemot U. lomvia: not a rolling matter?", with the decisive objection that the rolling arc is much wider than the breeding ledge. The alternative carried here is Birkhead, T. R. and colleagues, "The pyriform egg of the Common Murre (Uria aalge) is more stable on sloping surfaces," The Auk 135(4), 1020–1032 (2018); the mechanical-strength and contamination hypotheses are also Birkhead's and are given as hypotheses. Read at abstract and summary level, not in full. Kiwi — the largest bird egg relative to body size; printed as a range, 15 to 22 per cent of female body mass, against roughly 2 to 3 per cent for an ostrich, with one recorded 1.7 kg female laying a 406 g egg. This range is assembled from record-keeping and reference sources (Guinness World Records, the Smithsonian, Save the Kiwi) rather than from a primary morphometric study we read, published figures differ, and it is the figure on this page we would most like to improve. The frequent explanation — that the egg is a retained relic of larger ancestors — is a genuine hypothesis with genuine argument against it and is deliberately not asserted. Largely paternal incubation in most species is well established. Monotremes — eggs of roughly 13–16 mm with a soft leathery shell, porous enough to absorb nutrients secreted into the oviduct from the mother's circulation; incubation after laying about 10–11 days; lactation approximately 130 days in the platypus and 200 in the short-beaked echidna, taken from mammary patches rather than teats. Given as well-established comparative anatomy and physiology; the echidna's possession of both an egg tooth and a caruncle has a recent primary (Hughes and colleagues, Developmental Biology, 2022) which is cited and not read. Deep-sea octopus — Robison, B. H., Seibel, B. & Drazen, J., "Deep-sea octopus (Graneledone boreopacifica) conducts the longest-known egg-brooding period of any animal," PLOS ONE 9(7): e103437 (2014), open access and read at source: an individually recognisable female at about 1,400 m in Monterey Canyon, first observed May 2007, revisited 18 times, brooding for 53 months, never observed feeding. Megapodes — incubation by solar, geothermal or microbial heat, with parental care of emergent chicks not observed; eggs buried 40 to 150 cm deep; ascent to the surface at a median of about 40 hours (range 26–55) from the Journal of Ornithology work on brush-turkey chick behaviour following underground hatching; mound thermal tolerance from Eiby, Y. A. & Booth, D. T., The Auk 125(3), 594 (2008). Megapodes are superprecocial, hatching in the most mature condition of any bird, with some species able to fly on the day of hatching. Both papers read at abstract level. Sea turtles — temperature-dependent sex determination with a pivotal temperature around 29 °C, varying by species and population (some measured nearer 30.4 °C); nests averaging above roughly 31 °C through the middle third of development can produce 100% female clutches; green turtle rookeries in the northern Great Barrier Reef are reported at approximately 99% female hatchling production. The counterweight is stated with it: analysis of pivotal temperatures worldwide shows higher values at warmer sites, indicating local adaptation that may partially mitigate feminisation. Compiled from the TSD and climate literature at review and abstract level rather than from a single primary. Brine shrimp — encysted embryos arrest at gastrula and enter diapause, withstanding desiccation, freezing, heat, oxidation, ultraviolet and years of anoxia when hydrated; reported diapause durations run from about three months up to roughly 18 years, given as a reported maximum rather than a measured record. The 17-year field series is Belovsky, G. E. and colleagues, "Overwinter survival of crustacean diapausing cysts: brine shrimp (Artemia franciscana) in Great Salt Lake, Utah," Limnology and Oceanography 64 (2019), with initial hatchability 17.8–78.8% and overwinter survival 9.9–65.9%, varying dramatically between years. Claims of far longer dormancy in other crustaceans exist and are not carried here. Common cuckoo — gentes are host-specific female lineages laying a constant, often mimetic egg type; the pied wagtail gens and the experimental coevolution work are Brooke, M. de L. & Davies, N. B. (1988) and Davies & Brooke's experimental studies. Mimicry assessed by host retinal modelling rather than human perception is Cassey, P. and colleagues, "Egg colour mimicry in the common cuckoo Cuculus canorus as revealed by modelling host retinal function" (2008). The arms-race framing is flagged as contested rather than asserted: a 2025 preprint argues that cuckoo egg colour mimicry results from coevolutionary alternation rather than an arms race, and a 2025 Science paper addresses the genomic architecture of egg mimicry; the preprint has not been peer reviewed and nothing here leans on it. Brood parasitism as a nesting strategy, including that not building a nest evolved independently at least seven times in birds, is carded in A Field Guide to Nests (No. 15) and not repeated. The egg-crack accounts — egg and getting your egg cracked are trans community language, borrowed here with thanks and with no individual coiner identified, because none has been established: Bauer, E., "Cracking the History of the Trans 'Egg'," Them, 5 December 2024, traces the earliest documented use to an Urban Dictionary entry for egg mode dated 23 August 2016 and records Susan Stryker unable to date her own first encounter with the term. The extension of the term to Autistic and ADHD experience is Essy Knopf's, and he credits the trans origin himself. Bauer's warning that naming somebody an egg can push them further in is carried in No. 57's refusals. Held honestly: the sort into five distances is ours, arrived at after the entries and not before them — a material sort and a who-pays sort were both tried first and both failed, as the gates band says on the page. Sand tiger reproduction, mouthbrooding durations, monotreme physiology and megapode superprecocity are given as well-established natural history rather than from papers read for this guide, and precise figures are withheld where the sources are secondary. The kiwi range and the brine shrimp maximum are the two softest numbers here and are both printed as ranges with their provenance stated. Nothing on this page licenses any claim about human beings: the animals are kin with their own business, and the borrowed word belongs to the people who made it, not to a lizard.
