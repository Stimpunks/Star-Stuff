Lag 1 d

Nobody had systematically asked whether ADHD comes with gut symptoms, so somebody did

A pre-registered systematic review and meta-analysis screened 9,613 records and kept 23 studies, together covering more than 1.9 million participants, comparing gastrointestinal symptoms in people with a DSM- or ICD-defined ADHD diagnosis against controls. Studies of diagnosed organic bowel disease — inflammatory bowel disease and the like — were excluded, so what is left is functional symptoms. ADHD was associated with increased odds of every symptom investigated: total GI symptoms OR 1.48 (95% CI 1.35–1.62), irritable bowel syndrome 1.58 (1.39–1.78), constipation 1.97 (1.49–2.61), encopresis 4.32 (2.50–7.47). But the sentence to keep is in the abstract’s opening, not its results: an increase in gastrointestinal symptoms “is well documented in autism spectrum disorder, but the extent of GI involvement in ADHD has not yet been systematically evaluated.”

The star stuff frame Read as a finding about us, this is a list of things that are wrong with us. Read as a finding about the literature, it is something else entirely: in 2026, for a diagnosis given to a rising share of the world’s population, nobody had pooled the evidence. The symptoms were there the whole time. What was missing was the looking. We have written a good deal on this site about the difference between a trait and the environment that meets it; this is the same distinction one level up, in the research record itself — the absence of a finding is a fact about where attention went, and it is not a fact about anybody’s body. The authors say plainly that the work was done for autism and not for us.
Association, an unresolved confound, and a number the headline gets wrong Every constituent study is observational; an odds ratio here says that the symptoms travel together, not that either causes the other, and the paper claims nothing more. The authors name the confound themselves and it is the obvious one: future research “should differentiate intrinsic biological pathways from medication-related effects,” because stimulants have gastrointestinal effects and a great many people in these cohorts take them. The proposed mechanisms — microbiome, vagal regulation, behaviour — are listed as possibilities and are not tested here. And 1.9 million is a sum across 23 separate studies, not a cohort; the ScienceDaily headline, Study of 1.9 million people finds a striking link between ADHD and gut problems, reads as one enormous study, which would be a much stronger design than the one that exists. The widest interval is the loudest number: encopresis runs from 2.50 to 7.47, so 4.32 is the middle of a wide guess.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed. ADHD is named on No. 30, No. 36, the neurodiversity primer and the migrating guide; interoception is already worked on Always Half Lit and The Rest You Keep. No page here contains gastrointestinal, checked by grep. What is unspent is not the biology — it is the research-attention argument: a symptom class documented in one neurotype and never pooled in another, for decades, and the gap noticed only when somebody went looking. That is a How We Got Here shape, and the joint would be marked documented.

Lag 47 d

One shape, tiled forever, never repeating — and the light comes off it with a handedness

In 2023 a single tile shape was found that covers a plane and can only do so without ever repeating: the Smith hat, the answer to a question left open for fifty years after Penrose. This group took the hat tiling, put a point at the centre of each tile, and got what they call a monotile quasilattice — a point set with exact threefold rotational symmetry and no mirror symmetry at all. Then they built it: a 350-nanometre silicon-nitride film, electron-beam lithography, 372,100 holes of 100 nm radius over about half a millimetre square. Shine a laser at it — the film sits on silicon, so the pattern is read in reflection — and the diffraction comes back as sharp Bragg peaks whose positions do not move when you move the beam — the signature of long-range order — arranged in a pinwheel that has no mirror line. The pinwheel’s tilt is not fitted: θchiral = arccos[(3φ−1)/4] ≈ 15.52°, derived from the golden ratio because the tiling’s own metatiles twist by Fibonacci numbers. And the structure responds differently to left- and right-handed circularly polarised light, which the paper says has never been observed in conventional quasicrystals with inversion symmetry — the qualifier is the authors’ and it is the whole point. Build the mirror image and the handedness flips.

The star stuff frame Crystallography spent a century defining a crystal as a repeating unit, and then had to change the definition — the paper says so in its own introduction, dating the change to Shechtman’s quasicrystals and the 2011 Nobel that followed. This paper goes one step further into the same territory, and the step is the one worth carrying: the new optical behaviour comes from what the discipline would call defects. The authors’ own sentence is that they “globally reduce the overall symmetry of the structure to C3 by introducing quasiperiodic defects into a honeycomb lattice.” The honeycomb is the regular thing. The departures from it are what make the light do something the honeycomb cannot. Order without repetition is not disorder, and the tidy version — a lattice, a unit cell, a norm you can take one sample of and generalise — was always a special case that happened to be easy to describe.
The perfect symmetry belongs to the abstraction, and the name is not about Albert Einstein The hat tiling itself does not have exact threefold symmetry. The paper is explicit: it has weak threefold symmetry, and the exact symmetry appears in the centroid point set “because the detailed shape information of the individual hat tiles is removed.” The perfect thing is what is left after you throw the shapes away, which is a caveat worth keeping in any frame built on it. Chirality here means one specific thing: absence of mirror symmetry within a two-dimensional plane, which the paper distinguishes in a sentence of its own from the three-dimensional chirality of a molecule. And the hat is not quite a single tile: the tiling uses the hat and its reflection, and the paper notes that Smith’s team later found a truly single-type monotile, the specter — not the one used here. The circular-polarisation patterns also change with the lattice spacing, so the effect “depends not only on the properties of the monotile quasilattice but also on other parameters.” Two things about the coverage. The ScienceDaily headline is The shape behind the Einstein problem just revealed strange new physics, and neither headline nor body mentions that the “Einstein problem” is a German pun — ein Stein, one stone — and has nothing to do with Albert Einstein; that etymology is standard in the tiling literature and is not stated in this paper, so it is marked here rather than sourced to it. And the release credits the Institute of Industrial Science at the University of Tokyo alone, where three of the four authors carry NTT affiliations and two carry Tokyo Institute of Technology.
Where it goes Genuinely unspent, and by a wide margin. Grepped: no page on this site contains aperiodic, quasicrystal, monotile or tiling, and the one hit for Penrose is a changelog entry about Roger Penrose on entropy, which is a different Penrose argument and was read rather than counted. This is the strongest zine candidate the scan has turned up in four weeks and it belongs in Star Stuff: one settled, checkable fact — a shape that orders a plane without repeating — followed honestly until the belonging claim is already inside it. See the seedbed for what it still needs.

Lag 48 d

The last wiring to finish is the wiring that buffers the loss, and it was measured where such things are not usually measured

Superficial white matter is the layer of short U-shaped fibres running just under the cortex. It carries most of the connections from one bit of cortex to the next, and — the detail this turns on — it is among the last white matter in the brain to be myelinated, which leaves it with thinner sheaths, less mature support cells and an earlier exposure to damage. This study measured it by diffusion MRI in 459 older adults in India, from a community cohort in which over half are illiterate and three in five live rurally. Denser neurite structure went with better language ability; more free water went with higher odds of cognitive impairment. Gray-matter loss remained the strongest predictor of cognition — and its effect was attenuated where the superficial white matter was in better condition. The authors’ reading: superficial white matter health “may drive resistance to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.”

The star stuff frame Two things here and the second is the larger one. The first: the structure that develops last, and is therefore the most fragile, is the one found to be doing the buffering — late and exposed is not the same as unimportant, and a developmental schedule is not a ranking. The second is about who gets measured. The authors say it outright: people of lower socioeconomic status “have been historically underrepresented in both dementia and neuroimaging studies,” and most prior work in this area used clinic-based samples. So the reference range for a healthy older brain was built somewhere else, on somebody else, and has been applied outward ever since. This study had to build its own literacy measure — asking each participant to read a sentence aloud — because the standard cognitive instruments assume you can read. A norm that cannot describe most of the people it is applied to was never a norm; it was a sample.
Cross-sectional, one threshold missed, and the headline finding is the stratified one This is a snapshot, not a trajectory. Every association is cross-sectional, so “may drive resistance” is the authors’ hypothesis about a mechanism they did not observe unfolding. One number sits just the wrong side of the line the paper itself drew: the association between global free water and cognitive impairment is reported at odds ratio 1.23 per standard deviation with FDR q = 0.06, above the 0.05 the study uses elsewhere for multiple-comparison correction — it is in the abstract as a finding, and the figure legend prints the q. And the socioeconomic result is weaker than it reads. The paper states that it did not find significant interactions between socioeconomic status and white matter for language in the continuous models; the effect appears in subgroup analyses run afterwards. That is a legitimate way to look and a weaker claim than an interaction test would have been, and the ScienceDaily framing, Hidden brain wiring may help keep the mind sharp as gray matter shrinks, carries none of it. The cohort is 459 people from six of eleven sites.
Where it goes white matter, cognitive reserve and dementia return nothing on this site outside last week’s own edition, checked by grep. The half that is unspent and worth a piece is the second half of the frame above — a reference range built on the people who were easiest to scan — which is the same argument as a diagnostic instrument normed on one population and then used on everyone, and which this site has made about people without ever making it about an imaging atlas. That is a Star Stuff register piece if the science stays in front. It is not a piece about staying sharp, and anything that starts reading that way has gone into the advice genre this page declines twice a week.

Lag 55 d

Coral was reported off Benin in 1963, written off as dead, and nobody went back for sixty-two years

Surveys off the coast of Benin in 1963–64 found what looked like coral reef barriers on the continental shelf. Their condition was never established and they were presumed dead. Between April and December 2025 a team went back with side-scan sonar and underwater video, surveyed about 7 km of shelf west to east, found two distinct zones of hard bottom in the sonar, and put cameras on the targets. At 54 metres down, on rocky substrate, there are living coral communities. The paper records at least six octocoral morphotypes, two black coral taxa and eight associated reef fish species from the imagery. It is the first confirmed visual evidence of living mesophotic coral off Benin and, to the authors’ knowledge, the first documented anywhere on the Gulf of Guinea continental shelf.

The star stuff frame The reefs were not lost and then found. They were recorded, filed as dead on no evidence, and left alone for six decades — and the original survey was not even looking for them; it was looking for fishing grounds, and the coral was a by-product. The paper’s own framing is that West African mesophotic systems “remain largely unexplored,” which is the honest name for the gap: an absence in the record that was an absence of looking, in a stretch of ocean whose own researchers have not had the ships. This is a thing this site keeps meeting in human form — a blank in a file read as a blank in the world — and it is worth noticing that the correction came from the Beninese institute whose coast it is.
The paper declines both of the headline’s nouns ScienceDaily ran it as A coral reef thought dead for 60 years is teeming with life. The paper says it is not a reef: the assemblage is “dominated by octocorals, forming a mesophotic coral garden rather than a structuring scleractinian reef” — soft corals on rock, not the stony framework that builds a reef, and the distinction is the paper’s own and appears in its abstract. And teeming is the wire’s word for eight fish species identified from video. The survey is explicitly preliminary: visual observations at selected sonar targets over roughly seven kilometres, with the authors themselves calling for “broader acoustic mapping, quantitative surveys, and physical sampling” before anyone says how much is there. No physical sample was taken, so the identifications are morphotypes from imagery rather than named species. None of which makes it less good news. It makes it a first look, which is what the title says it is.
Where it goes coral appears on eleven pages here and mesophotic on none. The existing uses are metaphorical or incidental — reef as an example inside the symbioses guide and elsewhere — so the biology is not spent, but neither is a single first-look survey a zine. The honest shape is a Glimmers entry, where the occasion is the point and the piece can say that the news is the going back. If it is ever more than that, the argument is the one in the frame and not the corals.

Lag 122 d

The arrows in the textbook were an assumption first, and the numbers were fitted to them afterwards

Open an organic chemistry textbook at the inductive effect and you meet a chain of arrows — Cl←CH2←CH2←CH3 — showing an electron pull travelling down a molecule bond by bond, weakening as it goes. This paper computes it and concludes that in neutral molecules the inductive effect is effectively limited to one bond. Where the effect does travel further, in charged species, the authors argue it “should be considered as polarizability rather than ‘purely’ inductive,” and that the cases usually explained as a through-space field effect are better explained the same way. The evidence people cite for onward transmission — carbon-13 NMR chemical shifts, most often — “should be viewed with scepticism.” But the sentence that makes this more than a chemistry correction is about how the picture was made. Ingold named the effect and drew the arrows in 1934; Wheland and Pauling then quantified it with what is essentially Hückel theory, putting “suitable δ’s upon the proper atoms” and calling polarizability a “small correction.” And, in the paper’s words: “Due to limitations in the available quantum chemical techniques at the time, assumptions, rather than calculations, about the nature of charge transmission were invoked, and then parameters calculated based on these assumptions.”

The star stuff frame A picture was drawn from an expectation. Parameters were then fitted to the picture. The fitted parameters were taught as a measurement, and when reality declined to match, the response was to add another effect rather than revise the first one. The authors say so in their conclusions: the pioneers “had expectations for how electron-transmission would take place, and when those expectations did not match with reality, additional effects (such as the field effect) were introduced.” That is the exact machinery this site’s chain collection was built to trace, arriving here in the least political material imaginable — no people involved, no funding motive, no one harmed, just an arrow that felt right in 1934 and every cohort of students since who met it as a measured thing. It is the cleanest available demonstration that a model can be protected by patching, and that the patch can outlive everyone who remembers it was a patch.
A computational paper in an education journal, and not the first challenge No experiment is reported. The evidence is quantum-chemical calculation — natural population analysis charges via NBO, cross-checked against QTAIM, run on Cardiff’s supercomputing facility — so it is one well-argued computational account and not a measurement anybody made in a flask. It is also not the first to say this, and the paper does not claim to be: it cites Wiberg finding little onward transmission by QTAIM before them, and frames the textbook position as one that “does not align with more recent studies.” The venue is the Journal of Chemical Education, which is where this argument belongs and which also tells you its ambition is pedagogical. And the “nearly 100 years” in the headline is arithmetic, not a quotation: Ingold named the effect in 1934, which is 92 years, and Lewis described it unnamed in 1916, which is 110. The paper names neither span. Its own closing move is modest — it observes that under the IUPAC definition the polarizability effect is an inductive effect, and “respectfully suggest[s] that an opportunity exists to clarify this situation.”
Where it goes inductive effect returns nothing here, checked by grep. This is a How We Got Here chain and a strong one, because every link is dated and published: Lewis 1916 documented, Ingold 1934 documented, Wheland and Pauling’s fitted parameters documented, the introduction of the field effect to save the model contested, and the claim that the teaching diagram has outlived its evidence documented by this paper. What would need care is the jump from a chemistry diagram to anything about people; the collection’s own rule is that a chain must not reach its conclusion across three leaps, and this one has a perfectly good conclusion without leaving chemistry at all.

Lag 172 d

The Earth is made of star stuff that matches nothing else in the collection

Meteorites sort into two families by the isotopes forged in different stellar processes: non-carbonaceous, formed in the inner Solar System, and carbonaceous, formed further out. Where the Earth sits between them has been argued for years, with estimates of outer-Solar-System material running from about 6% to about 40% — and a lot rests on it, because that is the material usually credited with delivering our water. This paper takes ten nucleosynthetic isotope anomalies across fourteen meteorite and planetary reservoirs and asks the question with all ten at once instead of two at a time. The result is a straight line: extend the array traced by the non-carbonaceous bodies in any two of the ten, and it passes through the Earth’s own composition within one standard deviation, every time. The conclusion the authors draw is that the Earth “formed exclusively from inner Solar System material whose composition did not vary over the course of accretion and was, on average, unlike that of any chondrite.” Carbonaceous material comes out at likely under 0.1% of the Earth’s mantle and under 2% of the whole planet. The same method predicts Venus and Mercury sit further out along the line than we do — more extreme, not less.

The star stuff frame Every previous model was trying to build the Earth out of things already in the drawer — this chondrite plus that one, and where the arithmetic failed, a “missing component” was proposed to fill the gap. This paper’s answer is that the arithmetic was never going to work, because the Earth is an endmember: it sits at the edge of the range of everything we have collected, not inside it. The catalogue of known types does not contain us. That is the phrase this whole site is organised around, and here it is as cosmochemistry: the reference specimens were the specimens that happened to survive and fall and get picked up, and a thing outside them is not anomalous, it is simply not in the collection. The paper ends by asking for sample returns from Venus and Mercury, which is the honest version of what to do when your range is too narrow — go and widen it.
A reanalysis, some filled-in cells, and two readings the authors do not choose between Nobody measured anything new. This is a statistical reanalysis of a public compilation of existing isotope measurements, and its strength is the method rather than the data. The analysis needed all ten ratios for every reservoir; several reservoirs had only nine, and the missing values were “inserted as synthetic priors based on correlations among meteorite groups” — a defensible choice, made by the authors, that a reader should know about. The “Vesta group” is a composite of six different sample types treated as one reservoir. And the headline conclusion has an alternative the paper states and does not rule out: either the Earth accreted uniform material, or it accreted varied material and the variation was erased by perfect equilibration between mantle and core. The authors argue the first is “more probable” on the grounds that the second needs extra coincidences — probable is not established, and they say so. The Venus and Mercury numbers are a prediction resting on an assumed Gaussian distribution of mass with distance from the Sun, and are untested until something brings a sample back. Where the coverage is right, which is worth saying as often as where it is wrong: ScienceDaily’s “less than two percent of Earth’s total mass, and possibly none at all” checks out exactly against the paper’s own bound. Its “We were truly astonished” is from the release, not the paper.
Where it goes Not unspent, and the page it belongs to is already here. No. 84, There Have Been So Many Earths, is built on the isotopic crisis — the Moon’s oxygen isotopes being almost indistinguishable from Earth’s while nearly every meteorite class differs measurably — and this paper sharpens exactly that problem from the other end, by showing the Earth is an endmember among all known meteorites across ten isotopic systems rather than one. No. 45 works aluminium-26 in the Allende meteorite and No. 71 uses Vesta for orbital mechanics; neither collides. So this is a spread or an amendment to No. 84, not a new zine, and proposing it as a new one would be re-running our own scroll zine with fresher numbers.

Five crocodiles were asked where home was and gave five different answers

Saltwater crocodiles are famous for going home — move one, and it will often come back, which is why relocation so often fails as a management tool. This study put satellite tags on five mature animals in the Sundarbans of Bangladesh: three raised in captivity, one caught wild and released where it was caught, one caught wild and moved somewhere else. They were tracked for 53 to 127 days each. The paper’s first result is the one worth carrying: “Movement patterns varied substantially among individuals.” The translocated animal ranged up to 34.3 km a day across a home range of 2,515 km², consistent with the strong homing response already on record. The others settled into ranges of 14.2 to 125 km². And the captive-reared crocodiles “showed no indication of homing towards prior husbandry sites, despite long-term captivity” — they simply took up residence where they were put.

The star stuff frame Homing is the species-level story, and what the tags recorded was individuals. The thing that predicted what each animal did was not what it was but where it had been — and the captive-reared animals had no home to go back to, because a pen is somewhere you were kept and not somewhere you are from. That is a real distinction, and it is doing the whole of the work here: the instinct everyone credits to the species turns out to need a place to point at. Five animals, five answers, and a conservation programme that will succeed or fail on which answer it plans for.
n = 5, and two of the three categories hold exactly one animal This is the reason for the grade and it is not a criticism of the authors, who report the sample plainly in their own abstract. Five mature crocodiles: three captive-reared, one wild-caught and released, one translocated. Every statement above about how translocated animals differ from released ones rests on a comparison between single individuals, and the paper still goes on to recommend a management strategy — releasing subadult and adult captive-reared crocodiles “as a viable conservation strategy” — on that base. A hundred dollars of parts, and the sky is measuring itself through your desk

A cosmic ray hits the upper atmosphere, and the shower it makes rains muons down through the air, through the roof, through you, at a rate of order one a second through the palm of a hand — an approximate textbook figure, flagged below, and not this paper’s. Detecting them used to mean an institution. CosmicWatch is a desktop muon detector built from a plastic scintillator, a silicon photomultiplier and custom electronics, with onboard storage, a small display and environmental sensors; the v3X revision improves sensitivity and data acquisition over earlier versions. The paper’s own claim, in its own words, is “a total component cost under $100 and a build time suitable for high school students,” which makes it suitable for “education, outreach, and introductory research applications in particle and astroparticle physics.”

The star stuff frame The particles were always arriving. What changed is who is allowed to be the one who notices — and that is a question about apparatus, not about aptitude. A detector that costs less than a textbook and can be assembled by a sixteen-year-old moves the boundary of who counts as somebody who measures things, which is the same boundary this site keeps finding drawn around who counts as somebody who thinks. The instrument is the argument. And what it detects is the honest version of the phrase we are named for: the muon passing through the reader right now began as a nucleus travelling from something that exploded a very long way away.
A year old, and “under $100” is a component cost This is not news and the lag says how much it is not. Held — a hundred-year correction whose evidence is 652 and 295 days old

Textbooks may have misdrawn this basic brain structure for 100 years

Axons are drawn in every textbook as smooth cylinders. They are not: unmyelinated axons in the mammalian central nervous system carry nanoscopic swellings about 200 nm across, strung along a 60 nm cable like pearls on a string, and the shape is set by membrane mechanics rather than by anything the cell is doing on purpose — disturb the membrane with cholesterol removal or an osmotic change and the pearling changes with it. Neuronal activity shifts membrane cholesterol, which changes the pearls, which slows conduction. It is a lovely result and squarely on-frame: a structure everyone had drawn as uniform turns out to be variable along its length, and the variation is functional. We read that paper in full and held the story anyway, because of when it happened. Griswold et al., Nature Neuroscience 28(1), 49, was published online 2 December 2024 — 652 days before the release that carried it this week. The second paper under the same headline, Eddings et al. in Neuron 114(3), 408, was registered at Crossref on 24 November 2025, 295 days ago. Neither is this week’s news, and a digest whose whole axis is how long did that take to reach you cannot file a 652-day-old finding under a present-tense headline without saying what it is doing. It is not a lesser finding for being old — nothing about an axon changed while the story waited — and it stays on this list rather than moving to the seedbed, because what disqualifies it is the date on the headline and not the work. Read: Griswold et al. in full at PubMed Central. Not read: the Neuron paper — Crossref deposits no abstract and Cell Press is refused.

Held — four semaglutide items in seven days, and the loudest one is female mice

Ozempic may slow aging itself, and three more like it

One feed carried four semaglutide stories inside a week: that it may slow ageing, an analysis of side effects mined from 400,000 Reddit posts, a feature asking whether it can change your life, and the psychiatric-outcomes cohort held here last week. Held as a cluster, because the cluster is the finding. The ageing paper is real and is not what the headline says. Feng et al., Nature 657(8131), 469, published online 2 September 2026: 20-month-old female C57BL/6 mice, treated for three months, showed attenuated hallmarks of ageing and, on continued treatment, longer life, with effects paralleling calorie restriction. The title contains both words the headline drops — Late-life semaglutide treatment slows ageing and extends lifespan in female mice — and one of them is a restriction the authors chose to put in the title, which is not something a science desk gets to quietly generalise away. Scientists find Ozempic may slow aging itself is a sentence about a drug people take. The Reddit study is a different kind of object again — Sehgal et al., Nature Health 1(8), 806, on self-reported side effects in online communities, which is a real and useful method for surfacing what trials miss and is not a measurement of incidence. Four items, three registers, one drug, and a reader who meets all four in a week comes away with something none of them said. Read: publisher-deposited abstracts at Crossref for both papers. Not read: either paper; nature.com is refused.

Held — the paper’s own title says the moon is hypothetical

Venus may have swallowed its own moon

Venus has no moon, and this paper asks what would have happened to one if it had had one: a tidal-evolution survey across spin periods of 5 to 100 hours, moon masses from a hundredth to ten times the Moon’s, two different tidal models. The answer is that survival depends on a race between the satellite migrating outward and the synchronous radius expanding to meet it, and that explaining Venus as it actually is requires both losing a satellite and slowing an initially fast spin — which happens only in a restricted corner of the parameter space. The paper’s title is Tidal Demise: The Evolution and Fate of a Hypothetical Venus Moon, and the word is the authors’. The headline converts a parameter study of an object that has never been observed into an event that happened. This is exactly the trap this page recorded against itself a fortnight ago — parse the title’s actual subject — and here the subject is a hypothetical. There is a good piece somewhere in the fact that a planet’s spin and a missing satellite constrain each other, and it is not a piece about Venus eating anything. Kane, S. R., Selsis, F., Leconte, J. & Raymond, S. N., The Astrophysical Journal 1009(1), 31, online 14 September 2026, lag 2 days. Read: the publisher-deposited abstract at Crossref. Held — an exploratory crossover in 23 men, reported as a fact about men

Smelling chocolate helped men complete more reps without feeling more tired

Twenty-three moderately trained men did leg extensions to exhaustion after sniffing 90% dark chocolate, 60% milk chocolate, or water, in a randomised double-blind crossover. Total repetitions differed by condition, and dark chocolate beat control. The study says what it is in its own title — Chocolate odor enhances resistance exercise performance through appetite suppression in the fasted state: an exploratory study — and every qualifier in that title matters: exploratory, in the fasted state, and a proposed mechanism that is about appetite rather than about muscle. Held — a superb fossil, and a claim about extinction that one specimen cannot carry

A 66-million-year-old feather in dinosaur dung

A three-dimensionally preserved pennaceous feather, recovered from a theropod coprolite in the uppermost Maastrichtian of the Hell Creek Formation, and described by its authors as arguably the best-preserved feather ever recovered from Mesozoic rock. It shows that the modern lightweight stiff feather, with a square medulla-filled rachis, was already in place before the end-Cretaceous extinction; skeletal fragments in the same coprolite suggest it belonged to a hesperornithiform, a flightless diving bird, and its low structural index and high barb density match the water-resistant plumage of living aquatic birds. Tiny gar scales alongside are read as the bird’s last meal. We held it, and not because anything above is doubtful. The abstract closes by proposing that differences in plumage, especially body feathers responsible for thermal insulation, “may have played a role in the extinction of all non-neornithine pennaraptorans” in an impact winter — and that is a claim about which lineages survived a global catastrophe, resting here on one feather in one coprolite. The authors hedge it properly with may; a digest that files it will lose the hedge within one retelling. It is on-frame — variation in insulation as the filter — and it is a seed rather than a finding. O’Connor, J. K. et al., Current Biology, 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2026.08.054. Zine · Star Stuff

The shape that fills everything and repeats nothing

The strongest candidate the scan has produced, and it can be built from one paper read end to end. A single tile shape covers a plane and can only do it without ever repeating; its centres make a point set with threefold symmetry and no mirror; light comes off that set in a pinwheel whose tilt is the golden ratio, and it answers differently to left- and right-handed light. The argument the zine would have to earn, and could: for a century a crystal was a repeating unit, by definition, and then the definition had to change because reality had been ordered some other way the whole time. Order without repetition is not disorder. What the paper adds, and what makes it a Star Stuff piece rather than a metaphor, is that the useful optical behaviour comes from what the field would call defects in a honeycomb — the authors’ own word. What is missing before this is a piece: the Supplementary Information, which carries the symmetry proofs and the self-similarity analysis; and the two Smith et al. papers in Combinatorial Theory, which we have read about and not read — and a zine whose founding fact is a 2023 mathematical discovery cannot cite it from somebody else’s reference list. There is also a hard drawing problem, and this site has written down what it costs to get one wrong: a hat tiling rendered small enough for a spread will read as a honeycomb with smudges unless the tiles are filled rather than stroked, and the pinwheel has to be drawn from the real diffraction data or not at all.

Field guide entry · and a note on our own method

A second kingdom, for the seed that asked for one

Last week’s seed said that a species name covering several ecologically distinct populations was a good line, and that what held it back was that “one paper, however well read, is one paper, and the argument needs a second case from a different kingdom before it is a pattern.” Here is a second case, and it is from a different kingdom. A study of the Adenomera simonstuarti complex — western Amazonian frogs — separates one of nine molecular lineages inside a single named species as a new species on body size, the absence of a band on the forearm, and an unpulsed single-note call; and the new species breeds in seasonally flooded várzea forest, which is unique among the Amazonian members of its genus, all the others being animals of unflooded ground. Its abstract opens on exactly the claim the seed wanted: cryptic diversity inside named species “poses a challenge to traditional taxonomy, causing an underestimation of species richness and incorrect assessments of extinction risk.” Bacteria and frogs is two kingdoms, and the failure mode is identical in both: the name was doing the flattening, and the thing that broke it open was listening. What still holds this at seed: two cases is a pair, not a pattern, and the honest next move is to ask a taxonomist rather than to collect a third from a news feed. Spread · or a Notes page

A field publishing its own distribution of belief

Held above for want of a version of record, and carried here because the object is more interesting than the item. A large survey run through the American Physical Society’s own magazine asked physicists what they actually think about cosmology, black holes, quantum mechanics and quantum gravity, and found that several positions described publicly as the field’s consensus are held by narrow majorities or by pluralities. That is not a story about physicists being confused. It is a field measuring the spread of its own opinion and publishing the spread — which is the exact opposite of the move this site names as scientism, where the authority is kept and the practice dropped. A discipline that prints its own disagreement is doing the thing properly, and there is no equivalent instrument pointed at any of the fields that make claims about us.
