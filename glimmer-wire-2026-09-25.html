The fifth edition of Glimmer Wire, the scan that sits behind Glimmers. Five papers were read end to end — four of them versions of record, one of them the authors’ own preprint, which is not the same thing and is not graded as though it were. Items are sorted by lag, which runs from 2 days to 157, median 85. What that bought is five papers read end to end, and the distinction between them matters. Four are versions of record, all four at PubMed Central: the PNAS paper on magnetism in the oldest solids in the solar system, the Nature Communications paper on a leukaemia drug’s mechanism, the Nature Communications paper on three genomes of Clusia, and the Communications Biology paper on gentoo penguins. One grade needs saying plainly, because the key below can be read as a ladder and it is not one. The gentoo penguin paper was read in full, cover to cover, and is graded contested. Nothing about that grade is a comment on our access: the paper itself reports that the question it answers has been “widely debated since the 1920s, with ongoing disagreement on the number of unique lineages and their taxonomic rank,” and it reaches its answer partly by demoting a species another team formally described. Verified describes what we did. Contested describes where the claim stands.

Lag 2 d

The belief that difference destabilises a system turns out to be an artefact of the shortcut that made the maths possible

For decades the working understanding in network dynamics has been that heterogeneity among nodes inhibits stability — that a network of oscillators synchronises best when its members are nearly identical. The authors’ first move is to point out that this sits awkwardly beside the world: the natural and engineered networks that actually hold together are heterogeneous almost without exception. Their result is that the received conclusion “arises from model reductions introduced for mathematical tractability and breaks down when nodal dynamics are higher-dimensional.” Collapse each node to a single phase variable — the Kuramoto reduction, and the master stability function built on it — and you get a mathematically tractable model whose Jacobian is well behaved and in which sameness wins. Let each node keep more than one dimension, so the Jacobian is non-Hermitian, and the sign flips: heterogeneity enhances stability, “even when parameters are randomly disordered.” They show it for neural, power-grid and material networks, and show that in one-dimensional dynamics the same stabilisation can be bought instead through nonreciprocal interactions — an interaction that is not the same in both directions. The abstract’s closing sentence is the claim: disorder is revealed “not as a liability but as a general resource for stabilizing complex systems.”

The star stuff frame The interesting part is not the conclusion, it is the shape of the error. Nobody chose to believe that uniformity is what holds a system together. Somebody chose a simplification, because the honest problem was too hard to compute; the simplification was published, taught, and built into the standard tool; and a generation later the assumption inside it was being read back out as a finding about nature. The paper says so in its own words — the models were reduced for tractability, and reduced models “may misrepresent the impact of parameter choices and network structures.” That is the same shape as Five Sigma, No. 40, where a threshold chosen in 1925 with the word convenient hardened into a fact about evidence — and it is arguably the more dangerous version, because a threshold announces that somebody picked it and a model reduction does not.
What this is about, and the grade This is a result about coupled dynamical systems, and it is not a result about people. The nodes are oscillators, buses on a grid and species in a Lotka–Volterra community; the stability is linear stability at an equilibrium; the disorder is dispersion in nodal parameters. Nothing in it licenses a sentence about human variation, and the closest the paper comes to biology is a class of ecological models, where the authors say disordered networks offer “a natural route toward resolving May’s longstanding complexity-stability paradox.” That is a claim about a fifty-year-old mathematical puzzle, not about an ecosystem anybody has measured. Graded plausible, not verified, and the reason is the rule and not the reading. science.org answers with 403 and doi.org is refused, so what was read is the authors’ arXiv posting — their text, complete, including the propositions and proofs, and still not the version of record. This page wrote that rule down on 11 September and is now applying it for the second week running to a paper it would have liked to file higher.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed, and the honest answer is half spent. The conclusion is already the site’s: The Universe Runs on Difference, No. 8, argues that variety is resilience and monoculture the fragile engineered thing, and monoculture appears on 41 pages here. What is unspent is the mechanism of the mistake: a simplification adopted so the equations would close, mistaken afterwards for a property of the world. No page here contains Kuramoto, complexity-stability or power grid, checked by grep. That is a How We Got Here chain rather than a Star Stuff zine, and its first joint — the reduction was introduced for tractability — would be marked documented, because the authors document it.

Lag 15 d

The people being diagnosed now look more like everybody else — which is a fact about the boundary, not about us

A matched case-control study built on the Danish national registers: 2,194,951 children and adolescents born between 1994 and 2022, of whom 71,317 were diagnosed with ADHD or autism before eighteen between 2012 and 2022, each matched to ten controls on sex, birth year, municipality and country of birth — 713,170 of them. Nineteen prediagnostic characteristics were tested: parental and family factors, birth and perinatal factors, health care use. Diagnosed young people differed from controls on all nineteen, and the differences shrank, year by year, across the decade. Low birth weight ran at an odds ratio of 1.54 (95% CI 1.41–1.68) in 2012–13 and 1.17 (1.10–1.24) in 2020–22. The attenuation is much stronger for ADHD than for autism — household income moves 6.5% a year (95% CI 5.7–7.3) for ADHD against 0.7% (−0.1 to 1.5) for autism, an interval that includes zero — and stronger for those diagnosed at 10 to 17 than for younger children.

The star stuff frame The finding, stated as the paper states it, is that the boundary moved. The characteristics that used to mark out the diagnosed population are marking it out less and less, which is what happens when a threshold is drawn in a different place, not what happens when a population changes. We write from inside this, and the version of the story that reaches us is usually overdiagnosis — an accusation that some of us are not really what we say we are. The paper closes off that reading itself, in its own last sentence: the results “should not be interpreted as evidence that ADHD or ASD have become less impairing at the individual level.” That clause is the whole item. A person newly inside the boundary is not a person who was fine all along; they are a person who was outside it, and the old line is what needed explaining.
The words the paper uses, and the ones it is careful about Its vocabulary is not ours and is worth naming rather than quietly translating. The nineteen characteristics are called “well-defined risk factors,” and the outcome is a diagnosis; a study designed this way cannot say anything about the people it counts except that they were counted. The causal claim is carefully hedged and the hedge is the paper’s own: increasing rates “may reflect changes in diagnostic practices or health care capacity rather than changes in underlying risk alone” — alone being a word that survives into no headline anywhere. This is one country with one health system and universal registers, which is exactly why the data exist and exactly why the result may not travel. And the ADHD/autism asymmetry is a real number with a real interval: the autism figure of 0.7% a year has a confidence interval running through zero, so “autism attenuated more slowly” is supported and “autism attenuated” on its own is not.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed. overdiagnosis and diagnostic expansion appear on no page here, by grep; diagnostic criteria appears on ten. The unspent argument is not about prevalence at all — it is that a diagnostic boundary is a decision that can be watched moving in registry data, in a way a trait cannot. That belongs beside The Name Was Already the Verdict, No. 88, where a category did the work and the evidence underneath it was assembled afterwards.

Lag 31 d

The oldest solids in the solar system are still holding a magnetic field, and it is stronger than the one you are standing in

Calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions — CAIs, millimetre-scale white flecks in chondrite meteorites, the oldest known solar system solids — carry a remanent magnetisation, and the authors measure what field they cooled in. The answer is roughly 150 to 600 µT, against the few tens of microtesla at the Earth’s surface today. The question that number settles is which force was moving matter in the first act of the solar system: the paper opens by saying it “is unknown whether magnetism and/or gravity dominantly drove accretion in the youngest evolutionary stages” of protoplanetary disks, and closes by reporting an intensity “consistent with magnetic fields playing a key role driving disk accretion while also heating the very inner disk to 103 K at the earliest stages of solar system formation.” More than 90% of a star’s mass is accreted in the embedded phase these grains date from, and the disk at that stage is invisible: wrapped in an infalling envelope, unobservable by telescope. So the only surviving instrument pointed at it is the rock itself.

The star stuff frame This is the founding claim of the whole collection, arriving as an instrument reading rather than as a metaphor. The atoms were made in stars; these particular flecks are the first things that were ever solid after that, and they have been carrying a record of the conditions they condensed in for four and a half billion years without being asked to. A magnetic field is not a thing that leaves fossils — and it left one, in the only material old enough to have been there. What the grains preserve is not their composition but the state of the room, which is a different and stranger kind of inheritance.
The error bar is two-thirds of the number, and the paper prints it The headline range comes out of a chain of corrections, and the paper’s own corrected figure is 324.2 ± 218.3 µT — an uncertainty of about 67% of the value, wider than most of the intervals this page has flagged. The lower bound of that interval still clears the Earth’s present field, which is what makes the qualitative claim survive the quantitative wobble, and it is the kind of distinction that vanishes in one retelling. Two conditionals in the paper are load-bearing and are the authors’: “If we assume that the cooling rates measured for CAIs apply to the CAIs studied here,” the measurements are a near-instantaneous record of the field; and the age of that record is the age of the last event that heated the grains above the iron-nickel magnetic blocking temperature, which need not be their formation. The result is also “consistent with” magnetism driving accretion — support for a hypothesis, not the elimination of gravity.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed. supernova appears on 30 pages and nebula on 11, but calcium-aluminum, protoplanetary, solar nebula and presolar appear on none of them. The site has said repeatedly where the atoms were forged and has never once described the room they arrived into — a disk too young to see, too hot to survive, with a field strong enough to move the mass around. That is a Star Stuff register piece with one settled measurement at its centre, and it wants the wide error bar on its face rather than in its colophon.

Lag 85 d

Blocking the enzyme did nothing. Removing the protein did everything. Those were never the same experiment

6-thioguanine has been given to people since the 1950s, for leukaemia, autoimmune conditions and inflammatory bowel disease. A genome-wide screen had flagged an enzyme called NUDT5 as a mediator of its toxicity, and knocking the gene out makes cells resistant — the authors reproduce that, a four-fold increase in the concentration needed to kill half the cells. The obvious inference is that NUDT5’s catalytic activity is doing the work. So they tested it with two chemically distinct inhibitors, and report the result in the flattest possible words: “Contrary to expectations, neither inhibitor was able to phenocopy the NUDT5 KO.” They then built degraders — molecules that destroy the protein instead of blocking it — and those did reproduce the knockout. The confirming experiment is the one that settles it: they put a catalytically dead mutant back into the knockout cells, and sensitivity to the drug came back. The protein does not need to work. It needs to be there. Their generalisation is a sentence about method rather than about this molecule: “for some enzymes, phenotypes attributed to catalytic activity may instead reflect protein-dependent functions that are selectively interrogated by targeted degradation.”

The star stuff frame An enzyme is defined by what it catalyses, so for as long as the only available tool was an inhibitor, the only available question was what does its activity do? — and an unasked question comes back looking exactly like a settled one. The correction here is not that anybody reasoned badly; it is that a new instrument made a second question askable, and the second question had a different answer. This site has a standing line about a finding’s absence being a fact about where attention went. This is the sharper version: the absence was a fact about what could be done in a dish, and no amount of care would have found it with the older tool.
The headline is about a drug; the paper is about a protein “Scientists thought they knew how this 70-year-old leukemia drug worked” overstates the subject, and the subject is the whole thing. 6-thioguanine’s own mechanism — a prodrug metabolised and incorporated into DNA and RNA — is not what changed; the paper describes it that way itself. What changed is the account of one modifier of the cellular response. That is a real correction and a narrower one than a reader of the headline would carry away. Everything here is in cell lines: HAP1, derived from a chronic myelogenous leukaemia line, and HL-60. The clinical sentence in the abstract is properly conditional — depletion of NUDT5 is antagonistic to NUDT15 inhibition, “suggesting a distinct mode-of-action with potential implications for patient therapy” — and no patient has been treated on the strength of it. One of the two probes used, MRK-952, is listed in the paper as to be published, so its selectivity is not independently checkable from here.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed. mechanism of action appears on no page here and enzyme on eleven. The transferable line — switching a thing off and taking it away are two different experiments, and for most of the history of a field only one of them was possible — is unspent, and it generalises past biochemistry in a direction this site cares about, because a great deal of what gets said about accommodations is an inhibitor experiment reported as a knockout. That is a spread rather than a whole piece, and it would need the biology kept literal, because the moment it becomes a metaphor it stops being evidence.

Lag 140 d

One kind of tree, three different ways of breathing, growing side by side

In 1800 Alexander von Humboldt held a leaf of a tropical tree under water and noticed it doing something odd. The something was crassulacean acid metabolism: opening the stomata at night, fixing carbon dioxide into an acid in the dark, and releasing it internally by day with the pores shut — photosynthesis for a plant that cannot afford to lose the water. This paper sequences and compares three Clusia genomes chosen to span the range: C. major (weak CAM), C. minor (inducible, switching as conditions demand) and C. rosea (strong CAM). The word the field uses for these is physiotypes, and the genus is famous for holding them all — the reference the paper leans on, by one of its own co-authors, is titled “One morphotype, three physiotypes: sympatric species of Clusia with obligate C3 photosynthesis, obligate CAM and C3-CAM intermediate behaviour” (Lüttge, 1999). Sympatric means growing in the same place. One morphotype means you could not tell them apart by looking. The genomic finding is that all three are diploidised genomes of polyploid origin, and that whole-genome duplication followed by transposon-mediated diploidisation may have shaped the CAM gene families — particularly the ones governing how leaf starch is broken down to supply the acceptor molecule the night chemistry runs on.

The star stuff frame Twelve field guides on this site arrive at there is no standard star, nest, egg, shark, way to rest. Here is the same sentence about the most basic thing a plant does. These trees are the same shape, in the same soil, in the same light, and they are running three different metabolisms — and the intermediate one is not a halfway house or a failure to commit, it is a plant that keeps both options and chooses by conditions. The literature the paper cites calls CAM a continuum rather than a category. It is unusually hard to read that as deficit, because there is no baseline: the question which of these three is the normal Clusia has no answer, and 1999 is when somebody wrote that down.
Three genomes, and a mechanism the paper marks as speculation The causal claim is hedged twice by the authors and once by the journal. Polyploidisation and diploidisation “could have shaped” the CAM gene families; whole-genome duplication “might have driven” the diversification; and the editors’ own summary line says the authors “speculate” about it. That is three genomes from a genus of several hundred species, a comparison rather than a test, and it establishes a genomic framework rather than a history. What is not hedged is the phenotype. The three physiotypes have been measured and argued about since the 1990s, and the titratable-acidity work in this paper is a fresh drought experiment on the same individuals well-watered and then at 10% soil water. The genomes are new; the diversity was already there, and had been since Humboldt.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed. crassulacean appears on no page here; stomata on three, one of which is Measured Against a Tree, No. 73, which uses stomata as part of the tally a moss fails when it is measured against a vascular plant. That is the same argument from the other end, and the two would sit together: the moss fails a standard it was never built to, and Clusia demonstrates that the standard was not one thing to begin with. A field-guide entry, with an eye on the guide count being re-derived from the pages rather than quoted — our own notes record that the obvious grep for it returns one more file than the true answer.

Lag 150 d

A penguin described this year from a skin collected in 1897, and another that stopped being a species two years after it started

Gentoo penguins have been handled as one species, Pygoscelis papua, across the whole Southern Ocean. This paper reports four divergent lineages — northern, southern, southeastern, eastern — separating around 0.8 million years ago, with lineage-specific signals of positive selection in genes for thermoregulation, oxygen transport, metabolism and skeletal development, and with the Antarctic Polar Front doing much of the separating. It proposes recognising all four as species, and formally describes one: Pygoscelis kerguelensis, the southeastern gentoo. The holotype is AMNH SKIN 525824, an adult male collected by Robert Hall at Kerguelen Island on 31 December 1897. The paratypes were collected in 1888. The animal being described as new this year has been in a museum in New York for the better part of 130 years, and the measurements that distinguish it — culmen length, bill height at the gonys angle, flipper width, tarsus, middle toe — could have been taken off that skin at any point since.

The star stuff frame Read the same paper again and it does the opposite thing in the same breath. The South Georgia gentoo was formally described as its own species, P. poncetii, by another team, and this paper demotes it to a subspecies, P. e. poncetii. So within a very short span one population is a species, and then is not, and the birds did not move. The paper is admirably plain about where that leaves things: the taxonomy of these lineages “has been widely debated since the 1920s, with ongoing disagreement on the number of unique lineages and their taxonomic rank,” and there were four morphological revisions before anybody had a genome. The rank is a judgement. The divergence is a measurement. Those are two different objects and only one of them is in the animal.
The headline is refuted by this paper’s own reference list, and the grade is about standing “Scientists discover first new penguin species in more than 100 years” is checkably wrong, and the check is one page of the paper being described. Its introduction states that a sixth lineage from South Georgia “was later formally described as the South Georgia gentoo (P. poncetii),” by Tyler et al. — a description well inside the last hundred years, by people in this same field, cited by these same authors, and demoted three sections later. A desk that read as far as the introduction could not have written the headline. Graded contested, having been read in full, because a species proposal is a proposal until the checklists move: the authors say the disagreement is live, they resolve it one way, and the previous resolution went the other. Two further limits are theirs: molecular data for the new species come from Kerguelen alone, with Heard Island included on satellite tracking of juveniles rather than on genetics; and Macquarie Island birds were in the phylogeny and assignment analyses only. The niche projections — severe habitat loss for three lineages, possible expansion for the southern — are models of the future and are not a finding about the present.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed, and it is a third case for a line this page already opened, with one correction to make about it. Last week’s seed wanted cryptic diversity inside a name, and had bacteria and frogs — two kingdoms. Penguins and frogs are the same kingdom, so this is a third case and still a second kingdom, and the pattern the seed asked for is not closed by it. It is also a different claim: the frogs and the bacteria are about diversity a name hid, and the gentoos are about a boundary being re-cut, twice, in opposite directions, inside a decade. Field Guide No. 25 sorts eleven ways a name comes off a piece of work; nothing here sorts the ways a name gets drawn around a living thing. lumping appears in no page of the site, only in an archived changelog entry.

Lag 157 d

They scrambled their own gravity data for a decade so they could not tune it toward the answer they expected

Big G, the constant that sets the strength of gravity everywhere, is the worst-measured of the fundamental constants: precise experiments disagree with each other by far more than their stated uncertainties allow. This paper is the first replication of a high-precision measurement of G — not a new method, deliberately, but the same torsion balance, built at the International Bureau of Weights and Measures about three decades ago and rebuilt and re-run at NIST with its modifications documented. The result is G = (6.67387 ± 0.00038) × 10−11 m3 kg−1 s−2, a relative standard uncertainty of 5.7 × 10−5, lower than the original BIPM value by 2.5 parts in ten thousand. The authors call it “an independent verification” of that determination and a contribution to “assessing the reproducibility limits of current experimental techniques.” The part everyone reported is the part that is not in the abstract at all: the analysis was blinded, the key to unscrambling the data sealed in an envelope, and the envelope not opened for about ten years.

The star stuff frame This site has a working paper on the difference between science and scientism — the authority kept and the practice dropped. The envelope is the practice, in its least glamorous and most convincing form. A blind analysis exists because the people doing it know perfectly well that they will, without meaning to and without noticing, stop adjusting when the number looks right; so they arrange in advance to be unable to know what right looks like, and then live with that for a decade. And having done it, they published a number that disagrees with the one they were replicating. A field that measures its own worst constant, in public, with its own hands tied, is not a field in trouble. It is the only kind that can be believed when it says something else.
The best detail in this item is not from the paper, and we did not read the paper The sealed envelope is the release’s, not the abstract’s. The Crossref deposit says nothing about blinding; the ten-year interval, the envelope and the physicist not being sure he wanted to open it all come from the NIST-sourced news write-up, and are marked here as that. The paper is CC BY 4.0 — openly licensed, freely reusable, and unreachable from here, because iopscience.iop.org, doi.org and www.nist.gov are all refused at the network layer and no preprint or repository copy was found. An open-access paper we cannot open is still a paper we have not read, and this is the second consecutive week that has happened. Two things not to carry: this is a replication of one determination with one apparatus, which is a narrower and more useful claim than “a new value of G”; and a disagreement of 2.5 × 10−4 against a stated uncertainty of 5.7 × 10−5 is roughly four times the uncertainty — our arithmetic on the abstract’s two numbers, not a figure the abstract states, and not something to lean on without the paper.
Where it goes Checked rather than assumed. gravitational constant, blind analysis and fundamental constant appear on no page here; pre-registration appears on five, all of them about clinical and educational research, and reproducib on two, one of which is The Sky Was Not Regular Enough, No. 50. What is unspent is the positive case: this page and No. 40 are good at naming the discipline that got dropped, and have never once shown it being kept. It pairs with the physicists’ consensus survey seeded last week — a field publishing the spread of its own belief, and a field publishing a number it could not steer — and together those are a Notes page rather than two items.

Held

Six this week, and the pattern in them is a single one: five of the six have a headline whose central word is not in the paper, and in two cases the paper’s own abstract argues against it. The sixth is a 2024 paper wearing a 2026 date. Two of the six are papers we would have liked to file, and one of those is held for a reason that has nothing to do with its quality. Nothing here was invented to fill the section, and nothing looked at and declined has been quietly omitted.

Held — a 2024 paper, 631 days old at the shortest reading

Scientists solve a 50-year mystery and discover a new human blood group

An antigen called AnWj, first identified in 1972, sat unexplained for half a century while two candidate genes were proposed and neither held. Whole-exome sequencing of the few people with the rare inherited AnWj-negative phenotype found no shared mutation in either candidate — and instead homozygosity for the same large exonic deletion in MAL. Overexpressing the Mal protein in an erythroid cell line produced the antigen with or without the other candidate present, which the authors call “necessary and sufficient”; the result “forms the basis of a new blood group system.” This is squarely a piece we would have written. A handful of people whose blood does not match a category, treated as an anomaly for fifty years, turn out to be the evidence that the category was incomplete — and the clinical consequence runs the right way, because the antibody they can make is one that matters in transfusion. It is held because it is not this week’s. Tilley et al. appeared in Blood 144(26), 2735, in the issue of 26 December 2024, and the DOI was registered on 19 August 2024. Against a wire date of 18 September 2026 that is 631 days on the issue and 760 on the registration, and Crossref deposits no online-first date to split the difference. Either figure is larger than anything on the Filed axis above, and larger than the 652-day hold that led this section last week. A digest whose whole axis is how long did that take to reach you cannot file a two-year-old result under a present-tense headline without saying what it is doing. Read: the publisher-deposited abstract at Crossref, in full. Not read: the paper; doi.org is refused and no PubMed Central record was pursued once the date settled it.

Held — the abstract’s last sentence contradicts the headline

Stonehenge’s 6-ton altar stone may have traveled from Scotland by glacier

The Altar Stone came from the Orcadian Basin in northeast Scotland, some 700 km from Salisbury Plain, and the live question is how. This paper combines sandstone provenance analysis with ice-flow modelling and finds the two lines of evidence pulling against each other: the candidate sources whose position suits southward glacial dispersal are the ones that match the detrital zircon ages worst, and Caithness, which matches the zircons best, sits where the modelled ice went predominantly north-east. A localised south-eastward pathway exists, to Dogger Bank — which would cut the human carry from 700 km to 400 — and Dogger Bank was under the sea before the stone plausibly reached Stonehenge. The abstract ends: “Even under a glacially assisted scenario, substantial anthropogenic transport would have remained necessary.” The headline reports the hypothesis the paper spends its length constraining, and drops the constraint. Held for that, and it is the cleanest example this page has collected of a finding and its coverage facing opposite directions — the paper is a careful argument that glaciers cannot do the job alone, published under a sentence saying a glacier may have done the job. Held — the paper’s title says attendance; the headline says mourned; n = 1

Humpback whale mother may have mourned her dead calf for days

A female humpback was observed off the southern Gold Coast in 2025 with an escort, having apparently given birth to a dead calf, and stayed with the body — close, below the surface, positioned beside it — for several hours and possibly longer. The paper is titled “First documentation of humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae) post-mortem attendance of a stillborn.” Post-mortem attendance is what was observed. Mourning is an inference about an inner life, and it is not the paper’s word. The researcher does use grief language in the release — the encounter “raises the possibility” that humpback mothers may experience it, and the behaviours “indicated an inherent, strong maternal attachment” — so this is a scientist speculating in public, which is allowed, rather than a desk inventing. Held on three counts: it is a single observation of a single animal, offered as a first documentation and nothing more; the duration in the headline is itself uncertain in the source, where days are a maybe; and this site’s more-than-human writing is built on taking animals’ own umwelten seriously, which cuts against borrowing a human word to make an observation legible rather than describing what the animal did. It is a good record and we hope somebody gets a second one. Read: the title, authors, journal and date, from Crossref — no abstract is deposited. Not read: the paper; link.springer.com is refused. Everything above about the observation comes from the release and is the release’s.

Held — a sound paper, and not one this page can carry without rebuilding its frame

Rare pregnancy infections may triple the risk of autism

A Swedish register cohort of 3,666,002 people born between 1987 and 2021, with sibling comparisons, of whom 975 — about 0.027% — had a diagnosed congenital TORCH infection. Hazard ratios: intellectual disability 7.22 (95% CI 6.14–8.49), autism 3.10 (2.55–3.76), and the sibling models give the same or larger figures, which is a real strength because it controls for what families share. The number the headline does not reach is inside the paper: autism with co-occurring intellectual disability runs at HR 6.23, and autism without at 1.97. Those are different enough that “triple the risk of autism” is an average across two quite different findings. Associations with ADHD attenuated in the sibling analyses — the confounding control moved the answer — and no consistent association was seen for OCD. Held, and the reason is ours rather than the paper’s. Its outcome variable is a diagnosis treated as injury, alongside severe intellectual disability and lower school grades; that is a legitimate design for a question about preventing vertically transmitted infection, and it is not a frame this page can lift a figure out of without importing it whole. The release is better than the headline it got: the study’s own author says these infections “account for a very small proportion of all cases of autism in the population,” a sentence that made it into the release and not into the title above it. Sjöqvist, H., Dalman, C., Mataix-Cols, D., Gardner, R. M. & Karlsson, H., JAMA Pediatrics, online 21 September 2026, DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2026.4229, lag 2 days. Read: the publisher-deposited structured abstract at Crossref, in full, and every figure above is from it. Not read: the paper.

Held — 83 is a count of hits in a scan of 1,049 outcomes

More REM sleep linked to lower risk of 83 diseases

Wrist accelerometer data from 95,559 UK Biobank participants, sleep stages derived by algorithm, median follow-up 8.9 years, and a phenome-wide association analysis across 1,049 health outcomes. More REM was associated with lower risk of 83 of them; more deep sleep with 7; irregularity and wakefulness after sleep onset with elevated risk of 3 and 6. Eighty-three is not a list of benefits, it is the yield of a scan — a hypothesis-generating instrument whose output is “these are the places to look next,” and which is reported here as though the count were the finding. The paper is honest about what it is; its own stated main limitation is “its observational design, which remains susceptible to residual confounding and precludes causal inference,” and its actual conclusion is the modest one, that 6–8 hours is associated with better sleep architecture and lower risk. Held also for the direction nobody mentions: illness degrades REM sleep, often years before it is diagnosed, so an association between less REM and more disease is exactly what reverse causation would produce, and an accelerometer worn for one week cannot tell the two apart. Held with some regret, because this is a genuinely good use of a big cohort and it is CC BY. Luo, J., Liu, R., Yin, J., Cao, W., Sun, S. & Chen, R., PLOS Medicine 23(9), e1005213, online 17 September 2026, DOI 10.1371/journal.pmed.1005213, lag 6 days. Read: the publisher-deposited abstract at Crossref, in full. Not read: the paper, which is openly licensed and was not pursued to full text once the design settled the hold.

Held — two progenitors is not two organs

Stanford scientists discover the human brain may actually be two separate organs

The paper is titled “Two parallel neural ectoderm progenitors contribute to the developing brain.” Two progenitor populations contributing to one developing organ is a claim about lineage — where the cells came from — and “two separate organs” is a claim about anatomy, which the title does not make and which nobody who has held a brain would make. Held on the title alone, which is all we have: Crossref deposits no abstract, nature.com answers with an authentication redirect, and no PubMed Central record exists. This is the thinnest hold on the page and it is recorded rather than dropped, because a story declined for a reason we can state in one sentence still belongs in the list — and because a 24-author paper from four laboratories is very likely a substantial piece of work being sold badly. It is worth chasing when it reaches an archive. Jokhai, R. T., Dundes, C. E., Ahsan, H. S. et al. (24 authors; Loh, K. M., last), Nature Neuroscience, online 18 September 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41593-026-02433-7, lag 3 days. Read: the title, author list, journal and date, from Crossref. Seedbed

Three seeds, all three off papers read in full, which is a first. Plus the leads nobody opened — including, honestly, all four of last week’s.

Zine · Star Stuff, or a How We Got Here chain

The bird was already in the drawer

The strongest thing the scan produced, and it can be built from one paper read cover to cover. A penguin is formally described as a new species in 2026 from a skin a man collected at Kerguelen on 31 December 1897, with paratypes from 1888; the measurements that distinguish it are measurements of a museum object that has been available the whole time. In the same paper, a population that was made a species a couple of years ago is made a subspecies again. The argument the zine would have to earn, and could: a name is a decision somebody makes about where a boundary goes, and it moves in both directions, and the animal is unaffected either way — so a reader who has been told that a diagnosis, a category or a label is a discovery about them is entitled to ask who drew it and when it last moved. What is missing before this is a piece: the supplementary tables carrying the morphological discrimination; Tyler et al., the 2020s description of P. poncetii, which we know only through this paper’s summary of it and which a zine about taxonomic rank cannot cite at one remove; and the current state of the two major bird checklists, which is the actual test of whether four species is now the answer or one team’s proposal. There is also a drawing problem and this site has written down what getting one wrong costs. Four gentoo lineages differ mostly in size — the paper says size is the key delimiter — and four penguins drawn at spread scale differing by a few millimetres of culmen will read as four identical penguins. The honest figure is the map with the Polar Front on it, or the type specimen itself with its collection date, not a row of birds.

From the Communications Biology paper filed above, read in full at PubMed Central. Not obtained: Supplementary Tables 9 and 13; Tyler et al. on P. poncetii; any checklist authority’s current treatment.

Field guide entry

There is no standard way to breathe

Twelve of the field guides on this site arrive at there is no standard something. Here is the sentence about respiration, and it arrives with a 1999 title already written for it: one morphotype, three physiotypes — Clusia trees of the same appearance, growing in the same place, one running ordinary C3 photosynthesis, one running full CAM with its pores shut all day, one switching between them as the water allows. The entry writes itself around the fact that the intermediate is not a failure to commit: a plant that keeps both routes and picks by conditions is doing something neither specialist can do, and the literature has moved to calling CAM a continuum rather than a category. What still holds this at seed: the guide it would join has a count in its prose, and this site’s own notes record that the obvious grep for that count returns one more file than the true answer, so the set has to be re-derived and read hit by hit before anything is added to it. And Lüttge’s 1999 paper — the source of the phrase the entry would be built on — has not been opened; we have its title from the 2026 paper’s reference list, which is exactly the one-remove citation this site keeps catching itself at.

Notes page · and it now has two legs

The envelope, and the survey: two pictures of a field behaving

Held over from last week, and this week it acquired its second case, which is what it was waiting for. The seed of 18 September was a survey in which physicists published the distribution of their own belief and found that several positions described publicly as consensus are held by narrow majorities. Beside it now: a team that scrambled a decade of gravity data against a key sealed in an envelope, so that they could not, even by accident, stop adjusting when the number looked right — and then published a value that disagrees with the one they set out to replicate. This site is fluent in naming the discipline that got dropped — No. 40 on a threshold chosen for convenience, Too Good to Check on how a fact goes wrong here — and it has never once shown the discipline being kept. A Notes page that did would be worth more than another correction, because the corrections only mean something if somebody can point at what the alternative looks like. Not

Not a citation. Five papers were read end to end this week and the standing rule is untouched. Nothing filed here may be promoted to a zine, a Glimmers entry or a card without the primary source in hand, a changelog entry and its row in the fact-check ledger. For the two items resting on a publisher’s deposited abstract, lifting a figure out and printing it elsewhere would be laundering a deposit into a fact — and for the Science item, a preprint is the authors’ text and not the record, which is why it is graded below what it was read at.

Not “science says you are valid.” This is the week that temptation is sharpest, so it goes first. A paper in Science concluding that disorder is “a general resource for stabilizing complex systems” is about oscillators, power buses and Lotka–Volterra species; it says nothing whatever about people, and quoting it as though it did would be the most flattering mistake available to us. A finding that makes disbelieved people credible also makes them revocable. The belonging claim was never waiting on a result about non-Hermitian Jacobians, and it does not move if the next paper narrows this one.

Not a deficit. Seventy-one thousand young people in Denmark came to resemble their peers more closely over ten years, and that is a measurement of where a line was drawn, not of who was behind it. We write from inside this. The paper’s own last sentence refuses the reading that the diagnoses have become less serious, and we carry that sentence rather than the headline that dropped it.

Not a superpower either. Three physiotypes in one tree genus, four lineages of one penguin, a protein whose presence matters and whose activity does not: these are facts about architecture, history and range, never about merit. There is no best way to breathe in the Clusia paper and no ranking of gentoos in the penguin one, and inventing either would be the deficit frame with the sign flipped.

Not a cure list. Eighty-three diseases and one week of a wrist accelerometer is held as what it is, a scan. A hazard ratio computed across a national register is a statement about a population, and it becomes advice only by being addressed to a person who was never in the analysis.

Not anti-science. Two items here are corrections of long-held positions and neither is an argument against the field that held them. A model reduction was adopted because the honest problem could not be computed; an enzyme was studied with an inhibitor because that was the tool that existed. Both were corrected from the inside, by the same discipline, using instruments it built for the purpose — and the gravity item is a laboratory tying its own hands for ten years so its answer could not be steered. If a draft off this digest starts reading as a case against evidence, it has gone wrong.

Not because of. The oldest solids in the solar system are holding a field stronger than the one you are standing in. That is a rhyme, not a proof. The universe declining to pathologize its own variation is why the phrase carries — down to your star stuff measures how far the loving reaches, never why it is owed.
