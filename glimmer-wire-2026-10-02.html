A weekly read of what is going well in science and medicine, graded before it is enjoyed.

The sixth edition of Glimmer Wire, the scan that sits behind Glimmers. Six papers were read end to end, all six of them versions of record — the best week this page has had, and the reason is one open-access repository answering on the second attempt rather than any new skill of ours. Items are sorted by lag, which runs from 4 days to 91, median 20: less than a quarter of last week’s, because four of the eight reached us inside ten days. Five are graded verified, one contested, two plausible. The section that matters most is Held, which lists what we declined and why, and this week it contains a kind of refusal this page has not had to write before: an index offered us a summary written by a language model, in the slot where an abstract goes, correctly labelled and entirely convincing.

Three of those refusals sit in front of papers that are openly licensed — the stick-insect revision is CC BY at Wiley, the turtle tracking is CC BY at Frontiers, and the bat phylogeny is CC BY at Nature. An open licence is a permission, not a road.

Two host behaviours from last week repeated exactly, and one is worth the byte count again. A wrong search path at PubMed Central still returns the PMC home page at HTTP 200 — 67,236 bytes, the identical figure recorded on 25 September, delivered four times over for four different queries. A week apart, two different sets of search terms, the same byte count to the digit: that is the tell, and the status code is not. And PMC again served Google’s reCAPTCHA challenge at 200 — a 20,420-byte document whose first line is a <base> pointing at google.com/recaptcha — for every article requested on the first attempt. Retrying with a different user agent returned all four papers in full. The right route was open the whole time; the first knock was answered by a doorman.

The new one is worse than either, and it is the reason this edition holds a story it wanted. Where a publisher has elided an abstract, Semantic Scholar’s API returns "abstract": null and, beside it, a tldr field containing fluent, specific, paragraph-shaped prose about the paper. It is generated by a language model; the API says so, naming the model in the same object. For the Nature paper on zhelestid mammals that text was the only summary available anywhere we could reach, and it reads exactly like an abstract. It is not a source, and nothing about the response would stop a tool — or a person skimming — from treating it as one. The field is honestly labelled, which is to the archive’s credit and is the entire defence; the laundering here would be structural rather than anybody’s fault. A status code is not a source, a byte count is not a page, and now: a well-formed field in the right place is not an abstract. That paper is held.

Four at PubMed Central — the Science Advances paper on Enceladus’ ice grains, the Science Advances paper on children in early Christian burials, the Autism Research paper on Phelan–McDermid prevalence, and the Integrative Organismal Biology paper on blacktip sharks. Lag 4 d

Every grain of Enceladus is a different ocean, and the difference is made on the way out

Enceladus sprays its subsurface ocean into space, and Cassini’s Cosmic Dust Analyzer spent thirteen years tasting the spray. The salt-rich grains — Type 3 — have always been read as frozen droplets of ocean water, so the expectation was that they would all taste much the same. Across 961 individual mass spectra they do not: the authors find at least five compositional subtypes, dominated variously by sodium chloride, sodium carbonate or bicarbonate, sodium phosphate, sodium hydroxide, potassium chloride or potassium hydroxide — and in sodium-rich grains, chloride features and carbonate features are almost never present together. Freezing experiments and thermodynamic modelling supply the mechanism, and it is entirely about how the sample was taken. Different salts precipitate at different temperatures: phosphates first at around −1 °C, carbonates from about −4 °C, chlorides and hydroxides last, near −23 °C. In a droplet that freezes slowly — the paper’s condition is below 20 kelvins a minute, in droplets of tens to hundreds of micrometres — those events are separated in time, so the salts end up separated in space inside one grain. The droplets are then accelerated past 100 metres a second up narrow ice vents, collide repeatedly with the walls, and shatter. Each fragment that reaches the instrument carries mostly whichever salt happened to occupy its corner of the parent drop.

The star stuff frame Not one of those grains is a sample of the ocean, and the variety between them is not noise around the ocean’s composition — it is manufactured by the act of sampling. The paper says it plainly: the composition of each subtype “is inherited from the composition of regions within the parent grains.” Read a single grain and you learn about a corner of one droplet and the rate at which it froze. What makes this worth a page here is not the moon; it is that the same sentence is true of the prevalence figure four items down, where a number everybody quoted about a syndrome turned out to describe the route to a genetic test. Before you can read a measurement as a fact about its subject, you have to model the thing that selected it. One of those two cases is ice and the other is insurance criteria, and the error is identical.
What is careful about it, and the two numbers The abstract makes no habitability claim at all, which on an Enceladus paper in 2026 is a decision somebody took, and it deserves naming where this page usually names the opposite. The phosphates are there, the pH modelling is there, the words about life are not. Two numbers to keep straight, both the paper’s own: the abstract says “≈1000” spectra and the results work with 961; and the abstract’s freezing condition, “below 20 kelvins per minute,” is the outer bound, while the discussion says only rates “in the order of 10 K/min” produced the segregation actually observed. The authors are also explicit that some of the data is not yet reproduced by their experiments — the degree of sodium segregation, and the high potassium in the potassium-rich group. The lag is a floor, not a measurement: Crossref deposits no online-first date for this paper, only the 25 September issue, so 4 days is the shortest the true interval can be.
Where it goes Checked against the site as it stood before this page existed. Enceladus already appears on A Field Guide to Worlds That Work by Other Rules, so the moon is spent. The mechanism is not: bubble bursting and ascertainment return nothing here, and ascertain appears once, in a sourcing note on No. 1. The piece this wants to be is a Star Stuff zine about the sampling instrument rather than the moon, with the prevalence item as its second panel — one settled fact, followed until the belonging claim is already inside it.

Lag 6 d

A new bat family tree, and a paper that explains why the old answers were wrong rather than why the old people were

The Bat1K consortium assembled chromosome-level genomes for 103 bat species — 42 of them new — covering all 21 bat families, and combined them with a morphological dataset of 699 characters for 65 species, including 44 pre-Quaternary fossils. The resulting phylogeny moves Myzopodidae to the earliest branch within Vespertilionoidea and makes Emballonuroidea and Vespertilionoidea sister groups. Two results travel beyond systematics. First, the biogeography: fossilised birth–death and dispersal–extinction–cladogenesis analyses place the origin of bats, “and thus powered flight,” in Europe in the late Palaeocene, which the abstract says is “refuting African and North American origins.” Second, the placement of the fossil †Vielasia in the oldest ‘Eochiroptera’ clade “indicates that laryngeal echolocation predates crown-bat diversification” — echolocation before the living families split, rather than after. The sentence this page keeps is a smaller one: the analysis “revealed a mosaic evolutionary history across bats and explained why previous phylogenetic studies were misled.”

The star stuff frame Explained why previous studies were misled. Not corrected, not refuted — diagnosed. A mosaic history, where different parts of the genome carry different trees, will hand a consistent and consistently wrong answer to anyone sampling too little of it, and every earlier team was doing the honest thing with the data that existed. That is the How We Got Here move: the chain does not end at they were wrong, it ends at here is the property of the world that made the wrong answer look right. The site already owns the general case — Five Sigma, No. 40, on a threshold nobody re-examined — and this is the version where the shortcut was not a convenience but a sampling limit, which is the more common and less blameable kind.
Why contested, and the number the headline invented A continent of origin inferred from fossil-calibrated models is partly a statement about where fossils have been collected and prepared. The Palaeogene bat record of Europe is the best-sampled in the world, and the paper’s own pivotal fossil, †Vielasia, is European. The authors hedge accordingly — bats “probably originated in Europe” — and biogeographic reconstruction from an uneven fossil record is exactly the kind of result specialists argue about for a decade. We have not read the paper and cannot say how its sampling bias is handled; the abstract does not address it. And one figure in the coverage belongs to nobody but the headline writer. “Bats may have first evolved in Europe 65 million years ago” — 65 Ma appears neither in the abstract nor in the university’s own release, both of which say late Palaeocene. The late Palaeocene is conventionally about 59.2 to 56.0 million years ago. The headline is therefore roughly six million years early and puts the origin of bats at the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary rather than several million years after it, which is a different claim about a different world. Separately: the release says “137 researchers from 64 countries” where Crossref’s deposit lists 136 authors. Those need not conflict — a consortium has contributors who are not authors — but only one of the two numbers is checkable, and it is the smaller one.
Where it goes Bats are on this site already and in one register only: A Field Guide to Resting uses “the famous twenty-hour bat” as a sourcing caution — one 1960s measurement at one ambient temperature, which halves in a cooler room. Nothing here touches bat phylogeny, biogeography or the origin of flight; echolocation appears on three pages, all of them about what an animal does rather than when the ability arose. The unspent argument is the diagnosis: why a method produced a stable wrong answer. Hold it until the paper can be read — a chain built on an abstract would be a chain whose first joint is contested and whose evidence is a deposit.

Lag 9 d

One paper makes two species, raises one from the dead, buries a name, and nails three more to particular insects in drawers

A revision of the Australian members of the stick-insect genus Anchiale takes the group from two recognised species to five, and the five directions the names move in are the interesting part. A. mabiensis and A. robusta are described as new. A. albopunctata (Kirby, 1896) is removed from synonymy with A. briareus and restored as a species in its own right — stat. rev., a name published in 1896 and brought back out of another one, after a spell as a synonym whose start date the abstract does not give. Going the other way, Ctenomorpha tessulata Gray, 1835 is sunk as a new synonym of A. briareus, taking with it A. austrotessulata, the replacement name coined for it in 2007. And lectotypes are designated for three names — which is the quietest act in the paper and the most load-bearing: it fixes each name permanently to one chosen specimen, so that the next revision argues about animals rather than about what somebody meant. The decisions are supported by Bayesian and maximum-likelihood phylogenies on a mitochondrial COXI fragment, for every species except A. spinicollis. The paper closes with a dichotomous key to all five species and their eggs.

The star stuff frame Last week this scan filed a penguin described in 2026 from a skin collected on 31 December 1897, in a paper that simultaneously demoted a species another team had named in 2020, and the seed it produced was that a name is a decision somebody makes about where a boundary goes, and it moves in both directions, and the animal is unaffected either way. Seven days later, a second paper in a different phylum does all four motions at once. Two cases is the difference between an observation and a pattern, and this is the seed earning its second leg. For a reader who has been handed a diagnosis, a label or a category as though it were a discovery about them, the transferable fact is not that categories are arbitrary — these are evidence-led, careful, and better than what they replace — but that they have dates, authors, and a procedure for changing, and the organism goes on exactly as it was through every revision.
What the evidence is, and what the coverage added The molecular support is one mitochondrial gene fragment, and one of the five species was not sequenced at all. Species delimitation on COXI alone is the most common and the weakest widely used form of this evidence — mitochondria are inherited as a single unit and can tell a different story from the rest of the genome — and the paper is open about which taxon is missing. That is why this is graded plausible on its own terms as well as on ours; the abstract also makes clear the decisions rest on morphology and type material as much as on the tree. Separately, most of what the coverage says is not from this paper: the 62 cm Australian record, the 3,609 species worldwide, the ant-mimicking nymphs of Extatosoma and the leg-shedding are a fun-facts block in the release, not findings here, and none of them is graded on this page. The 1,000-plus iNaturalist and Atlas of Living Australia observations are part of the story — the release’s point is that a heavily photographed insect went unrecognised anyway — but we have that from the release, not the abstract.
Where it goes lectotype, stick insect and Phasmid each return zero across this site; synonym hits six pages, all of them using the word about words rather than about taxonomy. So the nomenclatural machinery is entirely unspent, and it is the half with the argument in it. This belongs with the penguin as one piece and not two — a How We Got Here chain through how a name is made, unmade and pinned, with the first joint marked documented because both papers document their own acts in the formal vocabulary of the code.

Lag 9 d

They wrote a scroll, carbonised it, and then read it back — because the only way to grade an algorithm is to know the answer first

The Herculaneum papyri were turned to charcoal by Vesuvius in 79 CE and are being read, slowly, by X-ray tomography and machine learning. The obstacle is that carbon ink on carbonised papyrus is nearly invisible to X-rays: both are carbon, so there is almost nothing to see. Lead in the ink would change that — lead has been found in two Herculaneum fragments — but you cannot test the idea on the scrolls, because they are irreplaceable and synchrotron time is scarce. So this team built one. Modern Egyptian papyrus; carbon ink mixed with a soluble lead salt at concentrations varied line by line; text written by hand with a stylus; rolled into a cylinder 40 mm across and 110 mm long; then carbonised in a semi-sealed steel container in a furnace with little or no oxygen, which produced an object the authors say closely resembles a real Herculaneum scroll, “with tight packing of layers and waviness that will challenge unrolling algorithms.” Imaged on a laboratory X-ray tomography system at NIST, virtually unrolled with their own software, and read. The leaded text came up. The two lines written without lead were, as predicted, invisible — the control doing its job. And a handheld XRF device detected the lead straight through the carbonisation, which suggests a cheap way to screen which real scrolls are worth the beam time.

The star stuff frame This site has, from its first week, named the discipline that gets dropped — and has never once been able to point at it being kept. No. 40 is about a threshold chosen for convenience; Too Good to Check is a catalogue of how a fact goes wrong here, our own facts included. All corrections, no picture of the alternative. Here is one, and the whole of it is in a single clause of the discussion: “Since that text was initially written on the scroll before carbonization…” — they know the answer. The object exists so that an algorithm’s output can be marked rather than admired, and so that failure is legible. That is the same move as last week’s gravity key in a sealed envelope and the fortnight-old survey that published the spread of physicists’ beliefs instead of a spokesperson’s: in all three, the people producing the result arranged in advance not to be able to steer it. This is the third case and the first outside physics, which is what the Notes-page seed was waiting for.
What it is not, and what the headline got backwards It is a proof of concept on one model scroll with a modern, soluble lead salt. How lead got into ancient ink is, in the paper’s own words, still “under investigation,” with the ink itself and drying additives both candidates — so a model made by dissolving lead nitrate into carbon ink reproduces the X-ray consequence of ancient leaded ink without claiming to reproduce its chemistry. Nothing here reads a real scroll, and the imaging was a laboratory system, not a synchrotron. The authors also apply no text-recognition algorithm at all: the fragment images were flat-field corrected and contrast-adjusted, nothing more, which is honest and also means the paper demonstrates contrast rather than recovery. And the coverage has the physics backwards in its verb. “Scientists burned their own scrolls” — they carbonised them, in a sealed container with the oxygen excluded, which is close to the opposite of burning and is precisely the reason anything survives at Herculaneum to argue about.
Where it goes ground truth appears exactly once on this site, on The Difference-First Frame, and in the opposite sense: a diagnostic model measures us against a typical profile “that was never the ground truth to begin with.” That is a collision rather than a duplication, and it is the better pairing — one page about a ground truth that does not exist and never did, one case of somebody building one on purpose because the work was impossible without it. Herculaneum, papyrus, blinding and pre-registration all return zero here. The piece is a Notes & Rationale page, not a zine; see the seed below for why it still does not have a title.

Lag 31 d

Nine turtles, and a home range whose spread is twice its own average

Two genetically distinct forms of green turtle share the water in the Galápagos and nowhere else: the Central Eastern Pacific “black morphotype” and the putative Western Indo-Pacific “yellow morphotype.” What the yellow-morph animals do there had never been tracked. Nine adults — two females and seven males — were fitted with Argos satellite tags across four islands and followed for between 45 and 418 days. They stayed. Core areas sat in shallow nearshore habitat, most individuals kept to a single island, several crossed between islands over tens of kilometres, and not one left the archipelago for the length of its tag. The sentence this page keeps is the one about spread: home ranges were “highly variable among individuals (mean = 1,497.9 ± 2,913.9 km² SE).”

The star stuff frame The dispersion is roughly twice the mean. Whatever that figure is — and see below, because its label is ambiguous — a single average home range for this group describes none of the nine animals in it; it is a number produced by nine quite different lives and resembling no one of them. A Field Guide to Being a Turtle already says, of the order as a whole, that something this varied has no standard member. This is that sentence arriving for range, in a group whose own name — putative Western Indo-Pacific — carries a prediction about where its members ought to go. The animals did not read the name. The variation is not error around a type; the type is a summary of the variation, and a poor one.
Three things to keep straight, one of them in the coverage n = 9, seven of them male, one archipelago, and tags that stop. The authors say what that buys and what it does not: longer migrations could occur outside the tracking window, and the corresponding author’s own expectation, in the release, is that yellow-morph females will eventually leave to nest in the western Pacific, because they are not known to nest in the Galápagos at all. So “they stayed put” is true of 45 to 418 days per animal and is not yet true of a life. The release says “the three years of our study” and the abstract says 45 to 418 days per turtle — those are two different durations describing two different things, and only the second is the paper’s. And the surprise in the headline is a quotation, not a result: “We expected them to migrate to distant beaches in the western Pacific” is the corresponding author speaking in the release; the abstract says only that how these animals use local habitat “remains unknown.” Last, the arithmetic: ± 2,913.9 is labelled SE in the deposited abstract, and a standard error nearly twice its own mean across nine animals is unusual enough that a standard deviation is the likelier reading — but we have not seen the methods, so we are quoting the label the paper deposited and declining to correct a statistic we cannot check.
Where it goes Galápagos, morphotype and site fidelity return zero across this site. Everywhere That Turtles Go, No. 35, is about shells and breathing rather than range, so the migration argument is not spent by it; home range appears on A Field Guide to the Ways of Being Alone, in a different sense. The natural home is a new entry in the turtle guide rather than a zine — there is no standard distance from home — and it owes the paper first, because an entry resting on a deposited abstract would be a field guide citing a deposit.

Lag 77 d

The children buried with adults were almost never their children

When a medieval grave holds more than one person, the reading has always been that they were family — not because anybody measured it, but because there was no way to. Children cannot be reliably sexed from their skeletons, early Christian burials carry no grave goods to read, and so children have largely been left out of the gendered archaeology of their own period. This team sequenced 142 new ancient genomes from three sites in Sweden — Fjälkinge, Sigtuna and Västerhus, late Viking Age into the medieval period — 68 of them subadults, and then ran kinship three separate ways, keeping only relationships all three methods agreed on. The result: at Västerhus, of 25 multiple burials with usable data from two or more people, three — 12.0% — held first-degree relatives, and no second-degree relationship was found within any single burial, though second-degree ties were common across burials. At Sigtuna the figure was two of sixteen, 12.5%. The abstract’s own sentence: “Contrary to the assumption that collective burials reflect close kinship, our results show that children interred with adults rarely shared close biological ties.” And the discussion’s: concepts of family and fellowship “extended well beyond the modern notion of the nuclear household.”

The star stuff frame The assumption was never a finding. It was the default reading of a grave, held for as long as the alternative was unmeasurable — and when the measurement arrived it came back at one in eight. This site already argues the present-tense version: The Village Was Never Just Family, No. 64, and A Field Guide to the Ways of Being Family, which is one of the guides whose cards are the families people make rather than the families people are issued. What is new here is the period: the nuclear household is being read backwards into the eleventh century by people who have no evidence for it and no way to get any, and now there is a percentage. The paper also notes at least one infant girl buried in a “typically male” context and more distantly related to the men beside her than to anyone else in the grave — one child, one grave, and exactly the kind of case a general rule would have eaten.
Three sites, and a word the paper refuses to let do too much This is three cemeteries in one region in one period, and 21 of the 115 newly sequenced individuals from multiple burials yielded too little data for kinship at all, which is why the denominators above are 25 and 16 rather than the full set. The authors are careful in a way worth copying: they define kindred as a purely genetic grouping that “does not imply any specific social, cultural, or household affiliations.” That cuts both ways, and the second way is the one to say out loud — genomics can exclude close biological kinship, and it cannot tell you what the relationship was. The absence of a father is not the presence of a fosterage. And the paper reports a result against itself, which we are not going to quote without its other half: the predominance of same-sex adult–child burials (17 of 28) survives a Monte Carlo simulation at P = 0.0002, and a Fisher’s exact test on the same contingency table finds no significant association, P = 0.4456. Both are in the abstract’s own results; the paper’s reading is that same-sex pairing was the most frequent practice without being dictated by the ages present. Lag 91 d

The shark was described by an organ it does not have, and nobody could test it until a drone could hover at fifty metres

Sound in water travels through two regimes. Close to the source — within three wavelengths, the near field — it is a hydrodynamic sloshing of water particles. Further out, in the far field, it is a pressure wave, and a fish detects pressure by having a gas-filled swim bladder that squeezes and expands and re-radiates the signal to the inner ear as motion. Elasmobranchs have no swim bladder. From that absence the field inferred, reasonably and for decades, that a shark cannot detect sound past the near field at all — the paper’s own opening words are that sharks “are presumed to lack the capacity to detect these sounds beyond the acoustic near field.” This study flew a drone at 40–50 m over aggregating blacktip sharks off southeast Florida, played pulsed noise bands at 100–200, 200–400 and 400–800 Hz from an anchored underwater speaker, and used the known length of the boat to calibrate the distance at which each shark reacted. The sharks turned hard away — a mean change of trajectory of 72.7°, to a mean heading of 136.1° from the speaker — and 71.5% of the 165 responses were initiated in the acoustic far field, from at least 62 metres. They did not respond once to a 10 kHz control at the same volume, across 209 presentations. Lower frequencies needed less signal: about 16 dB above ambient at 100–200 Hz, against about 45 dB at 400–800 Hz. What the shark has instead of a swim bladder is the macula neglecta, a patch of sensory epithelium whose cupula carries no dense otoconia — and in carcharhinid sharks it is the largest, and the most hair-cell-rich, measured in any vertebrate.

The star stuff frame An animal was characterised by a missing part, and the characterisation held because the experiment that would have tested it could not be built. The chain was clean: no swim bladder, therefore no pressure transduction, therefore no far-field hearing. Each step is sound. The conclusion is about what the shark cannot do, derived entirely from what it does not have, and never once addressed to what it does have instead — an organ so developed in this family that it holds the vertebrate record. This is the deficit model with a gill slit, and the thing that broke it is not a new theory but a cheap drone: somebody could finally watch a wild shark and measure its distance from the sound at the same moment. The paper is quietly clear that the capability was not the obstacle — a 1964 conference abstract describes sharks orienting from 200-plus yards, observed from a circling aeroplane, and was never published. Sixty years on, the same observation with a measurement attached.
The mechanism is not shown, and two of the paper’s own numbers disagree The authors do not claim to have found the receptor: “the mechanism remains unclear.” They could not measure particle motion in the field, which is the quantity that actually matters, and they say so — “ideally, particle motion would have been measured as well, but this was not practical under the field conditions.” They also raise the objection against themselves, that sound energy bleeding below each nominal band would push the near field further out than modelled, and answer it with the 400–800 Hz responses, which cannot be explained that way. Two internal figures do not match, and we are using the ones from the results. The synopsis reads “71.6% of all responses (n = 209)”; the results report 165 responses (59 + 47 + 59) and attach n = 209 to the control presentations instead, computing 71.5%. The discussion also gives the peak of the 100–200 Hz stimulus as 125 Hz where the results give 120 Hz. And the coverage adds two things the paper does not. “Nearly 250 feet” is about 76 m; the paper’s claim is at least 62 m (203 ft), and the farthest single response recorded was 74.0 m. “Pinpoint the source” is more than is shown: what the paper reports is directional information and a consistent turn away, and its own discussion floats the possibility that the consistency of that 72.7° is simply how far a blacktip’s body bends.
Where it goes Half spent, and the spent half is the same shape. A Field Guide to Being a Shark already carries the great hammerhead whose head, tested directly, widens the search area rather than sharpening the electrosense — an assumption about a body part overturned by measuring it. What is unspent is this organ and this inference: macula neglecta, swim bladder and far field each return zero across every page here. The guide is the place, not a zine — there is no standard way to hear, filed beside the hammerhead so the two cases sit together. If it ever becomes a zine, the argument is the inference and not the shark: what you conclude from an absence depends entirely on whether you went looking for the substitute.

Not

Not a citation. Six papers were read end to end this week and the standing rule is untouched. Nothing filed here may be promoted to a zine, a Glimmers entry or a card without the primary source in hand, a changelog entry and its row in the fact-check ledger. For the three items resting on a publisher’s deposited abstract, lifting a figure out and printing it elsewhere would be laundering a deposit into a fact. And one refusal is new this week: a fluent summary returned by an index in the field where an abstract goes, generated by a language model and labelled as such, is not a source at any grade. We did not quote it, and the paper it belonged to is held rather than filed.

Not “science says you are valid.” Two items make that offer this week and the second is the sharper one. A measure of brain ageing finds no difference at all in autism and ADHD, and it would be very easy to carry that number around. We are not going to — because the instrument that produced the null is the same instrument scoring those brains for abnormality in the first place, and a page cannot accept a method only when it agrees. The belonging claim was never waiting on an effect size, and it does not move when the next study finds one.

Not a deficit. A prevalence figure for a genetic syndrome rose by between fourteen and fifty-five times, and what changed was the counting: referrals, insurance criteria, an assay that cannot see half the cases, laboratories that skip an awkward gene. Nobody became more common. We write from inside this, and the people the old number missed were missed on the way to the test. The paper’s own vocabulary — burden, allocation, modelling — is named on this page rather than borrowed from it.

Not a superpower either. A shark hearing from 62 metres through an organ nobody credited, nine turtles whose home ranges disagree with each other by twice their own average, five stick insects where there were two: these are facts about architecture, history and range, never about merit. The paper on the sharks does not say blacktips hear better than you; it says the inference drawn from their missing swim bladder was wrong. Flipping a deficit to a gift keeps the ranking and changes the sign.

Not a cure list. A diet associated with a slower grey-matter slope in an observational cohort is not 2.5 years of anything a reader can buy, and a review of mulberry preparations in animal models of metabolic dysfunction is not a thing to do on Tuesday. Both are held above, and held with their numbers printed, because the figures are real and the sentence built on them is not.

Not anti-science. Three corrections on this page are of positions the field itself held for decades — that a shark without a swim bladder cannot hear into the far field, that a group of Cretaceous mammals could be placed from its teeth, that people sharing a grave shared a parent. Every one was overturned from the inside, by the same discipline, with instruments it built on purpose: a drone that could hover and a boat of known length, a nearly complete skeleton, three kinship algorithms made to disagree with each other. None of those corrections is an argument against evidence; each is an argument for the part that was missing. If a draft off this digest starts reading as a case against the field, it has gone wrong.

Not because of. An ocean under the ice of a small moon of Saturn is sorting its own salts as it freezes, and the grains carry the record of the sorting rather than of the sea. That is a rhyme, not a proof. The universe declining to pathologize its own variation is why the phrase carries — down to your star stuff measures how far the loving reaches, never why it is owed.
