### Almost everything known about sleep is known about vertebrates, and a spider was asked properly

The abstract’s first move is the one worth keeping: “Sleep is a fundamental behavioral state found across the animal kingdom, yet most of what we know about sleep is heavily vertebrate-biased. Studies are scarce or nonexistent in all but a few invertebrate lineages.” A 2022 report of REM-like twitching in a jumping spider had got a great deal of attention and, as this paper says plainly, “experimental evidence that these behavioral indicators truly represent sleep was lacking in spiders.” So they went and got it, in *Portia fimbriata*: “diurnal activity, a free-running circadian rhythm, robust sleep architecture, regularly occurring REM-like behaviors, elevated arousal thresholds during those REM-like states, as well as rebound effects following sleep disruption.” Those last two are the load-bearing ones — a raised arousal threshold and a rebound after deprivation are the behavioural criteria that separate sleep from sitting still. **The conclusion is stated at exactly the width the evidence supports**: “the behavior we observe in spiders is behavioral sleep, providing evidence for an REM-like sleep substate in a terrestrial invertebrate.”

**The star stuff frame** **The interesting thing is not that a spider sleeps; it is that nobody had checked, and that this is normal.** Sleep research built its account of a universal behaviour out of a handful of lineages and then described the result as what sleep *is*. Every so often somebody tests a creature outside the sample and the definition has to widen — not because the creature is exceptional but because the sample never was representative. *A norm assembled from whoever was convenient to measure is a norm about convenience*, and this site has an entire shelf arguing that point about human beings. Here it arrives as an ordinary methodological observation in the first two sentences of a PNAS abstract, about spiders, with nobody making anything of it. **That is the version we trust most.**

**What it says, and what the headline asked instead** **The wire headline is “Do spiders dream?” and the paper does not contain the question.** What it reports is *behavioural* sleep and an REM-*like* substate — both hedges are the authors’, in the abstract, and both are doing real work. No neural recording is described in the abstract; the criteria are behavioural throughout, which is why the word is *behavioral sleep* and not *sleep* flat. **Dreaming is a claim about experience and nothing here reaches it.** *We did not read the paper.* www.pnas.org answered HTTP 403 to the article URL and there is no PubMed Central copy, so the sample sizes, the arousal-threshold protocol and the rebound statistics are all unread, and the species count is one. The paper is CC BY, which bought nothing this week.

### No wheels, no axle, and a head that stays still anyway

Rolling is nearly absent from animals and the abstract says why in one clause: “Although rolling can confer high speed, it poses a fundamental mechanical challenge, as body rotation destabilizes the head and disrupts sensory-guided adjustment, particularly at high speeds.” A wheel works because an axle separates the spinning part from the rider; an animal has no axle. Dwarf reed snakes of the genus *Pseudorabdion* roll anyway, to escape, and filming them from several angles shows how: they “stabilize their head during dynamic rotational motion of the posterior body by using precisely coordinated body movements.” The authors’ own summary of what that means is the sentence to keep: it “illustrat\[es\] how behavioral innovation can circumvent deep morphological constraints and expand the capacities of animals.”

**The star stuff frame** **The body was never redesigned. The behaviour went around it.** There is no rolling organ here, no new joint, no structure that evolved for the purpose — there is a sequence of movements, learned or innate, that makes an impossible thing possible using the parts already present. *That is a different kind of answer from the one the question invites*, which is always “what structure lets it do that?” This one is: nothing does, and it does it anyway, by timing. The honest reading stops there and does not go on to a human. **A capacity can live in coordination rather than in anatomy, and if you only measure the anatomy you will report that the capacity is absent.**

**The numbers are the release’s, and the dates do not agree with each other** **“Four times faster” is not in the abstract.** The figures — peak forward velocities of 1.72 to 3.69 metres per second, roughly four times the maximum under lateral undulation, down a racetrack 20 centimetres wide — come from the ScienceDaily release and are marked here as the release’s, not the paper’s. The abstract gives no velocity at all. **This is a Current Biology *Correspondence***, two pages in the magazine section, not a research article, and the abstract is correspondingly short. **And the record carries no online-first date.** Crossref dates it to the October issue and created the record on 19 October; Semantic Scholar says 1 October. We have used 19 October, which gives a lag of 5 days; from the other date it would be 23. *We cannot reconcile them and are not going to pick the flattering one silently.* www.cell.com answered HTTP 403 and there is no PubMed Central copy, so the kinematics, the number of individuals filmed and the head-stabilisation analysis are all unread.

### They came through it by never having left the climate they already fit

Crocodile-line archosaurs have been through 250 million years, two mass extinctions and several hyperthermals, and the question is whether their famous attachment to warm wet places is an ancient constraint or a recent habit. This paper builds a time-scaled phylogeny of 402 tips, calibrates niche models on living crocodylians and projects them back about 245 million years, to the Anisian. The answer is conservatism: temperature and precipitation show strong phylogenetic structure, best fitted by a bounded Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, and the range shifts through time are the animals *following* a stable optimum rather than the optimum moving. The end-Cretaceous line is the one the coverage wants, and the paper gives it carefully: “Unlike non-avian dinosaurs, low-latitude suitability persisted across all but the most extreme end-Cretaceous scenarios.” **And the closing sentence refuses the tidy version of its own result**: “Climatic niche conservatism is therefore ancient in crocodile-line archosaurs, but not completely static.” Two regime shifts are identified and they are decoupled — thermal near the Cenomanian/Turonian hyperthermal (~93.9 Ma), precipitation near the Eocene/Oligocene aridification (~33.9 Ma), the latter confined to Australasian mekosuchines.

**The star stuff frame** **Survival here is not flexibility, and it is not toughness either.** The lineage did not adapt its way through the asteroid; it stayed inside a narrow band of conditions it had always needed, and that band did not disappear everywhere at once. *A specialist survived because a refuge persisted* — which makes the survival a fact about the world as much as about the animal. The generalist story is the one that gets told, because it reads as a virtue; this is the other one, and it is less flattering and more useful. **Nothing about a crocodylian got better at tolerating anything.** What happened is that somewhere stayed liveable, and the lineage was already there. The argument sits one step away from an obvious human application and we are deliberately not taking that step — see the [refusals](#refusals-1030).

**What the paper itself declines to claim** **Climate is explicitly not the whole answer.** The Discussion names the rest and we are quoting it rather than letting the headline stand: “Non-abiotic factors—including smaller body size, semi-aquatic lifestyle providing access to detrital food webs” also mattered, which means *“why did crocodiles survive?” has at least four answers and this paper supplies one of them.* **The limitations are stated in the paper’s own words:** “The main caveats involve coarse temporal bins, non-analog climates, and preservational biases.” Niche models calibrated on 25 or 26 living species and projected across 245 million years are an extrapolation far beyond their training range, which the authors say. **One small internal disagreement, recorded because we found it rather than because it matters:** the text refers to 26 currently recognised living crocodylian species while the table and methods list 25, and the abstract’s “\>400 fossil and extant species” is 402 tips pruned from an original 534. *We did not obtain the supplementary materials or the occurrence data.*

**Where it goes** niche conservatism returns nothing across this site. Crocodilians appear in [*The Shell Was Your Calcium*](https://starstuff.earth/the-shell-was-your-calcium-zine.html) (No. 57), for the caruncle they share with turtles and echidnas, and nowhere near this argument. ### The same asteroid, the opposite strategy — and a verb the design cannot carry

Transposable elements are the mobile, repetitive, long-dismissed fraction of a genome. This paper assembles an unusually broad comparison — “163 genomes spanning 12 of 16 extant ant subfamilies” — and looks at what those elements did over 100 million years. It reports “convergent TE acquisition bursts in ancestors of the most speciose ant clades, preceding rapid diversification” and places that diversification “following the Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction event approximately 66 million years ago.” It also finds that “TEs associate with expansions of odorant receptors and other key gene families,” which in an animal whose social life runs on smell is the specific and interesting part, and that ant genomes are compartmentalised into faster-evolving TE-rich and more conserved TE-poor regions. **Set beside the crocodile paper above it is the same catastrophe answered two ways:** one lineage came through by staying exactly where it fit, the other radiated into what was left.

**The star stuff frame** **The junk was the raw material, and that is the oldest correction in genomics still being made.** Transposable elements were selfish DNA, parasites, the stuff left over after the genes — and here they are sitting in the ancestry of exactly the clades that went on to become most of the ants. *A fraction of a genome gets classified by what it looked like to the people measuring it*, and the classification outlives the reason for it by decades. This site already owns the adjacent fact: genome size says nothing whatever about complexity. **What it does not own is this one** — that the part counted as surplus is where variation to work with came from.

**Why this is contested, which is a judgement about a verb** **The abstract says the findings “demonstrate the broad-scale impact of TE-dependent genome evolution” and the study is correlational.** We read the paper precisely to settle this, and the Discussion settles it against the abstract: “All evolutionary genomic studies are inherently limited in their ability to establish causal relationships,” and the K–Pg event itself “could have independently promoted both lineage diversification and TE proliferation.” *That second sentence is a confounder the authors name and cannot rule out* — the same extinction could have caused both things this paper connects. Their argument against reverse causation is good: the TE accumulation precedes the diversification in time. Their argument for causation is temporal precedence plus plausibility, which is not the same as demonstration. **So the grade is about the word, not the work.** The paper is careful in its Discussion and stronger than its design in its abstract and in the phrase “likely provided the raw genetic material facilitating diversification” — and the wire took the abstract. The authors also note, in their own words, that “we cannot exclude the possibility that extinct taxa exhibited different patterns of TE activity,” and 12 of 16 subfamilies is 12 of 16.

**Where it goes** [*The Onion Has More Than You*](https://starstuff.earth/the-onion-has-more-than-you-zine.html) (No. 107) is the only page here that mentions transposable elements, and it mentions them in service of a different claim — that genome size does not rank anything. **The unspent argument is the catalysis one**, and it needs the hedge in the previous paragraph attached to it from the first draft, not added in a colophon. *A zine built on this that used the word* drive *would be making exactly the move we have graded this paper down for.*

### Thirty years of treating one electron as the whole story, and two of them turn up together

High-harmonic generation is how attosecond light pulses are made: drive a gas with an intense laser, an electron is torn loose, driven, and slammed back into its parent ion, emitting a high-energy photon. The whole field has been built on a single-active-electron picture with a hard ceiling — “For over three decades, high-harmonic upconversion has been framed within the confines of a single-active-electron” interaction with a well-defined cutoff. This experiment, in helium driven at 400 nm in a gas-filled hollow-core waveguide, finds light past the ceiling: “We observe a weak secondary plateau that extends the conventional cutoff beyond 120 eV up to the water window at 280 eV.” The new scaling is “up to 5.5 times the ponderomotive energy of the rescattering electrons,” against “the conventional scaling factor of 3.2.” Argon, run for comparison, shows no extended plateau.

**The star stuff frame** **The limit was a limit of the model, and the model was a simplification everybody knew was a simplification.** Treating one electron as if it were alone is a choice made because the alternative is intractable, not because atoms contain one electron; three decades later the ceiling that choice implies has been read as a property of nature. *The correction is not that the old physics was wrong* — it is that a boundary drawn by what could be calculated got mistaken for a boundary in the world. The water window at 280 eV is the part that matters practically: it is the band where carbon absorbs and water does not, which is what you want for imaging something alive. **Correlation between two electrons, which the single-electron picture exists to ignore, is what reaches it.**

**The observation is solid; the mechanism is the authors’ reading** **Double-electron recombination is an interpretation, and the paper says so.** It calls that account “the most plausible explanation for the extended high-harmonic cutoff” and states that confirming it requires further quantum simulations — so the measured plateau is the result and the two-electron story is the leading candidate for it. **The signal is faint and the paper says that too**: “the extended X-ray emission remains intrinsically weak due to low densities of correlated electron pairs.” *“Scientists just broke a textbook rule of physics” is the wire’s sentence* and no rule broke; a limit derived from an approximation was exceeded by an effect the approximation discards, which is a different and more ordinary thing. We did not reproduce any analysis and the raw spectra were not obtained.

**Where it goes** high-harmonic returns nothing across this site. The nearest neighbour is [*Beyond the Optical Image*](https://starstuff.earth/beyond-the-optical-image-zine.html), which is about what an instrument can and cannot show; this is the same argument one level down, at the point where **the instrument’s limit turns out to be the calculation’s limit.** *Unspent and difficult:* it would need a reader to care about ponderomotive energy, and the honest version cannot skip it.

### A cell can be told what to become by how stiff the place is

A T cell has to move through tissues that differ enormously in how hard they are to push through, and this paper asks whether the cell notices. It does: activated T cells “sensed mechanical cues and responded with changes in cell morphology, nuclear envelope composition” and the initiation of DNA repair, protecting their chromosomes from force-mediated damage. The stiffness range is real tissue, measured: “Liver, lung, kidney and salivary gland were ~10-fold stiffer (2.2 kPa) than LN and spleen (0.2 and 0.4 kPa, respectively).” And the force does not merely stress the cell — it reprograms it, “modulating the expression of the tissue-resident memory T cell-associated transcription factors Klf2, Runx3 and Hic1.” **Cells preconditioned on stiff substrates and transferred into mice carried the change with them.** The conclusion, in the authors’ words: mechanosensing “impacted T cell fate, promoting a transcriptional program associated with tissue residency.”

**The star stuff frame** **This site has one founding fact about force and tissue — bone is piezoelectric, and a skeleton turns pressure into charge.** Here is a second, in a different cell and by a different route: the mechanical character of a place is an input to what a cell becomes, not just something it has to put up with. *Environment is not the backdrop to the biology; it is one of the instructions.* That sentence is as far as the finding goes and it is already enough — a cell in a stiff organ ends up a different kind of cell from its sibling in a soft one, and nobody had to tell it which organ it was in. **The temptation is to carry this straight across to rooms and people and we are not going to**; see the [refusals](#refusals-1030), and note that the paper itself cannot yet say what does the sensing.

**The mechanosensor is unidentified, and the paper says so in those words** **“While the mechanosensor(s) responsible remains to be determined”** — the paper’s own sentence, and the one that should stop anybody reaching for a tidy molecular story. PIEZO1, the obvious candidate and the channel a reader of this site would expect, *is not named anywhere in the text we read*. What the authors did instead is rule things out: integrins via Talin, YAP/TAZ, cPLA2, myosin II and lamin A/C are each tested and each found not to be the mediator. **Mouse first, human second, and only the mouse work is in a living animal.** Most experiments use mouse CD8+ T cells; human CD8+ T cells from healthy donors were used in vitro only; the in vivo component is intradermal transfer into recipient mice with analysis in the draining lymph node at day 7. **The stiffness numbers for the gels are relative, not absolute** — one collagen preparation is reported as “~3-fold stiffer” than another by nanoindentation, and the kPa figures quoted above are for tissues. The authors list reversibility as open: whether these force-instructed states can be undone “will be important to address.” *We did not obtain the supplementary data or the sequencing.*

### One of Europe’s most studied amphibians, and nobody had put a UV lamp on it

The fire salamander is about as closely watched as a European amphibian gets. Under ultraviolet it turns out to glow: “the first evidence of biofluorescence in the fire salamander (*Salamandra salamandra*), one of Europe’s most studied amphibians.” The fluorescence is “mainly on the ventral and lateral surfaces and in glandular secretions,” and histology matches the emission to the epidermis and the granular glands, with the speckled external pattern corresponding to glandular ducts. **Fluorescence lifetime imaging then finds the thing the authors clearly did not expect**: the glandular fluorophores differ from those in the skin and are “also ubiquitous in blood components, suggesting that fluorescent compounds may be transported via the circulatory system, a feature previously known only in tree frogs.” A second, yellow fluorescence sits in the dermal chromatophore layer and is excited by UV alone. And the secretions keep glowing after the animal has gone: fluorescence “persisted on substrates for over 24 h.”

**The star stuff frame** **It was always doing this. The light we look with could not see it.** A trait on one of the most-examined amphibians in Europe stayed unrecorded until somebody changed the illumination — not because it was rare, hidden or subtle, but because the instrument defined what counted as the animal’s appearance. *This is the umwelt argument without any need to argue it*: our visual range is a window, the window is narrow, and an entire channel of an animal’s surface sat outside it. The trail of glowing secretion lasting a day on a substrate is the part that makes the point concrete — there may have been a visible signal in these woods the whole time, in a band nobody was looking in.

**Communication is a suggestion, and the authors mark it as one** **The abstract’s verb is “suggesting a communicative function,” and the evidence for it is that the trait “fulfilled several criteria for ecological relevance”** — not that any salamander was observed responding to it. No behavioural experiment is described in the abstract. *A trait being conspicuous under UV does not establish that anything uses it*, and biofluorescence in amphibians has a literature full of exactly that gap. **We did not read the paper.** royalsocietypublishing.org is refused at the network layer and no PubMed Central copy exists, so the histology, the lifetime imaging and the criteria the authors applied are all unread; the paper is CC BY. **And the item is 153 days old**, carried this week in the present tense, which is the fourth consecutive edition to file or hold something of that shape.

### Seventy years of measuring meaning along three axes, and one of them was the wrong axis

Since work in the middle of the twentieth century, the essence of a word’s meaning has been taken to lie on three dimensions: valence, arousal and dominance. That scheme is not a historical curiosity — it is, as the paper says, the basis of “sentiment analysis across many fields,” which is to say it is what software means when it says a sentence is positive. This paper re-examines the English lexicon and then English corpora and arrives somewhere else. The best description is a five-part circumplex, goodness–power–aggression–danger–structure; and the sharper finding is the floor underneath it: **“the power-danger-structure framework is the minimal framework that represents essential meaning.”** Arousal and dominance, the two that carry most of the weight in a sentiment pipeline, are not in the minimal set. Along the way the corpora say something about their speakers: “large-scale English language corpora reveal a systematic bias toward safe, low-danger words.” Seven corpora are used, from Jane Austen’s six novels and the Sherlock Holmes canon to twenty years of *The New York Times*, a Wikipedia snapshot, radio transcripts and about a tenth of English Twitter in 2020; the lexicon is NRC VAD, “more than 20,000 English words and phrases,” 20,006 entries in the matrix.

**The star stuff frame** **An instrument got adopted because it was first, and then everything measured with it inherited its shape.** Three axes were proposed, the proposal was good, the axes became the cornerstone — and for decades the question “what are the dimensions of meaning?” was answered by what the standard lexicon happened to score. *That is not a scandal and nobody faked anything*; it is what happens when a measurement framework is useful enough to stop being examined. The second finding is the one to keep for its own sake: a huge sample of what people actually write leans toward the safe end of danger. **What we usually say is quieter than what we can say**, which is a fact about the corpus rather than about any person in it, and is exactly the sort of claim that would be ruined by being applied to one.

**The lag is wrong by a factor of eleven, and the paper itself names the limit** **This was public on arXiv on 13 October 2021, with an abstract identical sentence for sentence to the published one.** The journal version appeared 8 May 2026 and the wire reached it 24 October 2026 — so the bar above reads 169 days and the finding had been readable for **1,837**. *Our lag axis measures journal publication because that is the date records carry, and here it is measuring the wrong event.* **The paper’s own caveat is the right one to quote**: “As any lexicon reflects only the possible but not the used language” — a scored word list is a map of what is sayable, not of what is said, which is precisely why the corpus half of the work exists. The safety bias is reported as a direction, not a magnitude: the text we read gives lexicon medians of −0.019 and −0.038 for the NRC VAD lexicon, and for the corpora says only that “the median danger score is well below 0.” **English only, and “70 years” is the wire’s round number**; the paper says “the middle of the 20th century” and cites Osgood and colleagues as “published in the 1950s.” Three versions of the preprint exist and we read the first alongside the published text.

**Where it goes** valence and arousal appear across this site only in their ordinary senses; nothing here discusses sentiment analysis or the Osgood framework. **The unspent argument belongs in [How We Got Here](https://starstuff.earth/collection-how-we-got-here.html)**, and the joints are already visible — a mid-century proposal, an adoption, a lexicon, an industry, and a re-examination seventy years later finding two of three axes dispensable. ## Held

Six this week, and **the first of them is the story most squarely ours** — a paper about how Autistic people process faces, held because the only thing anyone could read was a university release and because the release’s own frame is one we would have to argue with. ### The autistic brain processes faces differently than scientists thought

**This is the one item on the page we most wanted to file, and it is the one we can read least of.** The work sounds genuinely good: James McPartland’s group at Yale decoding whole-scalp EEG rather than a handful of electrodes, in roughly 400 Autistic children, to ask how face-specific neural responses unfold in time. The release’s method line is the interesting one — previous studies watched a few channels, which he compares to understanding a city’s traffic by monitoring one downtown street. **We cannot grade a word of it.** www.nature.com answers the article path with HTTP 303 to idp.nature.com/authorize; Crossref holds no deposited abstract; Semantic Scholar holds no abstract; there is no PubMed Central copy and no preprint we could find. *There is no sample size for the comparison group, no effect size, no statistic and no stated limitation available anywhere outside a subscription.* **And the second reason is the frame, which is ours to say out loud.** The release describes what was found as responses that were “less clearly distinguishable” in Autistic participants, and a developmental pattern that non-Autistic children show “while autistic children did not show the same pattern” — a difference stated only as a shortfall, with one trajectory standing in as the one a brain is supposed to have. Held — no universe was created, and the paper does not say one was

### Physicists created a tiny universe and watched time emerge without a clock

**The experiment is real and rather lovely; the sentence on top of it is manufactured.** A Bose–Einstein condensate sits in a conservative trap divided by a thin optical barrier into an observed and an unobserved sector. The question — borrowed from the Wheeler–DeWitt problem of time — is “whether the dynamics of the observed sector can be ordered using only internal degrees of freedom,” and the answer is that an “entropic time” built from a coarse-grained entropy “can robustly order the events in the observed sector across repeated cycles of expansion and recollapse.” **The abstract’s own closing claim is modest and precise**: the results “establish a controlled experimental setting in which relational-time constructions can be quantitatively tested.” *A setting in which an idea can be tested is not a universe, and ordering events by an entropy you defined is not time emerging.* The paper’s title is *Testing the problem of time with cold atoms* and the word *universe* appears nowhere in the abstract. **Held because the gap between the two is the whole story**, and filing it would mean either repeating the headline or spending an entry correcting it. *Barontini, G., “Testing the problem of time with cold atoms,” Physical Review Research 8(2), DOI 10.1103/1h9j-df4k, online 11 June 2026 — complete deposited abstract read at Crossref, paper not read; the Crossref record was created 29 April, so the lag is 139 days by the publication date and 182 by the record; wire ScienceDaily, 28 October 2026.*

Held — a stability theorem offered as an observational alternative, and it is five months old

### Einstein’s equations may explain the universe without dark energy

**The paper is what its abstract says it is: mathematics.** It characterises “the local instability of pressureless Friedmann spacetimes to radial perturbation at the Big Bang,” working in self-similar variables, identifying the critical Friedmann spacetime as “an unstable saddle rest point,” and proving that solutions in a particular family “generically accelerate away from Friedmann spacetimes at intermediate times.” An apparent acceleration without a cosmological constant is a real and long-standing line of work. **What it is not is a measurement.** The claim that this explains the observed universe would need the model confronted with supernova, baryon-acoustic and microwave-background data, and the abstract describes no such confrontation; *“an alternative to dark energy” is in the title as a motivation, not as a result.* Dark energy is as contested as cosmology gets and the specialists have been arguing about inhomogeneity models for twenty years, so a single existence-and-instability theorem lands in the middle of a live dispute rather than settling it. **Held on both grounds, and on a third: it was published on 27 May 2026 and carried this week in the present tense**, a lag of 155 days. *Alexander, C., Temple, B. & Vogler, Z., “The instability of critical and underdense Friedmann spacetimes at the Big Bang as an alternative to dark energy,” Proceedings of the Royal Society A 482(2338), DOI 10.1098/rspa.2025.0912, online 27 May 2026 — complete deposited abstract read at Crossref, paper not read; royalsocietypublishing.org is refused at the network layer; wire ScienceDaily, 29 October 2026.*

Held — a relative risk from a genetic subgroup, with the harm in the same subgroup

### Low-dose aspirin linked to 70% lower dementia risk, but there’s a catch

**The paper’s own title is the correction.** It reads “Platelet count polygenic scores *may modify* the effect of aspirin in primary prevention of dementia” — a hypothesis about effect modification in a genetically defined stratum, which is a careful way to describe a subgroup analysis within the ASPREE trial. The headline converts that into a risk reduction with a number on it. **The number comes from a top-quintile stratum and is relative.** Among participants in the highest 20 per cent of platelet-count polygenic score, 1 per cent of aspirin users developed dementia against 3.3 per cent on placebo — an absolute difference of 2.3 percentage points, reported as “approximately a 70 percent lower relative risk.” *In the same stratum, major bleeding went from 2.1 per cent to 4.4 per cent*, which is a larger absolute movement than the benefit, in the wrong direction. **This is the shape this page calls scientism**: a trial’s authority borrowed for a post-hoc stratum, a relative risk doing the work an absolute risk could not, and a genetic score used to make a subgroup sound like a kind of person. The investigators are not doing that — their title says *may modify* — and we are not going to hand anybody a reason to start aspirin. *Fransquet, P. D., Yu, C., Tran, C., Hussain, S. ## Seedbed

Three seeds, all off papers read end to end this week. One of them is a pair rather than a paper, which is the shape this page has been hoping to find: two studies that never cite each other answering the same question in opposite directions. No lead list, for the reason given on 9 October: a list that only grows is a way of looking thorough while deferring the work.

Zine · How We Got Here, or Star Stuff — one catastrophe, two answers

### One of them stayed exactly where it was

**Two papers landed in the same week, in the same journal, about the same sixty-six million years ago, and they do not cite each other.** One says crocodile-line archosaurs came through the end-Cretaceous because their climatic requirements had barely moved in 250 million years and somewhere that met those requirements survived — *a specialist saved by a refuge.* The other says the ant clades that became most of the ants carry bursts of transposable-element acquisition in their ancestry, immediately before radiating into the world the extinction emptied — *a lineage with unusual genomic raw material meeting an unusual opportunity.* **Set side by side they refuse the single moral the extinction is always given.** The story everyone knows is that the adaptable inherited the Earth; here one lineage persisted by being rigid and lucky about geography, and another expanded by having had a messy genome for reasons that had nothing to do with an asteroid. *Neither survival was a virtue and neither was a plan.* A chain is already visible: the extinction, documented; crocodylian niche conservatism, documented; the ant TE bursts and their timing, documented; whether the TE bursts *caused* the radiation, contested — by the authors’ own Discussion, as the entry above records; and anything about what either case implies for anything alive now, leap. **The refusal has to be in the piece from the first spread:** this is not an argument that difference is adaptive, which would be the same ranking with the sign flipped. It is an argument that survival is a joint fact about an organism and a world, and that reading it as a property of the organism alone is how the just-so story gets made.

From the *Science Advances* crocodylian and ant papers filed above, both read in full at PubMed Central. **Not obtained:** either paper’s supplementary materials, the occurrence data, the genome assemblies, and — the real gap — any literature connecting the two, which may well exist and which nobody here has looked for. *The pairing is ours and is currently an observation about two abstracts landing in one week, not a result.*

Field guide · a companion entry, and a correction to our own evidence

### Nobody had asked a spider

**[*A Field Guide to Resting*](https://starstuff.earth/rest-field-guide.html) arrives, like a dozen other guides here, at *there is no standard way to rest* — and its evidence is a penguin huddle, a fur seal that suppresses REM for weeks without repaying it, a swift, a bat whose famous twenty hours rests on one 1960s measurement.** Vertebrates, nearly throughout, with *Cassiopea* as the one celebrated exception. This week a PNAS abstract opened by saying the field has the same shape: “most of what we know about sleep is heavily vertebrate-biased,” with studies “scarce or nonexistent in all but a few invertebrate lineages.” *That is a comment on our guide as well as on the literature it was built from.* The new entry writes itself — a jumping spider with a circadian rhythm, a sleep architecture, REM-like bouts, a raised arousal threshold during them and a rebound after deprivation — but the better piece is the one about the sample. **A guide that says there is no standard way to rest, built from the animals that were convenient to measure, is making its claim on narrower ground than it sounds.** Widening it is not a correction of the conclusion; it is the conclusion arriving for a second reason. *And the hedges travel with it:* behavioural sleep, REM-*like*, one species, no neural recording, and the word *dream* nowhere near it.

From the PNAS abstract filed above — **abstract only**, which is a real limit: www.pnas.org answered 403 and the arousal-threshold protocol, the rebound statistics and the sample size are unread. **Also not obtained:** the 2022 jumping-spider paper this one was written to test, which would have to be read before anything is said about what it did and did not show. *An entry in a field guide needs the paper, not the abstract.*

Zine · How We Got Here, and the chain is short and well documented

### The axes were a proposal, and then they were the furniture

In the 1950s somebody proposed that the meaning of a word could be placed on three dimensions. It was a good proposal; it was useful; it became the basis of sentiment analysis, which is now what a great deal of software means when it reports that a sentence is positive or negative. **Seventy years later a re-examination finds that the minimal set is power, danger and structure — and that two of the original three are not in it.** *Nothing was faked and nobody was careless.* What happened is the ordinary thing: a measurement framework was adopted because it was first and good enough, and then everything measured with it inherited its shape, including the question of whether its shape was right. **This is the collection’s own motive in a clean instance** — not a case against measuring meaning, but an argument for the part that gets left behind when an instrument becomes infrastructure. The joints: Osgood and colleagues in the 1950s, documented; the construction of scored lexicons, documented; sentiment analysis built on those lexicons, documented; *why* nobody re-derived the axes for seven decades, contested; and anything we would say about what else is measured this way, leap. The second finding is the one that would carry the ending: across seven corpora, from Austen to Twitter, the words people actually use sit toward the safe, low-danger end. **A fact about a corpus, and we would have to say so in the same breath, because it is one sentence away from being a claim about a person.**

From the *Science Advances* ousiometrics paper filed above, read in full at PubMed Central, and its 2021 arXiv preprint. **Not obtained:** Osgood, Suci and Tannenbaum’s original work — the first joint in the chain is a book nobody here has read, and the chain cannot be drafted until somebody has. **Also not obtained:** the supplementary materials, and any history of how the VAD scheme entered computational practice, which is the contested joint and the one that would decide whether this is a piece at all.

## Not

**Not a citation.** Five papers were read end to end this week and the standing rule does not move. Nothing filed here may be promoted to a zine, a [Glimmers](https://starstuff.earth/collection-glimmers.html) entry or a card without the primary source in hand, a [changelog](https://starstuff.earth/changelog.html) entry and its row in the fact-check ledger. **Three of the eight filed items rest on deposited abstracts** and are graded accordingly; lifting a figure out of one would be laundering a deposit into a fact, which is easy to do because a structured abstract looks exactly like a result.

**Not a claim about our own neurology sourced to a press office.** The paper on how Autistic brains build face representations over time is the item on this page we most wanted, and it is at the top of [Held](#held-items-1030) because the publisher answers with an authentication redirect and because the only available account describes what we do as what we fail to do. *We are not grading that from a release, and we are not repairing it into a gift either.* Reading a difference as a shortfall and reading it as a superpower are the same move: both keep the ranking and argue about which end we are at. **We write from inside this, which makes the standard higher here rather than lower.**

**Not a metaphor for a room.** A T cell reads the stiffness of the tissue it is in and that reading changes which genes it expresses and what kind of cell it becomes. The sentence this site is built to want — *the environment is an instruction, not a backdrop* — is right there, and it is about collagen gels and mouse ears. *The finding does not transfer by rhyme.* The paper cannot yet name the sensor; its human work is entirely in a dish; and “environment” in a six-kilopascal gel and “environment” in a classroom are the same word doing two jobs. **Bone being piezoelectric is a fact we are allowed to love and not an argument about anybody’s accommodations**, and this is the same, one tissue over.

**Not a picture of a finding.** Thirteen of this week’s eighty-eight scanned items lead with an image credited to a machine and forty-four more with a stock photograph; two further releases credit an institution and then say in the same line that the image was machine-made. Every credit is honest and we accuse nobody of deception. But more than three fifths of the pictures a reader meets before the research come from neither the research nor the institution that did it, and this page will not reproduce one or describe one as though it showed something. Three of the eight items filed above lead with an image credited to one of the paper’s own authors; one, the X-ray harmonics item, leads with a machine-made one and is named as such in its own entry.

**Not a bot check we will argue with.** The archive that carried every full text on this page for five weeks now asks its callers to prove they are not robots, and we did not try to get around it. *A challenge page is a publisher saying who may read automatically*, and defeating one would be a worse thing to publish than an unread paper. What that costs is stated in the first block rather than buried: last week a reader could reproduce our reads with one command and this week they cannot, and a method a reader cannot repeat is weaker evidence than one they can.

**Not a survival story about virtue.** Crocodile-line archosaurs came through the end-Cretaceous because their requirements had scarcely changed in 250 million years and a place that met them persisted. *Nothing in that is toughness, adaptability or deserving.* The paper is careful to add body size, a semi-aquatic habit and access to detrital food webs, and we are quoting it rather than letting one factor stand for four. **The reading we refuse is the obvious one** — that it is good to be flexible, or good to be constant, whichever the week suggests. A lineage persisted because a world stayed liveable, and the half of that sentence about the world is the half that never gets told.

**Not anti-science.** Three items here revise something standing: a single-active-electron ceiling thirty years old, a three-dimensional account of meaning seventy years old, and a literature on sleep assembled almost entirely from vertebrates. *Every one of those was corrected from the inside, with instruments built for the purpose* — helium in a hollow-core waveguide, twenty thousand scored words against seven corpora, a spider watched until it had a rhythm and a rebound. The ant paper is on this page graded contested for a verb, and its own Discussion is where we found the reason. **A field that publishes its confounder in paragraph four is working.** If a draft off this digest starts reading as a case against the field, it has gone wrong.

**Not *because of*.** A spider sleeps, a snake rolls, a salamander glows in a band we cannot see, and none of it is an argument for anything about us. That is a rhyme, not a proof. The universe declining to pathologize its own variation is why the phrase carries — *down to your star stuff* measures how far the loving reaches, never why it is owed.
