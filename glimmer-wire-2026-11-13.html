Lag 13 d

The courier is in doubt; the atoms never were

Anslow, McDonald, Bonsor, Rimmer and Rae revisit whether comets could have delivered the feedstock molecules prebiotic chemistry needs, combining impact simulations, comet–atmosphere interaction and dynamical modelling of short-period comets. Their abstract’s conclusion: “the rapid decline in Earth’s cometary impact bombardment and the efficient destruction of fragile cometary organics indicate that the successful delivery of prebiotic feedstock molecules was an exceedingly rare event.” The numbers are the interesting part. Comets below roughly 100 m “catastrophically fragment in explosive airbursts at high altitude,” so only kilometre-scale bodies arrive intact; hydrogen cyanide then survives only in low-velocity, highly oblique impacts, a very small region of parameter space. Across the Monte Carlo realisations the median number of successful deliveries to Earth is zero, with 54 per cent of runs recording none. And the comparison that does the work is planetary: “Mars receives 744± 27 impacts delivering >10 mM HCN, compared with 87± 9 for Earth” — with Earth’s figure collapsing to a median of zero once the delivery is required to land on dry ground.

The star stuff frame This is a negative result about where we came from, and it is still a good week for it. Nothing here touches the claim this site is named for: the carbon, nitrogen and oxygen were made in stars and are in you, and no model of impact chemistry has an opinion about that. What is in doubt is the courier — the picture in which a comet arrives carrying the ingredients ready-made, which is the version that travels because it is a story with a delivery van in it. The paper’s own alternatives are quieter and more interesting: endogenous synthesis of HCN in the early atmosphere, supplying exposed land steadily rather than dramatically, and cyanide stockpiled as ferrocyanide. Slow local chemistry over a spectacular arrival — and the authors are careful to say the slow route has its own problem, being limited by the atmosphere’s oxidation state.
The headline reverses the subject of the sentence ScienceDaily ran this as “Comets may have destroyed life’s ingredients instead of delivering them to Earth,” and the grammatical subject of that claim is wrong. The paper does not say comets destroyed ingredients that were already on Earth. It says the comets’ own organics are destroyed on arrival, in the very impact that would have delivered them — the destruction and the delivery are the same event, which is the whole difficulty. The authors hedge where they should: “we cannot formally exclude the possibility that a single fortuitous cometary impact” delivered a prebiotic concentration of HCN. And every stated limitation pushes the same way. The 1 bar atmosphere is “a best-case scenario” for survival; the survival model includes one destruction pathway and “neglect[s] comets’ internal strength and porosity”; only short-period comets are modelled; the land-area model is “probably an overestimate.” Those make the result an upper bound on a thing already found to be rare, which strengthens the claim rather than weakening it — and is worth saying out loud, because a list of limitations usually reads as a list of doubts. We did not read the Supplementary material, which holds the survival-fraction-against-velocity curves, so the shape of that small parameter space is unseen here.
Where it goes panspermia returns two pages — There Have Been So Many Earths and last week’s edition — and neither argues this. What is unspent is the shape rather than the chemistry: a mechanism that is loved because it is vivid, tested properly, and found to be the rare case rather than the normal one, with the undramatic alternative left standing. That is a Too Good to Check argument about our own favourite story, and the uncomfortable part is that the comet-delivery picture is the one a star-stuff site would reach for first.

Lag 21 d

The comparison is to the literature, not to somebody in the next room

Schmidbauer and Nieder put carrion crows (Corvus corone) on a classic visual motion discrimination task and recorded from the pallial endbrain. The crows report choices “in approximately 235 milliseconds on average, which is roughly twice as fast as has been observed in monkeys or humans and without sacrificing accuracy.” The recordings are the better half of the result. Direction-selective neurons “encode the upcoming choice as early as about 70 milliseconds after stimulus onset and about 170 milliseconds before the response, with ramping activity scaling with sensory evidence and converging at response onset” — so the decision is not merely quick at the output. It is settled in the brain about a sixth of a second before the bird moves, by an accumulation process that looks like the one described in primates, running at a different speed.

The star stuff frame This site already has a shelf on bird cognition, and its argument is that the comparison axis is the problem. Older Than Either of Us (No. 86) and A Field Guide to Bird Minds (FG 23) between them record that a kea carries 1.28 billion pallial neurons against a chimpanzee’s 7.4 billion and performs comparably — so whatever is doing the work, counting is not it. Speed is a second axis, and on this one the bird is simply ahead. Which is not a ranking either, and the paper declines to make it one: it reads the speed as “possibly shaped by evolutionary pressures for rapid visuomotor control” — an animal that has to decide while already in the air. A difference in what an arrangement is for, not a difference in how good it is.
“As has been observed” is doing load-bearing work The comparison is across studies, not head to head. The crows’ 235 ms is set against published monkey and human reaction times from other laboratories, other apparatus, other stimulus parameters and other reward contingencies — and reaction time is exactly the quantity that moves when any of those move. The authors write the sentence carefully, with “as has been observed” marking it as a comparison to a literature; the wire headline drops the clause — “Crows can make decisions twice as fast as humans” — and what is left reads as a race that was run. Those are different claims, and only the first one was tested. We did not read the paper. www.science.org answered HTTP 403 and no PMCID exists, so the stimulus parameters, the task timing and whatever comparison the authors actually tabulate are unread. The contested grade is about what we could check, not about what they did — and it would move to plausible or verified on sight of the methods.
Where it goes carrion crow returns nothing across this site and Corvus returns only the field guide. No. 86 owns where the circuit came from and FG 23 owns the thirteen birds and the arguments they were recruited into. Unspent: speed as a dimension nobody ranks on. Every popular comparison of bird and primate cognition is about capability, and this is a measurement on an axis where the answer has been sitting unremarked — which is a companion note to an existing pair, not a new piece. The guide’s own closing line is that there is no standard bird; it would hold a sentence about there being no standard clock either.

Lag 28 d

A story about why a thing is shaped like that, tested against a living fish

When tetrapods arose, the skull separated from the shoulder girdle and a neck region appeared. The standing explanation is that neck mobility increased and made the move onto land workable — and it has been hard to test, because those early vertebral components are not fully co-ossified and so cannot be articulated the usual way. Korneisel and colleagues went round it by measuring a living animal with a similarly configured axial column: “Ten A. fulvescens yearlings were partially dissected for material characterization” — lake sturgeon — put through three-point lateral bending, dorsoventral bending and whole-column torsion, with the notochord tested separately. The model that falls out is unexpected: “the soft tissue-dominated axial column was surprisingly rigid, despite lacking co-ossified vertebral components.” Most stem tetrapods “could move their heads less than 6° laterally and less than 3° dorsally but could not rotate them” — a 5° head rotation needed at least 200 Nm of torque. And the mobility does not change where the hypothesis says it must: “mobility did not shift in conjunction with the head–shoulder separation,” so “mobility in the tetrapod neck did not serve as a functional driver of head–shoulder separation.”

The star stuff frame The replacement explanation is not about the head at all. The authors argue the separation “is more consistent with the predicted origin of forelimb-driven locomotion”: the shoulder girdle came under rising demand to support a limb doing the walking, and the skull was let go of as a consequence. A structure can separate because of what is happening to its neighbour. That is the part worth keeping, and it generalises well past fish — a feature is found, a function is proposed for it, the function is plausible because the feature is right there, and the actual cause turns out to be a demand on something adjacent that nobody was looking at. And there is an outlier worth naming: Whatcheeria models at up to 32° lateral flexion and 12° extension, against under 3° and under 1.5° for Acanthostega, Greererpeton and Trimerorhachis. The authors flag it as an outlier and do not explain it away. One animal doing something ten times the group’s range is precisely what a mean hides, and leaving it visible in the results is the paper behaving well.
A narrower subject than the headline, and limitations that all push one way ScienceDaily ran “Scientists discover that necks didn’t evolve for the reason we thought,” and the paper’s claim has a smaller subject. It is not that necks did not evolve for a reason; it is that neck mobility was not the functional driver of the head–shoulder separation. The neck still happened, and the paper offers a reason it happened. The stated limitations are unusually candid. The method “is inappropriate for estimating the range of mobility in taxa such as these, with restricted notochords,” which puts embolomeres and amniotes outside it; evaluating animals with more developed zygapophyseal articulations “will require a different approach”; integument, muscle, osteoderms and ribs add stiffness the model omits; internal organs such as gills could restrict head motion further; and sturgeon differ from early tetrapods in vertebral details the authors list. Note the direction of every one of those: each would make the real animals less mobile, not more, so the limitations reinforce the central claim instead of threatening it. We did not read the Supplementary Information or the Extended Data, which hold the per-taxon modelling.
Where it goes Tiktaalik returns one page — A Field Guide to Unfinished Animals, where it appears inside an entry about contested trackways rather than about necks. What is unspent is the inference, not the fish: a correct observation with a functional story attached afterwards, repeated until the story is quoted as the observation. See the seedbed, where it meets a second case from a completely different field in the same week.

Lag 31 d

A habitat measured in how hard it is to swim through

Marine snow — the drifting aggregate of mucus, faeces, silt and dead plankton — carries carbon out of the surface ocean, and how much reaches the deep depends on how fast bacteria take it apart on the way down. Inman, Humphries and Azam report in Science that aggregates “are highly structured microhabitats with less-viscous regions that are accessible for colonization and more-viscous regions that correlate to a barrier to bacterial infiltration,” and that “more viscous aggregates take longer to degrade, sink farther, and contribute more to carbon sequestration, revealing a microscale physical constraint of the global ocean carbon pump.” The instrument is the thing, and it is described in a companion paper we did read in full: molecular rotors — fluorescent molecules whose emission depends on how freely they can turn — imaged by confocal microscopy, which maps viscosity in three dimensions without destroying the material. The numbers, relative to seawater at 1: diatom aggregates sit at a most probable 1.55 and 1.45 with regions “up to a factor of 16 greater than seawater”; a jellyfish ephyra’s mucus layer sits at 1.35 with granules reaching “viscosities up to a factor of ~ 250 greater than seawater.”

The star stuff frame This is an umwelt measured in a unit nobody thinks of as sensory. To a bacterium the difference between 1.35 and 250 is the difference between open water and a wall, and the paper’s word for the resulting landscape is the right one: “mapping the microscale viscous ‘mucoscape’ provides an important physical context for microbial processes.” The best single sentence is about a jellyfish. The mucus around a Cassiopea xamachana ephyra “may maintain a local microbiome while preventing direct contact with the animal,” and in the results “the mucus layer prevents potentially harmful bacteria from reaching the surface of the ephyra.” An animal keeping its bacteria at a measured distance, in a medium whose thickness it sets. A Field Guide to Symbioses reads partnership across the tree of life as mutual aid; what this adds is that a partnership has a geometry, and the geometry here is thickness. Two diatom cultures make the point without an animal: one aggregate “small and consolidated,” with bacteria “only found around the exterior”; the other “larger, patchier, and held together by a lattice of pennate diatom cells,” with bacteria “found around the edges and throughout the interior.” Same relationship, two architectures, and the architecture decides who gets in.
The six-times figure is the release’s, and the n is one The headline number does not appear in anything we read. ScienceDaily ran “Why some ocean ‘snow’ carries six times more carbon,” and the UC San Diego release says the stickiest particles “can transport more than six times as much carbon to the deep ocean”; the deposited Science abstract says only “contribute more to carbon sequestration.” We have not seen that factor derived and are not repeating it as the paper’s. www.science.org answered 403 and there is no PMCID for it, so the Science paper is unread and this item is graded plausible even though its companion was read end to end — reading one paper of a pair does not verify the other. And the methods paper is scrupulous about its own n. “We focus our analysis on two example aggregates,” each reported as “N = 1 aggregate”; the bacteria–viscosity relationships are “correlative” in the authors’ word; other factors “may influence bacterial colonization”; and the measurement “is not the same as hydrodynamic viscosity measured with a rheometer,” which matters before anyone quotes 250× as a bulk property. One small thing, recorded because transcription is where quotations go wrong: the release quotes the first author calling it “the muscoscape”; both papers write mucoscape.
Where it goes marine snow returns nothing across this site; mucus appears on A Field Guide to Rest and the symbioses guide, in both cases as a substance rather than as a place. The unspent idea is habitat-as-viscosity, which belongs beside A Field Guide to Life in Impossible Places and its standing line that the condition gets called extreme and never the organism. A guide entry needs the Science paper, not its abstract.

Lag 84 d

Thirty years of counting, and the animal everybody blames is not in the equation

Ostfeld, LaDeau, Oggenfuss, Canham, Fargione, Winchcombe and Keesing test a conceptual model of the Lyme system against more than three decades of monitoring — six 2.25-hectare plots in oak forest at the Cary Institute in Millbrook, New York, with data running from 1991. “Contrary to conventional wisdom,” they report, the abundance of nymphal black-legged ticks “was not correlated with the abundance of white-tailed deer, the prior abundance of larval ticks, or seasonal temperature extremes.” In the text: “Despite sixfold variation in deer abundance across years,” they “observe no statistical relationship between deer abundance and subsequent” nymph density. What does predict it is a chain with a two-year reach — red oak acorns in year t−2, white-footed mice in year t−1, nymphs in year t — with “a strong mouse year increased nymphal abundance in the following year by approximately 40%.” And infection prevalence is not a mouse story either: at these sites it “appeared to arise from the distribution of larval ticks across the entire community of larval hosts,” because “high densities of mice strongly reduced the tick burdens on individual mice and chipmunks, suggesting intraguild competition for parasites.” The abstract’s own summary: “Lyme disease risk arises from the dynamics of the ecological community, rather than from the dynamics of any single population of hosts or from extreme seasonal weather.”

The star stuff frame This is the single-cause habit, caught by a measurement rather than by an argument. Everyone knows deer carry ticks, and the fact is true; the inference — fewer deer, less Lyme — is what thirty years of counting does not support at these sites. The property we were attributing to one species turns out to be a property of a relationship among several, and the species we blamed was simply the one large enough to see from a car. The hopeful part is not that the risk is smaller; it is that the risk is legible. The authors close with “These dynamics are complex but predictable, such that understanding them can facilitate improvements in management and education aimed at protecting public health” — which is the opposite of the fatalism that usually arrives attached to the word complex. Complexity here means the unit of description was wrong, not that there is nothing to describe. An oak makes a decision two years before a person gets bitten, and nobody involved is thinking about anybody.
Where it holds, in the authors’ own fencing The dilution result is specific to intact forest and they say so: “Our long-term study plots are located within large tracts of eastern deciduous forest.” The competition-for-parasites finding should not be carried into fragmented or suburban habitat without someone measuring it there. One sentence in the paper does more extrapolating than we are willing to carry: the authors say their results apply to oak-poor habitats, which they do not test here, so we are reading that as an expectation rather than a finding. And one of their central negatives is unexplained by them: “The mechanisms underlying this surprising negative effect of warm years on DON remain to be fully evaluated.” The monitoring design has a limit they name and cannot fix — it “was not designed to monitor the abundances of mammals too large” for their methods, and “long-term studies such as ours are particularly difficult to replicate,” which is the honest statement of a dataset that cannot be reproduced by anyone in under thirty years. And a transparency note about our own reading: the path coefficients sit in a figure rather than a table (standardised estimates from −0.53 to 0.50; Fisher’s C = 4.26, P = 0.64; fixed effects explaining 44 per cent of the variance in peak nymphal abundance and the full model 80), so those are read off a caption. The SI Appendix tables holding the test statistics were not read.
Where it goes lyme as a whole word returns one page across this site, and acorn returns one — A Field Guide to the Ways of Migrating, where oaks appear because a forest travels by relay, which is about what acorns are rather than what they feed. The unspent piece is a chain, and the collection for it already exists. See the seedbed: this is a How We Got Here sequence that arrives with its own joints already graded by thirty years of data, including one joint that is documented absent — a tag that collection does not yet have.

Lag 102 d

The paper’s title puts the species first, and that is the entire finding

Marrero and colleagues tested 119 people in Miami against three mosquito species — Aedes aegypti, Ae. albopictus and Culex quinquefasciatus. The paper is called “Individual humans are more attractive to certain mosquito species,” and the grammatical weight of that title is the result: “Each mosquito species exhibited distinct responses to individual humans,” and “Mosquito species can rank the attractiveness of individual humans differently.” The three rankings of the same 119 people barely agree. Pearson correlations between species run 0.3492 (aegypti against albopictus), 0.1431 (aegypti against quinquefasciatus) and 0.06034 (albopictus against quinquefasciatus) — weak, negligible, and essentially nothing. The chemistry splits the same way: alpha-pinene and (E)-2-hexen-1-ol formate were higher in high-attraction participants for all three species, while ethyl 2-methylbutanoate mattered only to Ae. aegypti, which also had thirteen odours of its own. On skin bacteria, Staphylococcus was enriched in high-attraction Ae. aegypti participants and Pseudomonas in low-attraction ones. A sex effect appears for one species only: Ae. aegypti averaged 91.5 per cent attraction to male participants against 87.4 per cent to female (p = 0.0204), with no significant difference for the other two.

The star stuff frame “Mosquito magnet” is a property people are assigned, usually by other people at a barbecue, and this paper says the property does not exist until you name which animal is doing the ranking. Twelve field guides on this site arrive at some version of there is no standard star, nest, egg, shark, way to rest, family. This is the same sentence with the subject moved: not that there is no standard human, but that there is no standard observer — and here that claim comes with three correlation coefficients attached. The ranking is not a fact about the person being ranked. It is a fact about a relationship, and there are at least three of them running at once over the same 119 bodies. Nobody comes out of this measurement with a score. They come out of it with three scores that do not agree, which is a different kind of object entirely.
What it measures, and one discrepancy that is ours to report This is attraction in an apparatus, not choice in a room, and the authors say so first: “we are not directly assessing whether a given mosquito will select an individual out of a group.” The Culex arm used worn nylon sleeves, which “are not a direct replacement for live hosts,” and the authors note larger standard deviations and lower response rates for two of the three species that “may or may not reflect biology.” The high- and low-attraction groups are the top and bottom tenth of the sample — twelve or thirteen people a side — so the odour and microbiome comparisons rest on small contrasts even though the ranking rests on 119. One thing we noticed by reading both halves: the Discussion reports different odour counts from the Results. We have not worked out which is right, we have not asked the authors, and we are therefore not quoting either count as settled — the correlations above come from the Results. And attraction itself was not tested for stability over time. The stability claim in the introduction is about the skin microbiome, not about whether the same person would rank the same way next summer, which is the question most readers will take away.
Where it goes mosquito returns four pages, and the substantial one is A Field Guide to Third Nature, where the London Underground mosquito appears as a habitat story with a genomic correction attached — a different argument entirely. Two coats, one rule, and the difference is a setting

The textbook account of how a mouse grows hair is “expansion-induction”: as embryonic skin stretches, new follicle precursors appear equidistant from the existing ones, in successive waves. It is a clean geometric rule and it fits the laboratory mouse. Ibrahimi, Jahanbakhsh, Tzika and Milinkovitch argue that the rule is a description rather than a mechanism — it “is incompatible with more recent developmental data” and “does not fit well with the known self-organized, spontaneous cellular and molecular interactions” that build placodes. Their replacement is motion, not spacing: a continuum model of motile dermal cells following an epidermal chemoattractant across an expanding sheet, in which “the chemotactic component of this framework suffices to describe the dynamics of placode insertion” — and which reproduces the equidistant rule as an output. The second half is the better half. The spiny mouse Acomys dimidiatus “exhibits a strikingly regular and long-ranged pattern of placodes,” elongated dorsoventrally with the lattice stretched head to tail; that pattern “is not generated by an expansion-induction mechanism, but is recapitulated by an anisotropic chemotaxis model” combined with the anisotropic growth actually measured in the embryo. Same system, different value of one component.

The star stuff frame Two animals, two coats anyone would describe as different kinds of thing, and the difference is not two mechanisms. The authors’ own sentence: “variation in the chemotactic component of the corresponding self-organizational system might be a key determinant of interspecific differences in hair placode patterning dynamics and resulting spatial organizations.” Neither pattern is the default and neither is a deviation. The laboratory mouse is not the baseline that the spiny mouse departs from — both fall out of one rule under different settings, and the only reason one of them looks canonical is that it is the animal the field happened to standardise on. That is what a paradigm of variation looks like when somebody writes it as a partial differential equation, and the thing that makes it worth having is that nobody involved was making a point about anybody. A model that produces two patterns from one mechanism is not an argument about difference. It is just what the mathematics did, which is why it is worth more than an argument.
What a model has shown when it reproduces a pattern Sufficiency is not identity, and the paper’s verbs are careful about it. The authors write that chemotaxis suffices, that variation might be a key determinant, that the spiny mouse pattern might be dominated by a chemotaxis process. Reproducing a pattern shows a mechanism could do it, not that it is the one in the animal — and this is a continuum model, not a measurement of cells moving. Three concrete limits. It is two-dimensional: “both chemotaxis and expansion-induction models are defined in 2D, and therefore neglect vertical cell movements,” collapsing dermis and epidermis onto one surface. It does not identify the chemoattractant — a fibroblast growth factor is offered as an example, not a result. And the obvious rival is left standing: whether reaction–diffusion systems alone could reproduce mouse patterning “remains an open question for future work.” Two species is also two species, and the claim about interspecific variation is a proposal drawn from a pair.
Where it goes chemotaxis, chemotactic, reaction-diffusion, spiny mouse and Acomys all return nothing across this site; placode returns two pages and neither is about patterning. The unspent argument is one rule and two outputs, which is The Difference-First Frame stated in somebody else’s mathematics rather than in ours — and it is also the second half of the seedbed note, because the rule it retires was a correct description of an animal that got promoted to an explanation of a process.

Not a metaphor, in either direction. Three mosquito species rank the same 119 people differently and the rankings barely correlate. The sentence this site is built to want is right there and it is about Aedes aegypti and a skin microbiome. No person is being ranked by anybody in that experiment, and the thing that makes the result worth having — that nobody involved was arguing about human worth — is exactly what carrying it across would destroy. The same applies to a jellyfish holding its microbiome at a measured viscosity, and to an oak that sets a tick population two years out.

Not an animal cleared of a charge, either. Deer did not turn out to be innocent; deer turned out not to be the variable. The correction is not that we blamed the wrong species but that the thing we were explaining was never a property of a species — it is a property of a community, over a two-year lag, with oaks in it. Swapping deer for mice would keep the scheme and change only the defendant, which is why the paper puts community in the sentence instead of a name.

Not a bot check we will argue with, even in a good week. PubMed Central challenged nothing this week and served eight full texts. That is a fact about this week. We did not disguise the caller when the challenges were firing and we did not need to now, and the three-week sequence — all blocked, four of seven, none — is on the page because it is the only way a reader can tell how much of what we read is method and how much is luck.

Not anti-science. Three items here retire something standing — a functional story about why tetrapods have necks, a geometric rule for how a coat is laid out, and the deer at the centre of thirty years of Lyme advice — and a fourth retires a romantic account of where our own ingredients came from. Every one was corrected from the inside, by ten sturgeon on a bending rig, a chemotaxis model run against two species, three decades of plots counted by hand, and a Monte Carlo over cometary impacts. The Lyme authors fence their own result to intact forest in the same paper; the comet authors state that their own model is a best case. A field that marks the edge of its claim in the sentence after making it is working. If a draft off this digest starts reading as a case against the field, it has gone wrong.

Not because of. A butterfly was two butterflies all along in a band of light we cannot see, two coats fall out of one rule at two settings, and the atoms were made in stars whatever the courier turns out to have been. None of it is an argument for how anybody should be treated. That is a rhyme, not a proof. The universe declining to pathologize its own variation is why the phrase carries — down to your star stuff measures how far the loving reaches, never why it is owed.
