Edition of 20 November 2026

A weekly read of what is going well in science and medicine, graded before it is enjoyed.

The thirteenth edition of Glimmer Wire, the scan that sits behind Glimmers. Six versions of record were read end to end, every one of them out of a public archive rather than a publisher’s own site — not one publisher answered this week. Items are sorted by lag, which runs from 2 days to 233, median 12. Five are graded verified, two plausible, one contested. The lead item is a deficit hypothesis about Autistic brains, tested by the researchers who held it and abandoned in their own discussion — and the finding underneath is that two Autistic people’s neural patterns sit further apart than an Autistic and a non-Autistic person’s, so the group average describes nobody. A paper about penguins, from a drone over the Southern Ocean, reaches the same conclusion about averaging in the same week. The section that matters most is Held, which is weaker than last week’s and says so.

A refusal this page has listed for weeks was listed too broadly, and the error is ours. export.arxiv.org — the API host — is refused here, and that is where every edition has stopped. But arxiv.org itself answers: an abstract page returns HTTP 200 and 39,307 bytes, a subject listing 572,980. They are two hosts and only one of them is shut. So a preprint has been readable from this container by its own address the whole time, while we were recording arXiv among the unreachable because the route we were in the habit of using was unreachable. That is the bioRxiv correction of 30 October arriving again at a different host, and the lesson did not transfer the first time: a route that fails is not a source that is closed. Nothing in this edition turned on it. The next one may, and the blocked list above now names the API rather than the archive.

PubMed Central served six of seven articles and refused the seventh ten times running. Four of the six needed a second attempt; the seventh — a JAMA Network Open paper that is in Held — returned a reCAPTCHA interstitial on ten consecutive requests and never yielded. That is a fourth distinct state in four weeks: total block on 30 October, four of seven challenged on 6 November, none at all on 13 November, and now six of seven with one absolutely closed. The sequence is the whole argument for recording this every week instead of describing the archive once. We did not disguise the caller, which remains the rule whether or not anybody is checking.

Lag 2 d

They predicted we would be harder to predict, and we were not

Van De Water, Byrge, Kennedy, Kliemann and Traer showed six videos — five to twenty minutes each, from a Pixar short to film trailers — to 48 Autistic and 90 non-Autistic adults in an MRI scanner, and trained encoding models to predict each person’s brain activity from the sound, the images and more abstract social features of what they were watching. Their stated hypothesis was that our brain activity would be predicted less accurately and would vary more. It was not, and it did not. “Brain activity was predicted significantly above chance across brain networks and videos with striking consistency between groups,” and “within-participant predictions were equally variable between groups.” The discussion does not hedge this: “we consider the present work a robust rejection of our initial hypothesis.” What did differ is the shape of each person’s map. Autistic participants “exhibited lower within-group encoding pattern similarity” — and then the sentence the whole item turns on: “Strikingly, we observed the opposite direction of effects in the autistic group: the patterns of encoding accuracy observed in autistic participants were (on average) more similar to those of neurotypical participants than to other autistic participants.”

The star stuff frame There is no standard Autistic brain, and this is the first measurement we have seen that says so in the arithmetic rather than in the discussion. The claim is not that we are all different, which is cheap. It is narrower and much harder to wave away: on this measure the distance between two Autistic people is larger than the distance between an Autistic person and a non-Autistic one. A group average built from those patterns describes nobody. The authors’ own Significance Statement draws the conclusion in the version of record: “Variability in neural representation across individuals, rather than systemic population differences, may thus be associated with atypical brain function in complex social information processing in autism.” That is a sentence about a method, not about us — and it quietly removes the floor from a decade of studies that averaged a hundred Autistic brains and reported what the average did.
What the paper does not support, including where we would like it to The exploratory brain–behaviour result is the part to be careful with, and the paper is careful with it first. It reports that people whose patterns were more like other Autistic people’s scored higher on the AQ and the ADOS — a tempting sentence, and one we are declining to use, because the paper’s own complementary analysis “further confirmed no robust relations between within-across group similarity and AQ, ADOS, or MASC scores combined across videos after FDR correction (all pFDR > 0.05).” The directions also disagree between videos: greater within-group similarity went with higher ADOS scores in one Office clip and lower in the other. An exploratory finding that reverses between two halves of the same sitcom is not a finding. The sample is the second limit. 48 Autistic adults, 34 of them men, deliberately matched to “the male-to-female ratio observed in ASD populations at the time of study design (approximately 75% male)” — a ratio that is itself an artefact of who gets diagnosed, so the heterogeneity measured here is mostly heterogeneity among Autistic men. Mean full-scale IQ was 113.8. And the vocabulary is the paper’s, not ours: social difficulties, atypical, pathophysiology, impairment. We are quoting a result that dismantles a deficit hypothesis, written in the language that hypothesis came in. Both of those things are true at once and the second does not cancel the first.
Where it goes no standard autistic, group average, Libet and fMRI all return nothing across this site, and heterogene returns four pages, none of them about us. The nearest page is Helen Edgar’s Why Difference Comes First (No. 11), which argues the paradigm; this would be the measurement under it. The shelf’s standing formula — there is no standard star, nest, egg, shark, way to rest, which twelve field guides arrive at independently — has never once been pointed at a human nervous system, and this is the week somebody measured it. Unspent, and it is the best thing here.

Lag 5 d

The rock is the habitat, and it is on the list to be removed

Diaz-Recio Lorenzo and sixteen colleagues went through polymetallic nodules from the Clarion–Clipperton Zone of the central Pacific — the potato-sized mineral lumps that are the main target of deep-sea mining — and counted what lives in their cracks. “We show that nodules contain internal cracks and microfossils, that their crevices harbour meiofaunal communities dominated by nematodes and copepods, and that faunal abundance increases significantly with nodule volume.” The comparison is the point. Crevice nematode and copepod abundances ran at “approximately 10% and 25%, respectively,” of those in the sediment nearby — and on 18S rRNA the crevice assemblages “differed from those recovered from nearby sediments collected during the same expedition and within the same exploration contract areas.” Not a sample of the seabed that happened to be inside a rock. A different community, living in the rock.

The star stuff frame A nodule takes millions of years to grow and about a second to lift. What this paper adds is that the thing being lifted is not an ore body with animals on it but an address — and one whose population scales with its size, so the biggest nodules, which are the most worth taking, are the ones holding the most. A Field Guide to Life in Impossible Places (FG 3) collects habitats nobody would have designed for living in; a crack inside a metal concretion four kilometres down, colonised by animals you need a microscope to see, is squarely one of them. The abyssal plain reads as empty and is not, and the measurement that shows it is a count of animals per crevice.
The number in the headline is not in the abstract we read “245 animals living inside a single deep-sea rock” appears in the coverage and nowhere in the publisher-deposited abstract, which gives relative abundances and no per-nodule maximum. It is presumably in the paper; we could not check, because www.nature.com redirects to an authentication endpoint that then does not connect, and no PMCID exists yet. We are not repeating it as a finding and it is not in our summary above. The grade is plausible for that reason and no other: the abstract is complete, the method is described, and nothing in it is surprising — which is exactly when a number quoted from a press release is easiest to pass along. The authors also state their own interest plainly, closing on “the consequent risk of biodiversity loss associated with nodule removal through potential future commercial mining,” and that is a policy sentence in a results abstract. It is a reasonable one. It is still worth marking as the authors’ conclusion rather than their measurement.
Where it goes polymetallic and deep-sea mining both return zero across this site. Advice to a Young Marine Biologist (Trigger No. 2) grades twelve responses to the ocean’s crisis and does not mention the seabed at all — which is a gap in a page built to name them, and the cleanest placement here is a thirteenth rung rather than a new piece. Two Hundred Years Before You’re Grown (No. 34) already owns slow deep-sea growth against fast human decisions. Unspent: that the unit of destruction and the unit of habitat are the same object.

Lag 8 d

It gave up breaking down sugar and spent a quarter of its genome on the handshake

Yoshida and twelve colleagues sequenced the bacteria living in the body wall of Scotoplanes — the deep-sea sea cucumbers that walk the abyssal mud on tube feet and are called sea pigs — collected from the Northwest Pacific. They recovered two high-quality genomes, including a complete 649-kilobase circle, and named a new genus and species: “Candidatus Abyssoplasma scotoplanesicola.” It dominates absolutely, occupying “76.4%–98.9% of the body-wall microbiome” of the specimens, in aggregates that fluorescence imaging placed “on the epidermal side of the body wall.” And the genome is a study in what a symbiont stops doing and what it keeps. The two genomes “show a highly reduced metabolic repertoire, lacking central pathways including glycolysis” — it cannot break down glucose — while “acidic cell-surface-associated proteins, including large proteins exceeding 5000 amino acids, accounted for 27.6% of the complete genome and clustered near defense islands.” More than a quarter of everything it carries is the surface it meets its host with.

The star stuff frame This is the trade that You Were Never One Thing (No. 7) is about, caught in the act and costed. A free-living bacterium keeps glycolysis because it has to find its own sugar; this one threw the pathway away and reinvested the space in the interface. The genome is a ledger of a relationship — what you can stop carrying when something else carries it, and what you must carry more of when you live on somebody. The localised plasticity is the second half: comparing the two closely related genomes shows the surface-protein regions are where they differ, which the authors read as “a possible mechanism for the diversification of cell-surface proteins at the host-symbiont interface.” The part that touches the other organism is the part that varies fastest. Not a metaphor; a measurement, on an animal that looks like a toy.
The journal’s own metadata disagrees with itself about when this was published Crossref carries two dates for this paper and they cannot both be right: an online date of 11 November 2026 and a published-print date of 5 August 2026, three months earlier, with a record creation date of 28 October in between. A print issue cannot precede the online version by ninety-eight days, and this is volume 1, issue 1 of a journal launching, which is when deposits are likeliest to be wrong. We could not resolve it: academic.oup.com is refused at the network layer here, and Europe PMC and the PubMed eutils endpoints are too, so the three routes that would answer are all shut. The lag of 8 days above is therefore a ceiling, not a measurement, and if the August date is the real one the lag is 106. We have put the smaller number on the bar and are telling you it may be wrong by a factor of thirteen, which is the honest shape of a measurement whose input is a disputed field in somebody else’s database. The biology is unaffected either way. We did not read the paper, so the sampling depth, the specimen count and the phylogenetics behind the new genus are unseen here.
Where it goes subcuticular and holothuri are effectively unspent — the second returns one page and not for this. A Field Guide to Symbioses (FG 6) is the obvious home and this would be a new entry rather than a zine: a named symbiont, a host, a measured dominance and a genome that shows the cost. What is unspent and larger is the ledger reading — a genome as a record of what a relationship let you put down — which The Layer We Call Essential (No. 36) approaches from the other side.

Lag 10 d

The most crooked face in the class, and it was built that way on purpose

Benites-Palomino and ten colleagues describe Scaphokogia huachafo, a new pygmy sperm whale from the Late Miocene Pisco Formation of Peru, about six million years old. Toothed whales are already the most asymmetric skulls in the animal kingdom — “Directional asymmetry … reaches its most extreme manifestation in the skulls of odontocetes” — and this one goes further than any of them. Its “extreme craniofacial asymmetry … extends beyond the external bony nares to the surrounding cranial structures,” and geometric morphometric analysis indicates the genus “exhibits the most extreme facial asymmetry documented among cetaceans, and potentially among mammals.” The rest of the animal is as odd: a “robust, ventrally oriented, tubular rostrum composed of dense pachyostotic bone and reinforced by ossified cartilage,” pointed down at the seabed, with a reconstructed neck joint suggesting “enhanced ventral neck flexibility” — a whale built to feed face-down on the bottom. The authors’ summary: “a unique adaptation with no direct modern analogues.”

The star stuff frame This site already owns the argument and owns it in an owl. One Ear Higher Than the Other (No. 101) records that ear asymmetry evolved independently in at least five lines of owl, “built a different way each time,” that some owls have none, and that the little owl grows it as an embryo and loses it again. The whale is the same argument in a mammal and in the same sensory domain — cetacean facial asymmetry is tied to the sound-emitting structures in the forehead. A lopsided face is not a defect that got away; it is what a particular job looks like when it is solved. And the solution here has no living equivalent, which is the part a child notices first and the part a classification scheme handles worst: the animal does not fit the shelf, so the shelf was the wrong shape.
“Potentially among mammals” is the authors’ hedge and it should stay on The cetacean claim is measured; the mammal claim is an extrapolation and the paper marks it. Nobody has run the same geometric morphometrics across mammals generally, so “the most crooked face in mammal history” in the headline is firmer than “potentially among mammals” in the abstract — a small drift, in the direction headlines always drift. The functional reading is an inference too, and the paper words it as one: the benthic-feeding behaviour is “consistent with” a reconstructed joint in related species, not observed. And this is one fossil. A new species described from material in a single formation carries exactly the weight of that material, which is why “sp. nov.” is a proposal addressed to other palaeontologists and not a settled fact. We read the version of record in full, including the figures’ captions, and did not read the Supplementary Data, which holds the landmark coordinates the symmetry analysis is computed from.
Where it goes cranial asymmetry returns zero pages here; sperm whale returns seven, none about the skull. No. 101 is written for ages 4–8 and is the right companion rather than the right home — a one-paragraph note on its own page would do it, because its argument is five times, never the same way twice, and a whale makes it six and a mammal. Unspent: pachyostotic bone, the deliberately heavy kind, which this site has never written about despite being named around what bone does under load.

Lag 14 d

It does not replace the glaciers; it supplies what the glaciers needed

Dhungana and Flament ran palaeogeographically constrained mantle convection models back a billion years and compared the continental uplift and subsidence they predict against the volume of sediment preserved on continents, to ask what produced the Great Unconformity — the erosional gap of 200 million years or more that sits under the base of the Phanerozoic across Laurentia, Baltica and North China. Their claim: “continental-scale uplift and subsidence associated with long-wavelength dynamic topography … due to mantle convection drove the formation and preservation of the Great Unconformity.” The models predict “prolonged continental-scale Neoproterozoic uplift and long-term Phanerozoic subsidence” for all three cratons — rock lifted into the weather for a very long time, then dropped back down to be buried and kept. And the paper explains the geography of the evidence as well as the gap: “the large area of Laurentia and limited deformation at its margins explain why the Great Unconformity is best preserved and documented there” — North America is where it was first described, in the Grand Canyon.

The star stuff frame A missing billion years is a strange thing to be cheerful about, and the cheerful part is that the absence is itself a measurement. The rock is not there, and the shape of its not-being-there — where, for how long, on which continents — turns out to be readable, and to be about convection currents in the mantle a few thousand kilometres below. A gap in the record is a record of something else. This is also the week’s second item in which a thing everyone can see turns out to be produced by a process nobody can: the Grand Canyon’s most famous line, and a whale’s lopsided face.
Read the sentence after the claim: the glaciers are still doing the eroding “Scientists may finally know why” is not what this paper settles, and the paper itself keeps Snowball Earth in the story. Its own next sentence: “The predicted prolonged continental uplift throughout Cryogenian times would have resulted in marked erosion due to the action of glaciers.” Dynamic topography is proposed as what raised the rock; glacial erosion is still what removed it. The headline frames this as the answer replacing the old answer, and it is better described as the two fitting together — which is a less exciting sentence and a more useful one. The grade is contested on the method, not on the reading. We read the paper in full and it is careful; but the Great Unconformity has at least four live candidate mechanisms in the literature the authors themselves list — glacial erosion, rifting and plume activity, sea-level fall, dynamic topography — and a mantle convection model that is consistent with the sediment record is a consistency argument, not an observation of the Neoproterozoic. The authors tested six models and preferred one, M1, chosen because its predicted lower-mantle structures matched seismic tomography best; a different preferred model is a different set of uplift histories. Specialists will disagree about this for years and should.
Where it goes Great Unconformity and Snowball Earth both return zero here. The shape is a How We Got Here chain and the joints would be unusually honest — Powell naming the surface in the Grand Canyon, a century of blaming ice, a database of sedimentary volume, and a convection model — with the last joint marked contested rather than documented, because it is. Unspent, and the collection’s own rule applies with force: a chain that reaches its conclusion across a leap has gone wrong, and this one would have to stop short of the conclusion the headline reached.

Lag 23 d

The virus is in the other animal, and that is the finding

Gerdes and colleagues at Lund asked what retroviruses did to primate brain development. PTERV1 is an endogenous retrovirus family that invaded the chimpanzee genome after the split from our lineage and is “completely absent from the human genome,” leaving at least 150 fixed insertions in chimpanzees. Growing chimpanzee neural organoids, the team found those insertions “are heavily DNA methylated, forming over 150 species-specific heterochromatin domains” — pockets of shut-down chromosome. Then they found one that matters: an insertion sitting in intron 1 of the chimpanzee gene LINC00662, whose methylation spreads outward and silences it. “CRISPR-mediated deletion of this PTERV1 restored LINC00662 expression in chimpanzee induced pluripotent stem cells.” Cut the retrovirus out and the chimpanzee gene switches back on. And the gene does something: LINC00662 “plays a role in neuronal maturation, axon outgrowth, and neural organoid development.”

The star stuff frame Read the direction carefully, because it runs the opposite way from the way these stories are usually told. Nothing was added to us. The gene is in both species — the CRISPR experiment proves it, by turning the chimpanzee’s copy back on — and what humans have is the absence of an infection the other lineage caught. The paper even names a candidate reason: “the human version of TRIM5α prevents infection of human cells with PTERV1.” A difference between two kinds of primate, produced by a pandemic that one of them had and the other did not, and preserved as a thing that is switched off rather than a thing that was invented. That is the endosymbiosis argument arriving at a harder place: not only is some of you descended from something that infected your ancestors, some of what distinguishes you is a passenger your cousins picked up and you missed.
No ranking is available here, and the paper leans toward one in its first paragraph “An ancient virus may help explain what makes the human brain different” puts the virus in the wrong animal. PTERV1 is a chimpanzee retrovirus. The human contribution to this difference is having not been infected. That is not a quibble about phrasing; it changes which organism the reader pictures as having been acted upon. And the paper’s own framing wants watching. Its opening describes the human brain as standing out “as the largest and most complex” with “unique cognitive capabilities” — which is the register in which a mechanism becomes an achievement. The mechanism reported is a regulatory difference between two apes, and nothing in it licenses a ranking of the two. What is actually shown is also narrower than the title: one insertion, one lncRNA, in organoids and iPSCs. Neural organoids are not brains; they are a few millimetres of tissue with no body and no input. The paper says “contributed to” in its own title, which is right, and the wire says “may help explain what makes the human brain different,” which is a larger object.
Where it goes endogenous retrovirus and retroviru both return zero pages across this entire site, which is surprising for a collection with Every Nervous System (No. 27) and No. 7 on it. Unspent and it is a whole piece, not a note: a difference that is an absence. Every popular account of human distinctiveness is additive — a gene we gained, a region that grew — and here is a well-worked case where the human side of the ledger is a thing that did not happen. Minor Worlds (No. 16) is the nearest neighbour on not reading difference as rank.

Lag 24 d

Two populations, opposite directions, and the average says everything is fine

Mattern and eleven colleagues surveyed the entire global population of the Erect-crested penguin (Eudyptes sclateri) between 2022 and 2024 — the least studied penguin on Earth, confined to two subantarctic island groups, listed Endangered on a basis the authors describe as “limited and inconsistent observations from the 1970s.” Drone surveys across the whole range found about 27,800 breeding pairs on the Bounty Islands, roughly 50 hectares of rock, and about 29,800 on the Antipodes, “roughly 40 times larger and historically considered the species’ stronghold.” The two are going opposite ways. “The Bounty Islands population appears stable over at least the past 25 years, whereas the Antipodes Islands population has declined by more than 40% since the mid-1990s.” And then the sting, which is a sentence about arithmetic: “the binary population trajectories – one stable, one declining – combine to improve the global threat category while effectively obscuring a severe and genuine population-level crisis.” Summed, the species looks better. Looked at, half of it is collapsing.

The star stuff frame This is the week’s other paper about an average that describes nobody — and the two could not be further apart in field, method or subject. One is a scanner full of adults watching Pixar; this is a drone over a rock in the Southern Ocean. Both find that the group mean is an artefact of the grouping, and both find it by finally looking at the members separately. The authors reach for Schrödinger deliberately and invert him: the cat is in one state until observed, while the penguin is genuinely in two until somebody opens the box and stops averaging them. The proposed mechanisms are about place, not about the species: Bounty birds have access to “potentially more productive subtropical waters” out of season, while on the Antipodes “Subantarctic skua predation on fragmenting colonies generates a self-reinforcing Allee effect.” The same animal, doing differently because of where it is standing.
The trend data are thin and the authors say so first “Although data since the mid-1990s are sparse, available evidence suggests divergent population trajectories” — that hedge is theirs, it is in the abstract, and it governs both halves of the result. The 2022–24 census is solid; the trajectories are drawn from a handful of earlier counts of varying quality, and the paper notes the 1978 baseline of 115,000 pairs at each island group has had “its validity … questioned.” A decline computed against a disputed starting figure inherits the dispute. The conclusion is also a recommendation, and it argues against its own good news: the authors note that stability at the Bounty Islands “would likely reduce the species’ global conservation status to ‘Vulnerable’” and warn this “may even weaken the fisheries protections critical to safeguarding what may be the species’ last stable stronghold.” That is a policy argument from scientists about how their own finding will be used, clearly marked as such in the paper, and we are reporting it as their position rather than as a result.
Where it goes IUCN and Red List return nine and ten pages here, and none of them is about the aggregation step. Unspent, and it pairs with the lead item: a measurement that is an average over heterogeneous members tells you about the average and not about the members — in a brain scanner and on a rock in the Southern Ocean in the same week. Two Hundred Years Before You’re Grown (No. 34) and Advice to a Young Marine Biologist (Trigger No. 2) are the neighbours; the Trigger’s grading ladder is the closer relative, because this is a paper about a scale being read as a ranking.

Lag 233 d

The paper never says “free will,” and we counted

Fong, Garrett, Smith, Hester, Bode and Feuerriegel recorded EEG from 49 adults making two kinds of decision — voluntary, a free choice between two options, and forced, where only one was available — with every response made by the same right-handed keypress, so that deciding could be separated from choosing a hand. The result is about the readiness potential, the slow negative wave that has been read since the 1960s as the brain committing before you know you have. Here the centro-parietal positivity and Mu/Beta signals “showed hallmark accumulation-to-bound dynamics — faster responses had steeper ramping signals that converged to stereotyped pre-response amplitude levels,” the signature of evidence piling up toward a threshold. The readiness potential did not behave that way: it converged too, but “with less reliable evidence for response time (RT)-dependent slope scaling, resembling a late, effector-specific motor gate rather than an evolving decision variable.” And “waveform amplitudes across the time course of these signals also did not differ reliably between voluntary and forced decisions.”

The star stuff frame A sixty-year-old story about what a brain wave means is being quietly replaced by a duller and better one, and the duller one is good news. If the readiness potential is a motor gate rather than a decision, then the famous finding — your brain decided before you did — was reading the wrong signal. The decision variable is the accumulation, and it works the same way whether the choice is yours or handed to you, which is a claim about machinery rather than about agency. And it retires an explanation rather than adding one, which is the shape this scan looks for hardest.
“Free will” is the wire’s frame, not the paper’s, and this is checkable by counting The string Libet occurs zero times in this paper. The phrase free will occurs twice, and both occurrences are in the reference list, inside the titles of other people’s papers. The authors’ own text uses “voluntary decisions” and “volition” and never raises the philosophical question at all. “Is free will an illusion?” is a headline over a paper that declines the subject — which we are stating as a fact about the two documents rather than as a complaint, because the wire item here is unusual: it is syndicated from The Conversation rather than from a press release, and one of the paper’s authors writes for that outlet. A researcher explaining their own work in a popular register is entitled to a frame the paper does not use. The reader meeting it on ScienceDaily is not told which is which. That also explains the 233-day lag, and makes it a different object from the long lags this page usually prints. Nothing was neglected for eight months; the paper came out in March and an explainer was written in November. Our axis measures journal-to-wire and cannot tell a slow press release from a later essay, so the bar is accurate and the inference a reader would draw from it is not. On the science: 49 adults, one task, EEG. The readiness-potential result is the negative half of the finding and is stated as “less reliable evidence” rather than absence, which is the correct weight for it.
Where it goes Libet, readiness potential and free will all return zero across this site. This is a How We Got Here chain with its joints already visible — Kornhuber and Deecke in 1965, Libet in the 1980s, thirty years of the finding in popular psychology, Schurger’s reinterpretation, and a 2026 measurement that reads the wave as a gate. Unspent, and it is also a Too Good to Check case of the first water: a result everyone can recite, which travelled because it was startling, resting on a signal that may not have been the one anybody thought.

Held

Six this week, and the composition is weaker than last week’s, which is stated here rather than buried. Three are held on a complete publisher-deposited abstract read end to end — the claim is simply not the one the headline makes. One is held on the paper’s own title, which names the species the work was done in. And two are held partly because we could not open them at all, which is the weakest kind of hold and is marked as such in each. Two of the six carry lags over two hundred days, and in both cases the age is part of why they are here: a finding that has been available since spring is not news, and treating it as news is its own kind of inaccuracy. A held item is not a bad study. Four of these six are work we have no quarrel with at all.

Held — the authors propose a detector and the headline hands the reader a behaviour to change

A 19-year study reveals a troubling link between napping and death

The study is good and the inference the headline invites is not in it. Gao and colleagues put actigraphy watches on 1,338 community-dwelling older adults in the Rush Memory and Aging Project — mean age 81.4 years, 76% women — and followed them for up to 19 years, during which 926 of them (69.2%) died. Longer naps were associated with higher mortality at “adjusted hazard ratio [AHR] per 1-hour increase, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.04-1.23,” more frequent naps at “AHR per additional daily nap, 1.07,” and morning nappers fared worse than early-afternoon ones at “AHR, 1.30.” Then read the authors’ own conclusion, which is the whole reason this is held: the findings “suggest incorporating wearable device–based daytime nap assessments into clinical and public health practices may provide novel opportunities for early risk identification of health conditions.” That is a proposal to use napping as an instrument for noticing illness. It is not a proposal to nap less, and nothing in a cohort of eighty-one-year-olds could distinguish napping that causes harm from napping that is an early symptom of the thing that does. In a sample this age the reverse direction is the obvious reading and the paper is written as though it knows that. The hazard ratios are also modest — 1.13 per hour, with a lower bound of 1.04 — and the variability measure was flatly null (“AHR per 1-hour increase, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.89-1.14; P = .93”). We could not read the full text, and it is the one article PubMed Central refused this week, ten consecutive times. This is the only held item where that refusal might have changed the hold, because the Discussion is where reverse causation would be argued, and we have not seen it. Held — the paper is about physical activity, and “inversely associated with reaction time” means faster

Scientists tracked kids for 8 years. What they found about screen time was surprising

Two things are wrong here and only the second one is subtle. The paper is titled “Associations of Physical Activity and Sedentary Time From Childhood to Adolescence With Cognition in Adolescence” — screen time is one self-reported exposure among several in a study whose subject is movement, and the coverage promotes it to the headline. The subtle one is a sign. The abstract reports that “self-reported screen time was inversely associated with reaction time” in working-memory tasks — and in a CogState battery lower reaction time is better, so an inverse association means the children with more screen time responded faster. A reader who has not met the convention will read “inversely associated with reaction time” as a harm. Meanwhile nonscreen-based sedentary time was inversely associated with both reaction time and accuracy — faster and less accurate — which is the signature of a speed–accuracy trade-off rather than of better or worse cognition, and the abstract does not read it that way. The conclusion as deposited is: “Accumulating less unsupervised PA and more screen time from childhood to adolescence was associated with better cognition in adolescence.” That is a startling sentence to find under this headline and it is the authors’. Held because the effect sizes are small (standardised β from −0.127 to 0.187, several confidence intervals nearly touching zero), the exposures are self-reported, n = 260, and the design is observational: a cohort in which the children who report more screen time differ in a hundred other ways. One clause of the deposited abstract is garbled — it lists two coefficients for screen time and labels only the first — so even the measurement we are declining to repeat cannot be read cleanly from the record we have. Held — the title says mice and the headline says “your brain”

Too much exercise could leave your brain stuck in overdrive

The paper’s own title is the entire hold: “Excessive training alters hippocampal tyrosine hydroxylase and dopamine homeostasis in mice.” The species is the last two words of the title and is absent from the headline, from the standfirst and from the summary, which addresses the reader’s own brain in the second person. This is the most common failure on this page and the easiest to check — the journal reference is printed at the foot of the same page as the headline. Nothing here is a criticism of the work, which looks like a careful rodent neurochemistry study and makes no claim about humans. An overtraining protocol in mice is a forced-exercise regime with no human equivalent in either dose or consent, and a reader who takes the headline at face value has been handed an instruction about their own training from an experiment nobody could run on a person. Held — “every single one” over a paper that has been available for at least six months

Scientists tested 212 plant-based foods. Every single one contained fungal toxins

The date is the first problem. Crossref records this paper in Food Control volume 183, a May 2026 issue, with a record created on 9 December 2025 — so it has been public somewhere between six and eleven months, and the wire carried it on 19 November as a finding. Held — the measurement is a carbon isotope ratio, and seaweed is one candidate explanation for it

Dinosaurs may have been eating seaweed 100 million years ago

What was measured is δ13C in tooth enamel, and what was found is that coastal Cretaceous vertebrates carry more of it than inland ones. Forster, Suarez, Cullen, Zanno and Hyland compared enamel from several coastal formations against the inland Cloverly Formation and report “evidence of MS of coastal ecosystems with relatively high δ13C value of coastal vertebrate enamel (up to >5.5‰ above expected δ13C enamel values) but not in upland environments.” MS is marine subsidization — “marine-derived organic matter, energy, and nutrients” entering a land ecosystem, by any route. Seaweed is the mechanism the abstract reaches for, since “macroalgae and marine plants use a modified C3 photosynthetic pathway resulting in a relatively increased δ13C value” — but an isotope ratio in enamel records what the carbon was, not what the animal chose to put in its mouth, and a dinosaur eating plants grown in salt spray or an animal that ate something marine would land in the same place. “May have been eating seaweed” hedges correctly and still names a meal the enamel cannot distinguish. The genuinely strong claim is methodological and the abstract leads with it in its last lines: “previous estimates did not include marine-derived carbon sources in calculations,” so a body of published Cretaceous dietary reconstructions is off by an unaccounted term. That is a retired method, and we would have filed it on that — held because we could not open the paper to see how large the correction is or how many studies it reaches, www.frontiersin.org being refused at the network layer. Worth recording: the lead image on this item is credited to a machine, which on a story about what an extinct animal ate is a picture of nothing anybody observed. Seedbed

Three. Notes toward pieces, not drafts — each says what was read, what was not, and what would have to be obtained before anybody built it. The first is the strongest seed this page has produced, because two papers from unrelated fields arrived in the same week making the same methodological argument, and neither cites the other.

Zine · Star Stuff — one settled fact, followed until the belonging claim is already inside it

The average is a member of nothing

A scanner in Iowa and a drone over the Southern Ocean produced the same finding in the same week. In one, Autistic adults’ neural encoding patterns were on average more similar to non-Autistic people’s than to each other’s, so the group mean describes no member of the group. In the other, two penguin populations moving in opposite directions sum to a global trend that is reassuring and false, and the authors say in their abstract that the aggregate “effectively obscur[es] a severe and genuine population-level crisis.” Neither paper knows about the other. The fact that would carry the piece is older and settled: an average is a statistic about a set, and nothing obliges a member of the set to resemble it — which is trivially true, universally taught, and routinely forgotten the moment a group has a name. The belonging claim is already inside it and needs no help: if the mean is an artefact of the grouping, then a person compared to it is being compared to nobody. The danger is the opposite of the usual one. This argument is so congenial to this site that it would be easy to overstate — the autism paper's own effect is on average, with real exceptions by video and by brain region, and the penguin trend data are explicitly sparse. The piece has to survive its own best evidence being hedged.

From the J Neurosci and PLOS One papers filed above, both read in full. Not obtained: the autism paper’s Table S4, which holds every per-video similarity statistic the central claim rests on, and the penguin paper’s supporting information. Also needed before building: the statistical literature on ecological fallacy and on Simpson’s paradox, traced to primaries rather than to summaries — this is a well-worked area and a zine that rediscovers it without citation would be doing the thing the chains exist to argue against.

Zine · Star Stuff — a difference that is an absence

Nothing happened to us, and that is the difference

Every popular account of what makes humans distinctive is additive. A gene we gained, a region that expanded, a mutation that arrived. The PTERV1 paper filed above is a worked counter-example: the chimpanzee lineage was invaded by a retrovirus that left at least 150 fixed insertions, one of which sits inside LINC00662 and silences it, and the human version of that gene is expressed because our ancestors were not infected — possibly because of a difference in TRIM5α. The CRISPR experiment is what makes it a fact rather than a story: delete the retrovirus from chimpanzee cells and the chimpanzee gene comes back on. The shape is the argument. A difference held as an absence cannot be read as an achievement, which is precisely why this version will never be the one that travels. And it points the same way as the shelf already points: difference as a record of what happened to whom, not a ranking of who ended up better. The piece would have to be careful in one specific direction — the paper's own opening describes the human brain as “the largest and most complex” with “unique cognitive capabilities”, and a zine quoting its result while quietly dropping that sentence would be doing the smoothing this page is written against. Quote it and argue with it.

From the Science Advances paper filed above, read in full at PubMed Central. Not obtained: the supplementary figures, and the TRIM5α primary (Kaiser, Malik & Emerman and successors) that the resistance claim rests on — which would have to be traced to source, since here it is a citation inside somebody else’s introduction. A piece built on “we were not infected” owes the reader the evidence for why.

Chain · How We Got Here — sixty years of a wave meaning one thing

The signal was never the decision

Kornhuber and Deecke described the readiness potential in 1965; Libet built the famous experiment on it; and for forty years the finding that the brain begins to act before you report deciding has been the single most quoted result in popular neuroscience. The paper filed above reports that the wave looks like “a late, effector-specific motor gate rather than an evolving decision variable”, and that voluntary and forced choices do not differ in it. If that holds, the most repeated claim in the field was about the wrong signal — not wrong data, a wrong identification. That is a Burke chain with unusually clean joints, and the final one would be marked contested, because one EEG study of 49 adults does not retire sixty years. It is also the house’s own standing warning arriving in the best possible place: the smoothed popular retelling here is not a tabloid’s, it is one the field told about itself and repeated for decades. And the piece has a second subject it must not duck: this scan reached the result through a syndicated explainer written by one of the authors, 233 days after publication, under a headline the paper itself never uses. How a finding reaches people is part of how we got here.

From the Imaging Neuroscience paper filed above, read in full at PubMed Central, including its reference list. Not obtained, and all of it required before building: Kornhuber & Deecke 1965, Libet’s own papers, and Schurger, Sitt & Dehaene — the paper cites Schurger once and this chain would stand on him. A chain whose joints are all quoted from one recent paper’s introduction is not a chain; it is that introduction with pictures.

Not

Not a citation. Six papers were read end to end this week and the standing rule does not move an inch for it. Nothing filed here may be promoted to a zine, a Glimmers entry or a card without the primary source in hand, a changelog entry and its row in the fact-check ledger. Two of the eight filed items rest on deposited abstracts and are graded accordingly. And one grade here is about us rather than about the paper: the Great Unconformity item is contested because the question has four live mechanisms in its own literature, not because anything in the modelling looked wrong.

Not a verdict on an Autistic brain, including a flattering one. A study of 48 Autistic adults found that our encoding patterns resemble non-Autistic people’s more than they resemble each other’s. That is a finding about a measurement and about what averaging does to it. It is not evidence that we are more various than other people in some general sense, it is not a superpower, and it is not a diagnosis — the paper’s own brain–behaviour correlations collapsed after correction and reversed direction between two clips of the same programme. We write from inside this, which raises the bar rather than lowering it: a result that flatters us is the one we are least able to check, and the only honest use of it is the narrow methodological one the authors themselves make.

Not an instruction about your own body, from mice or from eighty-one-year-olds. A forced-overtraining protocol in mice became “your brain stuck in overdrive”; an actigraphy cohort whose authors propose napping as an early signal of illness became a troubling link between napping and death. In both cases the headline hands the reader a behaviour to change, and in both cases the paper does not. The second is the sharper one, because the direction is almost certainly backwards: in a sample with a mean age of eighty-one and a 69% mortality rate, the nap is far likelier to be a symptom than a cause.

Not a ranking of two kinds of ape. A retrovirus infected the chimpanzee lineage and not ours, silencing a gene whose human copy stays on. The difference is that something happened to them and did not happen to us — which is a fact about an ancient pandemic, not a measure of either animal. The paper's own first paragraph reaches for “largest and most complex” and “unique cognitive capabilities”, and we are quoting the mechanism while declining the register it arrived in.

Not a metaphor, in either direction. A symbiont gave up glycolysis and spent 27.6% of its genome on the surface where it meets its host; a sea pig walks the abyssal mud wearing it. The sentence this site is built to want is right there and it is about a bacterium in a body wall four kilometres down. Nothing in that experiment is about anybody’s worth, and the thing that makes it worth having — that no one involved was arguing about human value — is exactly what carrying it across would destroy. The same goes for a nodule full of nematodes, and for a whale with the most crooked face in its class.

Not a bot check we will argue with, in a good week or a bad one. PubMed Central served six of seven articles and refused the seventh ten times running, and that seventh is the only paper this week whose full text might have changed what we wrote. We did not disguise the caller. The four-week sequence — all blocked, four of seven, none, six of seven — is on the page because it is the only way a reader can tell how much of what we read is method and how much is luck. And one entry on that list was our own mistake, not the archive’s: arXiv has been reachable here all along at arxiv.org while we recorded it as refused because export.arxiv.org is. A route that fails is not a source that is closed, and this is the second host we have learned that on.

Not anti-science. Four items here retire something standing — a deficit hypothesis about Autistic brains, a sixty-year reading of the readiness potential, a conservation status computed by addition, and a dietary reconstruction missing a carbon source — and every one was corrected from the inside. The strongest case is the lead: a team hypothesised that we would be harder to predict, tested it across six videos, two models and five spatial scales, found nothing, and wrote “we consider the present work a robust rejection of our initial hypothesis” in their own discussion. That sentence is what a working field looks like. The penguin authors argue against the good news their own paper produces; the Great Unconformity authors keep the glaciers in the mechanism they are replacing. If a draft off this digest starts reading as a case against the field, it has gone wrong.

Not because of. No two Autistic brains encode a film the same way, a whale’s face is lopsided because of what it listens for, and a gene is on in us because a virus missed our ancestors. None of it is an argument for how anybody should be treated. That is a rhyme, not a proof. The universe declining to pathologize its own variation is why the phrase carries — down to your star stuff measures how far the loving reaches, never why it is owed.
