### They predicted we would be harder to predict, and we were not

Van De Water, Byrge, Kennedy, Kliemann and Traer showed six videos — five to twenty minutes each, from a Pixar short to film trailers — to 48 Autistic and 90 non-Autistic adults in an MRI scanner, and trained encoding models to predict each person’s brain activity from the sound, the images and more abstract social features of what they were watching. **Their stated hypothesis was that our brain activity would be predicted less accurately and would vary more.** It was not, and it did not. “Brain activity was predicted significantly above chance across brain networks and videos with striking consistency between groups,” and “within-participant predictions were equally variable between groups.” The discussion does not hedge this: **“we consider the present work a robust rejection of our initial hypothesis.”** What did differ is the shape of each person’s map. Autistic participants “exhibited lower within-group encoding pattern similarity” — and then the sentence the whole item turns on: **“Strikingly, we observed the opposite direction of effects in the autistic group: the patterns of encoding accuracy observed in autistic participants were (on average) more similar to those of neurotypical participants than to other autistic participants.”**

**The star stuff frame** **There is no standard Autistic brain, and this is the first measurement we have seen that says so in the arithmetic rather than in the discussion.** The claim is not that we are all different, which is cheap. It is narrower and much harder to wave away: on this measure *the distance between two Autistic people is larger than the distance between an Autistic person and a non-Autistic one*. A group average built from those patterns describes nobody. **The authors’ own Significance Statement draws the conclusion in the version of record:** “Variability in neural representation across individuals, rather than systemic population differences, may thus be associated with atypical brain function in complex social information processing in autism.” *That is a sentence about a method, not about us* — and it quietly removes the floor from a decade of studies that averaged a hundred Autistic brains and reported what the average did.

**What the paper does not support, including where we would like it to** **The exploratory brain–behaviour result is the part to be careful with, and the paper is careful with it first.** It reports that people whose patterns were more like other Autistic people’s scored higher on the AQ and the ADOS — a tempting sentence, and one we are declining to use, because the paper’s own complementary analysis “further confirmed no robust relations between within-across group similarity and AQ, ADOS, or MASC scores combined across videos after FDR correction (all pFDR \> 0.05).” The directions also disagree between videos: greater within-group similarity went with *higher* ADOS scores in one Office clip and *lower* in the other. *An exploratory finding that reverses between two halves of the same sitcom is not a finding.* **The sample is the second limit.** 48 Autistic adults, 34 of them men, deliberately matched to “the male-to-female ratio observed in ASD populations at the time of study design (approximately 75% male)” — a ratio that is itself an artefact of who gets diagnosed, so the heterogeneity measured here is mostly heterogeneity among Autistic men. Mean full-scale IQ was 113.8. **And the vocabulary is the paper’s, not ours:** *social difficulties*, *atypical*, *pathophysiology*, *impairment*. We are quoting a result that dismantles a deficit hypothesis, written in the language that hypothesis came in. *Both of those things are true at once and the second does not cancel the first.*

**Where it goes** no standard autistic, group average, Libet and fMRI all return nothing across this site, and heterogene returns four pages, none of them about us. **The nearest page is Helen Edgar’s** [*Why Difference Comes First*](https://starstuff.earth/why-difference-comes-first-zine.html) (No. 11), which argues the paradigm; this would be the measurement under it. The shelf’s standing formula — *there is no standard star, nest, egg, shark, way to rest*, which twelve [field guides](https://starstuff.earth/collection-field-guides.html) arrive at independently — has never once been pointed at a human nervous system, and this is the week somebody measured it. **Unspent, and it is the best thing here.**

### The rock is the habitat, and it is on the list to be removed

Diaz-Recio Lorenzo and sixteen colleagues went through polymetallic nodules from the Clarion–Clipperton Zone of the central Pacific — the potato-sized mineral lumps that are the main target of deep-sea mining — and counted what lives in their cracks. “We show that nodules contain internal cracks and microfossils, that their crevices harbour meiofaunal communities dominated by nematodes and copepods, and that faunal abundance increases significantly with nodule volume.” **The comparison is the point.** Crevice nematode and copepod abundances ran at “approximately 10% and 25%, respectively,” of those in the sediment nearby — and on 18S rRNA the crevice assemblages “differed from those recovered from nearby sediments collected during the same expedition and within the same exploration contract areas.” *Not a sample of the seabed that happened to be inside a rock. A different community, living in the rock.*

**The star stuff frame** **A nodule takes millions of years to grow and about a second to lift.** What this paper adds is that the thing being lifted is not an ore body with animals on it but an *address* — and one whose population scales with its size, so the biggest nodules, which are the most worth taking, are the ones holding the most. [*A Field Guide to Life in Impossible Places*](https://starstuff.earth/impossible-places-field-guide.html) (FG 3) collects habitats nobody would have designed for living in; a crack inside a metal concretion four kilometres down, colonised by animals you need a microscope to see, is squarely one of them. *The abyssal plain reads as empty and is not, and the measurement that shows it is a count of animals per crevice.*

**The number in the headline is not in the abstract we read** **“245 animals living inside a single deep-sea rock” appears in the coverage and nowhere in the publisher-deposited abstract**, which gives relative abundances and no per-nodule maximum. It is presumably in the paper; we could not check, because www.nature.com redirects to an authentication endpoint that then does not connect, and no PMCID exists yet. *We are not repeating it as a finding and it is not in our summary above.* **The grade is plausible for that reason and no other:** the abstract is complete, the method is described, and nothing in it is surprising — which is exactly when a number quoted from a press release is easiest to pass along. *The authors also state their own interest plainly*, closing on “the consequent risk of biodiversity loss associated with nodule removal through potential future commercial mining,” and that is a policy sentence in a results abstract. It is a reasonable one. It is still worth marking as the authors’ conclusion rather than their measurement.

**Where it goes** polymetallic and deep-sea mining both return zero across this site. [*Advice to a Young Marine Biologist*](https://starstuff.earth/trigger-young-marine-biologist.html) (Trigger No. 2) grades twelve responses to the ocean’s crisis and does not mention the seabed at all — **which is a gap in a page built to name them**, and the cleanest placement here is a thirteenth rung rather than a new piece. [*Two Hundred Years Before You’re Grown*](https://starstuff.earth/two-hundred-years-zine.html) (No. 34) already owns slow deep-sea growth against fast human decisions. *Unspent: that the unit of destruction and the unit of habitat are the same object.*

### It gave up breaking down sugar and spent a quarter of its genome on the handshake

Yoshida and twelve colleagues sequenced the bacteria living in the body wall of *Scotoplanes* — the deep-sea sea cucumbers that walk the abyssal mud on tube feet and are called sea pigs — collected from the Northwest Pacific. They recovered two high-quality genomes, including a complete 649-kilobase circle, and named a new genus and species: *“Candidatus Abyssoplasma scotoplanesicola.”* **It dominates absolutely**, occupying “76.4%–98.9% of the body-wall microbiome” of the specimens, in aggregates that fluorescence imaging placed “on the epidermal side of the body wall.” **And the genome is a study in what a symbiont stops doing and what it keeps.** The two genomes “show a highly reduced metabolic repertoire, lacking central pathways including glycolysis” — it cannot break down glucose — while “acidic cell-surface-associated proteins, including large proteins exceeding 5000 amino acids, accounted for 27.6% of the complete genome and clustered near defense islands.” *More than a quarter of everything it carries is the surface it meets its host with.*

**The star stuff frame** **This is the trade that [*You Were Never One Thing*](https://starstuff.earth/you-were-never-one-thing-zine.html) (No. 7) is about, caught in the act and costed.** A free-living bacterium keeps glycolysis because it has to find its own sugar; this one threw the pathway away and reinvested the space in the interface. *The genome is a ledger of a relationship* — what you can stop carrying when something else carries it, and what you must carry more of when you live on somebody. **The localised plasticity is the second half:** comparing the two closely related genomes shows the surface-protein regions are where they differ, which the authors read as “a possible mechanism for the diversification of cell-surface proteins at the host-symbiont interface.” The part that touches the other organism is the part that varies fastest. *Not a metaphor; a measurement, on an animal that looks like a toy.*

**The journal’s own metadata disagrees with itself about when this was published** **Crossref carries two dates for this paper and they cannot both be right:** an online date of 11 November 2026 and a *published-print* date of 5 August 2026, three months earlier, with a record creation date of 28 October in between. *A print issue cannot precede the online version by ninety-eight days*, and this is volume 1, issue 1 of a journal launching, which is when deposits are likeliest to be wrong. **We could not resolve it:** academic.oup.com is refused at the network layer here, and Europe PMC and the PubMed eutils endpoints are too, so the three routes that would answer are all shut. **The lag of 8 days above is therefore a ceiling, not a measurement**, and if the August date is the real one the lag is 106. We have put the smaller number on the bar and are telling you it may be wrong by a factor of thirteen, *which is the honest shape of a measurement whose input is a disputed field in somebody else’s database.* The biology is unaffected either way. *We did not read the paper*, so the sampling depth, the specimen count and the phylogenetics behind the new genus are unseen here.

**Where it goes** subcuticular and holothuri are effectively unspent — the second returns one page and not for this. [*A Field Guide to Symbioses*](https://starstuff.earth/symbioses-field-guide.html) (FG 6) is the obvious home and this would be a new entry rather than a zine: a named symbiont, a host, a measured dominance and a genome that shows the cost. **What is unspent and larger is the ledger reading** — *a genome as a record of what a relationship let you put down* — which [*The Layer We Call Essential*](https://starstuff.earth/the-layer-we-call-essential-zine.html) (No. 36) approaches from the other side.

### The most crooked face in the class, and it was built that way on purpose

Benites-Palomino and ten colleagues describe *Scaphokogia huachafo*, a new pygmy sperm whale from the Late Miocene Pisco Formation of Peru, about six million years old. Toothed whales are already the most asymmetric skulls in the animal kingdom — “Directional asymmetry … reaches its most extreme manifestation in the skulls of odontocetes” — and this one goes further than any of them. **Its “extreme craniofacial asymmetry … extends beyond the external bony nares to the surrounding cranial structures,”** and geometric morphometric analysis indicates the genus “exhibits the most extreme facial asymmetry documented among cetaceans, and potentially among mammals.” The rest of the animal is as odd: a “robust, ventrally oriented, tubular rostrum composed of dense pachyostotic bone and reinforced by ossified cartilage,” pointed down at the seabed, with a reconstructed neck joint suggesting “enhanced ventral neck flexibility” — a whale built to feed face-down on the bottom. **The authors’ summary: “a unique adaptation with no direct modern analogues.”**

**The star stuff frame** **This site already owns the argument and owns it in an owl.** [*One Ear Higher Than the Other*](https://starstuff.earth/one-ear-higher-than-the-other-zine.html) (No. 101) records that ear asymmetry evolved independently in at least five lines of owl, “built a different way each time,” that some owls have none, and that the little owl grows it as an embryo and loses it again. *The whale is the same argument in a mammal and in the same sensory domain* — cetacean facial asymmetry is tied to the sound-emitting structures in the forehead. **A lopsided face is not a defect that got away; it is what a particular job looks like when it is solved.** And the solution here has no living equivalent, which is the part a child notices first and the part a classification scheme handles worst: the animal does not fit the shelf, so the shelf was the wrong shape.

**“Potentially among mammals” is the authors’ hedge and it should stay on** **The cetacean claim is measured; the mammal claim is an extrapolation and the paper marks it.** Nobody has run the same geometric morphometrics across mammals generally, so “the most crooked face in mammal history” in the headline is firmer than “potentially among mammals” in the abstract — a small drift, in the direction headlines always drift. *The functional reading is an inference too*, and the paper words it as one: the benthic-feeding behaviour is “consistent with” a reconstructed joint in *related* species, not observed. **And this is one fossil.** A new species described from material in a single formation carries exactly the weight of that material, which is why “sp. nov.” is a proposal addressed to other palaeontologists and not a settled fact. *We read the version of record in full, including the figures’ captions, and did not read the Supplementary Data*, which holds the landmark coordinates the symmetry analysis is computed from.

**Where it goes** cranial asymmetry returns zero pages here; sperm whale returns seven, none about the skull. No. 101 is written for ages 4–8 and is the right companion rather than the right home — **a one-paragraph note on its own page would do it**, because its argument is *five times, never the same way twice*, and a whale makes it six and a mammal. *Unspent: pachyostotic bone*, the deliberately heavy kind, which this site has never written about despite being named around what bone does under load.

### It does not replace the glaciers; it supplies what the glaciers needed

Dhungana and Flament ran palaeogeographically constrained mantle convection models back a billion years and compared the continental uplift and subsidence they predict against the volume of sediment preserved on continents, to ask what produced the Great Unconformity — the erosional gap of 200 million years or more that sits under the base of the Phanerozoic across Laurentia, Baltica and North China. **Their claim: “continental-scale uplift and subsidence associated with long-wavelength dynamic topography … due to mantle convection drove the formation and preservation of the Great Unconformity.”** The models predict “prolonged continental-scale Neoproterozoic uplift and long-term Phanerozoic subsidence” for all three cratons — rock lifted into the weather for a very long time, then dropped back down to be buried and kept. *And the paper explains the geography of the evidence as well as the gap:* “the large area of Laurentia and limited deformation at its margins explain why the Great Unconformity is best preserved and documented there” — North America is where it was first described, in the Grand Canyon.

**The star stuff frame** **A missing billion years is a strange thing to be cheerful about, and the cheerful part is that the absence is itself a measurement.** The rock is not there, and the shape of its not-being-there — where, for how long, on which continents — turns out to be readable, and to be about convection currents in the mantle a few thousand kilometres below. *A gap in the record is a record of something else.* This is also the week’s second item in which a thing everyone can see turns out to be produced by a process nobody can: the Grand Canyon’s most famous line, and a whale’s lopsided face.

**Read the sentence after the claim: the glaciers are still doing the eroding** **“Scientists may finally know why” is not what this paper settles, and the paper itself keeps Snowball Earth in the story.** Its own next sentence: **“The predicted prolonged continental uplift throughout Cryogenian times would have resulted in marked erosion due to the action of glaciers.”** *Dynamic topography is proposed as what raised the rock; glacial erosion is still what removed it.* The headline frames this as the answer replacing the old answer, and it is better described as the two fitting together — which is a less exciting sentence and a more useful one. **The grade is contested on the method, not on the reading.** We read the paper in full and it is careful; but the Great Unconformity has at least four live candidate mechanisms in the literature the authors themselves list — glacial erosion, rifting and plume activity, sea-level fall, dynamic topography — and a mantle convection model that is *consistent with* the sediment record is a consistency argument, not an observation of the Neoproterozoic. *The authors tested six models and preferred one, M1*, chosen because its predicted lower-mantle structures matched seismic tomography best; a different preferred model is a different set of uplift histories. **Specialists will disagree about this for years and should.**

**Where it goes** Great Unconformity and Snowball Earth both return zero here. **The shape is a [How We Got Here](https://starstuff.earth/collection-how-we-got-here.html) chain and the joints would be unusually honest** — Powell naming the surface in the Grand Canyon, a century of blaming ice, a database of sedimentary volume, and a convection model — with the last joint marked contested rather than documented, because it is. *Unspent, and the collection’s own rule applies with force:* a chain that reaches its conclusion across a leap has gone wrong, and this one would have to stop short of the conclusion the headline reached.

### The virus is in the other animal, and that is the finding

Gerdes and colleagues at Lund asked what retroviruses did to primate brain development. **PTERV1 is an endogenous retrovirus family that invaded the chimpanzee genome after the split from our lineage and is “completely absent from the human genome,”** leaving at least 150 fixed insertions in chimpanzees. Growing chimpanzee neural organoids, the team found those insertions “are heavily DNA methylated, forming over 150 species-specific heterochromatin domains” — pockets of shut-down chromosome. Then they found one that matters: an insertion sitting in *intron 1* of the chimpanzee gene *LINC00662*, whose methylation spreads outward and silences it. **“CRISPR-mediated deletion of this PTERV1 restored LINC00662 expression in chimpanzee induced pluripotent stem cells.”** Cut the retrovirus out and the chimpanzee gene switches back on. And the gene does something: *LINC00662* “plays a role in neuronal maturation, axon outgrowth, and neural organoid development.”

**The star stuff frame** **Read the direction carefully, because it runs the opposite way from the way these stories are usually told.** Nothing was added to us. *The gene is in both species* — the CRISPR experiment proves it, by turning the chimpanzee’s copy back on — and what humans have is the **absence** of an infection the other lineage caught. The paper even names a candidate reason: “the human version of TRIM5α prevents infection of human cells with PTERV1.” *A difference between two kinds of primate, produced by a pandemic that one of them had and the other did not, and preserved as a thing that is switched off rather than a thing that was invented.* **That is the [endosymbiosis](https://starstuff.earth/you-were-never-one-thing-zine.html) argument arriving at a harder place:** not only is some of you descended from something that infected your ancestors, some of what distinguishes you is a passenger your cousins picked up and you missed.

### The paper never says “free will,” and we counted

Fong, Garrett, Smith, Hester, Bode and Feuerriegel recorded EEG from 49 adults making two kinds of decision — voluntary, a free choice between two options, and forced, where only one was available — with every response made by the same right-handed keypress, so that deciding could be separated from choosing a hand. **The result is about the readiness potential, the slow negative wave that has been read since the 1960s as the brain committing before you know you have.** Here the centro-parietal positivity and Mu/Beta signals “showed hallmark accumulation-to-bound dynamics — faster responses had steeper ramping signals that converged to stereotyped pre-response amplitude levels,” the signature of evidence piling up toward a threshold. The readiness potential did not behave that way: it converged too, but “with less reliable evidence for response time (RT)-dependent slope scaling, **resembling a late, effector-specific motor gate rather than an evolving decision variable.**” And “waveform amplitudes across the time course of these signals also did not differ reliably between voluntary and forced decisions.”

**The star stuff frame** **A sixty-year-old story about what a brain wave means is being quietly replaced by a duller and better one, and the duller one is good news.** If the readiness potential is a motor gate rather than a decision, then the famous finding — *your brain decided before you did* — was reading the wrong signal. The decision variable is the accumulation, and it works the same way whether the choice is yours or handed to you, which is a claim about machinery rather than about agency. *And it retires an explanation rather than adding one*, which is the shape this scan looks for hardest.

**Not a ranking of two kinds of ape.** A retrovirus infected the chimpanzee lineage and not ours, silencing a gene whose human copy stays on. *The difference is that something happened to them and did not happen to us* — which is a fact about an ancient pandemic, not a measure of either animal. The paper's own first paragraph reaches for “largest and most complex” and “unique cognitive capabilities”, and we are quoting the mechanism while declining the register it arrived in.

**Not a metaphor, in either direction.** A symbiont gave up glycolysis and spent 27.6% of its genome on the surface where it meets its host; a sea pig walks the abyssal mud wearing it. The sentence this site is built to want is right there and it is about a bacterium in a body wall four kilometres down. *Nothing in that experiment is about anybody’s worth*, and the thing that makes it worth having — that no one involved was arguing about human value — is exactly what carrying it across would destroy. The same goes for a nodule full of nematodes, and for a whale with the most crooked face in its class.

**Not a bot check we will argue with, in a good week or a bad one.** PubMed Central served six of seven articles and refused the seventh ten times running, and that seventh is the only paper this week whose full text might have changed what we wrote. *We did not disguise the caller.* The four-week sequence — all blocked, four of seven, none, six of seven — is on the page because it is the only way a reader can tell how much of what we read is method and how much is luck. **And one entry on that list was our own mistake**, not the archive’s: arXiv has been reachable here all along at arxiv.org while we recorded it as refused because export.arxiv.org is. *A route that fails is not a source that is closed*, and this is the second host we have learned that on.

**Not anti-science.** Four items here retire something standing — a deficit hypothesis about Autistic brains, a sixty-year reading of the readiness potential, a conservation status computed by addition, and a dietary reconstruction missing a carbon source — and *every one was corrected from the inside*. The strongest case is the lead: a team hypothesised that we would be harder to predict, tested it across six videos, two models and five spatial scales, found nothing, and wrote **“we consider the present work a robust rejection of our initial hypothesis”** in their own discussion. *That sentence is what a working field looks like.* The penguin authors argue against the good news their own paper produces; the Great Unconformity authors keep the glaciers in the mechanism they are replacing. **If a draft off this digest starts reading as a case against the field, it has gone wrong.**

**Not *because of*.** No two Autistic brains encode a film the same way, a whale’s face is lopsided because of what it listens for, and a gene is on in us because a virus missed our ancestors. None of it is an argument for how anybody should be treated. That is a rhyme, not a proof. The universe declining to pathologize its own variation is why the phrase carries — *down to your star stuff* measures how far the loving reaches, never why it is owed.
