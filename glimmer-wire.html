Lag 1 d, a floor

The oldest way we can show an animal made a living, and it was a partnership

Conotubus was a small tube-dwelling animal in the Ediacaran seas of what is now China, about 550 million years ago. Sulfur and molybdenum isotope signatures in its fossils are reported to indicate symbiosis with sulfide-oxidising bacteria — the bacteria feeding the animal, and drawing down a hydrogen sulfide load that would otherwise have been lethal. It is reported as the earliest geochemically supported case of chemosymbiosis in the fossil record: a partnership that let an animal live somewhere oxygen was scarce and poison was not.

The star stuff frame A hydrothermal-vent tubeworm doing this reads as a specialisation — an animal that adapted its way into an impossible place. Push the same arrangement back to the first chapter of animal life and it stops looking like an adaptation and starts looking like one of the ways animals began. The partnership is not a late accommodation bolted onto an independent creature; on this reading it is load-bearing from the start. Nothing about needing another organism to survive puts an animal in deficit. It is how the oldest one we can read managed at all.
What is inference and what is ours Isotope ratios in a 550-million-year-old tube are a proxy, not an observation of a partnership; the paper's own hedging is exactly what we could not read. Every figure in the paragraph above reached us through a search summary. And the frame is ours — the authors are reporting a trophic strategy, not making a claim about deficit.
Where it goes Checked against the shelf rather than assumed: chemosymbiosis is not unspent here. A Field Guide to Life in Impossible Places already carries Riftia, the vent tubeworm, living on “oxygen and sulfide at once.” What is unspent is the age, and that the evidence is isotopic rather than anatomical. So: a companion entry to that card, or the evidence spread of a piece — not a new argument. No. 27, Every Nervous System, is the only other page here that says Ediacaran.

Wang, Z., et al. “Chemosymbiotic trophic strategy in an Ediacaran tubular animal.” PNAS 123(35), e2526201123, 2026. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2526201123. Lag 11 d

Where the solar system happened to be standing may be part of Earth's climate record

A NASA-funded group modelled the heliosphere — the bubble of solar wind the whole solar system sits inside — backwards along its path through the galaxy, and reports the Sun crossing dense, extremely cold interstellar clouds roughly 2–3, 6–7 and 13–14 million years ago. In the strongest version of the claim, one of those encounters compressed the bubble to smaller than Earth's orbit, leaving the planet briefly outside it. A companion strand uses retired Kepler observations of superflares on young Sun-like stars to argue that proton bombardment made nitrous oxide in the early atmosphere, which could have kept the young Earth warm while the Sun itself was still dim.

The star stuff frame Umwelt at galactic scale. The atoms in a body were forged in dying stars; on this account the conditions were partly set by whichever piece of the galaxy the solar system was passing through at the time. An organism's environment does not stop at its skin — the whole site keeps arriving at that — and it turns out a planet's may not stop at its orbit either. Nothing here was ever self-contained, at any scale we have looked.
Why contested, not plausible This is a review article and a modelled trajectory, not a measurement. “May help explain the ice ages” is a causal claim the method cannot close, and the aggregator headline — Earth lost the Sun's protective shield — is far more definite than a modelled encounter. The three date windows, and the figure putting nitrous oxide at 300 times the warming potency of carbon dioxide, are both summary-sourced; the second is a standard textbook number, which is exactly why it should be checked rather than waved through.
Where it goes Unspent: no page here says heliosphere, checked by grep. (Interstellar does appear on five pages, The Cloud Phase and Bodies of Water among them; we have not re-read them, so treat the overlap as unchecked rather than absent.) It is a lead, not a piece. A zine built on this would have to carry the modelling caveat in the argument itself rather than in the colophon, and that is a harder piece to write than it looks.

Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics, paper dated 21 August 2026, from NASA's SHIELD centre — both per search summaries. Read: search-result summaries only. Not read: the review, NASA's own release, or any of the coverage. Not obtained: title, authors, volume, DOI. Related earlier work on the same encounters exists as a preprint we also could not open. Lag 34 d

Vesper bats are reported to build antibodies from two gene sets, where every other mammal has one

An antibody's heavy chain is assembled from one immunoglobulin heavy-chain locus — in humans, and in every other mammal anyone had looked at. Vesper bats, reported as the largest bat family at more than 500 species, are reported to carry two distinct copies of that locus: a second, independent route to generating antibody diversity, and an arrangement not previously found in any mammal. The group offers it as part of an explanation for why bats carry so many viruses without becoming seriously ill.

The star stuff frame This is No. 33 run backwards. The Ick Is a Border is about anglerfish that lost functional antibody-maturation genes so that two bodies could fuse — adaptive immunity given away to permit contact. Here a whole family of mammals appears to have doubled the same stretch of genome. One lineage surrendered the machinery; another kept two copies of it. Same locus, opposite directions, and neither is the standard. The variation is at the level of the architecture, not the tuning — which is the difference-first claim arriving in a place nobody was looking for it.
The verb is doing work Tolerate is not resist, and the gap matters: a bat is not better at winning a fight with a virus, it is differently arranged. This is also precisely the shape of finding that gets rewritten into a superpower on the way out the door, at which point it stops being a claim about architecture and becomes a claim about merit. Every figure above — the species count, the “largest family” claim, the two-locus arrangement — is summary-sourced.
Where it goes A card or a field-guide entry in the same family as No. 33 rather than a zine of its own, because the pairing is the finding and one half of it is already published here. Nothing on this site says immunoglobulin or vesper; No. 33 owns the loss direction and would need a link back.

Lag 47 d

Organ ageing traced to one cell type losing the knack of clearing up after another

Tissue-resident macrophages are the immune cells that live permanently inside an organ and clean up after it. A Stanford group reports that with age they lose the ability to clear away worn-out neutrophils, and that the loss is regulated through EP2, a receptor for prostaglandin E2. Blocking that one receptor in 22-month-old mice for two months is reported to have restored youthful clearance and preserved function across several organs. The paper's own title claims the impairment limits organ ageing; the framing in the abstract, as summarised, is a reversible driver of decline.

The star stuff frame The failure is not in either cell. It is in the relationship between them — one cell type's job is to take care of what another leaves behind, and what breaks is the taking care. That is a shape this site keeps arriving at from the other direction: a deficit that reads as located in a body is often located in a relation, and the repair is to the relation rather than a correction to the part. Stated in plain words on purpose, and not as a model — the argument for keeping it that way is already a page here.
Mice, and a verb the coverage changed These are mice, and the distance from a mouse organ to a human life is the whole argument. The coverage says reverses ageing; the paper, on its own title, says limits. Ages, durations, the receptor and the organ list all reached us through summaries, and one summary's author ordering looked garbled enough that we are not reproducing it. This is also the item most exposed to the cure frame, which the Not block refuses outright.
Where it goes Not a Glimmers entry — a mouse result is not an occasion. At most the evidence spread of a piece about deficits that turn out to live in relations, and that piece would need the human end of the claim to be honest about how far away it is. Nothing here says macrophage, neutrophil or efferocytosis.

Tan, Y. J., Conley, T. E., Yao, F., et al. “Restored clearance of senescent neutrophils by tissue-resident macrophages limits organ aging.” Science, 16 July 2026. DOI 10.1126/science.aea3075 · PMID 42462036. Stanford Medicine. Read: search-result summaries only. Not read: the paper, the abstract, the Stanford release, or the PubMed record. Lag ~96 d, provisional

A Hall voltage where the textbook geometry says there should not be one

Run a current through a conductor, put it in a magnetic field perpendicular to the sample, and a voltage appears across the sides. That is the Hall effect, and the perpendicular field is how it is taught and how it is built into sensors. A Carnegie Mellon group reports an anomalous Hall response with the field lying in the plane of a low-dimensional sample. The coverage frames it as a century-old assumption overturned, and as a route to a single ultrathin device that could sense field direction in more than one axis.

The star stuff frame Take the framing carefully and the interesting shape is not novelty at all: a response may have been there the whole time, hidden by the one geometry everybody measured in. That is the recurring How We Got Here move — a rule that turns out to describe the instrument rather than the world, and a variation that was invisible because of where the apparatus was pointed. It is a good shape and this is not yet a good enough source for it.
Why contested — and the doubt is about the headline, not the physics “Century-old assumption overturned” reads like release language rather than a claim from the paper. Searching around it surfaced prior work on Hall responses in parallel magnetic fields, which suggests the assumption was already under revision before this result — and if that holds, the honest story is a narrow, real finding inside an active field, not an overturning. We could not open the paper, the release, or any of the earlier work to settle it. The publication date is also unconfirmed: a summary put it at 28 May 2026 “from metadata,” and the lag above is computed from that, so treat the 96 days as provisional.
Where it goes Nowhere yet, and it is filed so that it is on the record as unresolved rather than quietly dropped. Possibly one joint in a chain about measurement geometry — but a chain link needs the literature history read, which is the exact thing this week refused.

Kao, I.-H., Bandapelli, R. K., Cui, Z., et al. “In-plane anomalous Hall effect in a low-dimensional system.” Nature Materials, 2026. DOI 10.1038/s41563-026-02611-9. Carnegie Mellon; institutional release dated 19 August 2026. Read: search-result summaries only. Not read: the paper, the release, the coverage, or the prior parallel-field literature the caveat turns on. Date approximate and flagged above.

Lag 146 d

The merger at the root of complex cells, watched instead of reconstructed

Asgard archaea are the closest known relatives of the archaeal lineage that gave rise to complex cells, and the standard account of how complex cells began has an archaeon and a bacterium entering a partnership that eventually became the mitochondrion. A group working in the microbial mats of Shark Bay, Western Australia, reports isolating a new Asgard archaeon and then imaging it by electron cryotomography in direct physical contact with bacteria, joined by nanotubes and apparently exchanging material. It is offered as a living model for the hypothesised partnership, not as the event itself.

The star stuff frame No. 7 and No. 84 already argue that you are a merger, and that the merger was a long negotiation rather than one swallow. What is new is the verb. The origin story's usual verb is engulfed; this is two intact cells with tubes between them, trading. Partnership without absorption is a different biological picture and a different political one, and it is the version that does not require one party to disappear.
One bay, one imaging result A model for a hypothesis is not evidence of an event that happened once, something under two billion years back — No. 84 carries the dating and its hedges, and this entry borrows neither. The coverage's own hedge — the cells appear to exchange nutrients — is kept here rather than tidied away. The species name reported for the new archaeon is summary-sourced and its spelling unconfirmed, so it is not printed. And the lag is the largest in this edition: 146 days, an April finding reaching a September feed.
Where it goes The origin argument is spent — No. 7 holds it and No. 84 holds the “long negotiation” reading, citing the Asgard literature by name. Unspent: partnership without engulfment as the picture, and the fact that it was imaged rather than inferred. A coincidence worth noting and not a pairing: Shark Bay is already on this site, in No. 36, for dolphin sponging in the water above the same mats.

Lag 0 d

Saturn grew a ten-sided pole, and the famous six-sided one stopped being a law

Saturn's northern hexagon — a jet stream bent into six straight sides, wider than Earth — has been in every image anyone has taken for more than forty years. That durability is exactly why it read as a rule about how gas giants behave. Hubble has now tracked a decagon, ten sides, encircling the south pole: the first large regular-sided jet pattern seen in Saturn's southern hemisphere. It has been visibly developing across 2023, 2024 and 2025, and the authors offer two candidate causes — an instability of the jet itself, or a 4,000-km red anticyclone at 55°S — without being able to choose between them.

The star stuff frame One planet, one atmosphere, one set of physics — and two poles that resolved it differently, six sides at the top and ten at the bottom. Neither is the standard; the hexagon only looked like the standard because it was the one we had measured. Forty years of stability was mistaken for a rule, and the correction came from looking at the other end of the same object. The universe doesn't pathologize its own variation — and here it declines even to standardise a planet against itself.
Where it goes Glimmers, gold accent. It also pairs hard with No. 50, The Sky Was Not Regular Enough — that piece is about the sky failing to be steady enough to keep time by; this is the sky failing to be regular enough to generalise from. A companion note, not a rewrite.

Sánchez-Lavega, A., et al. “A decagon wave around Saturn's south pole.” Science Advances 12, eaee4251, 2 September 2026. DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aee4251, CC BY 4.0. Upgraded to verified 2026-09-02, when the paper itself was obtained and read in full. The figures the NASA release did not carry are all in it: latitudes 58°S–63°S, mean zonal wavelength 16,782 ± 1,100 km, drift +2.5 ± 0.2 m/s against a 116 m/s jet, vertex oscillation 32.5 d at 4.6°–8.4°. Two corrections to what was filed here first: “nobody knows why” overstated it — the authors name two candidate mechanisms and say they cannot discriminate; and the formation window is bounded by the south pole being unobservable from mid-2012, not 2017 as some coverage has it. Now a piece: Until There Were Ten.

Lag 5 d

The clock on “you are made of star stuff” may start 13.7 billion years ago

The first stars were enormous, and some ended as pair-instability supernovae — explosions that can throw out more than a hundred solar masses of heavy elements at once. Simulations from Portsmouth follow that debris as it settles around a later, smaller star, roughly 70% of the Sun's mass, and find a water-rich disc capable of building planetesimals: the precursors of rocky worlds. Carbon, oxygen, iron. Water at a few times less than the Solar System had. All of it about 100 million years after the Big Bang, before the first galaxies had finished assembling.

The star stuff frame This is the founding fact of the whole collection with its date moved. The atoms in a body were forged in dying stars and scattered — and the scattering, and the assembling of them into rock and water, may have begun almost immediately. Sagan's line stops being a story about the late universe and becomes a description of how it worked from the start. If it holds, the ingredients were never scarce and never late.
Why contested, not plausible This is a simulation chain, and the release describes one example disc meeting the criteria. Coverage elsewhere is already framing it as a debate. The gap between “planetesimals could form” and the habitability language in the headlines is wide, and it is the kind of gap this site exists to hold open rather than close.
Where it goes Glimmers if the paper supports the narrow claim, with the wide claim named and refused on the page. It is also the strongest zine candidate here for Star Stuff proper — one settled-enough fact, followed honestly, and the belonging claim already inside it.

Jessop, C., & Whalen, D. J. “Planet Formation at Cosmic Dawn: Planetesimals in H2O-Rich Disks Around Low-Mass Stars.” The Astrophysical Journal Letters, 2026. Read: University of Portsmouth. Not read: the paper. Not obtained: DOI, full author list, redshift or metallicity thresholds. The title came from a search summary rather than the release — confirm it before quoting.

Lag ~7 d

Somebody asked Autistic adults what we are good at, in our own words

Autism research overwhelmingly measures deficits; instruments ask what is hard and rarely what is there. This study put open-ended questions to 127 legally independent Autistic adults and coded what they said about their own strengths and talents. The answers spread across problem-solving, creativity, character, academics and getting along with others — and close to a third named good people skills, which is the specific thing the literature is most confident we lack. The authors argue strengths should be measured during assessment, not just difficulties.

The star stuff frame A spiky profile has two sides, and the field has instruments for one of them. This is not a claim that we are secretly fine; it is a claim about who holds the pen. A third of a sample reporting social strength, inside a paradigm built on social deficit, is variation showing up the moment the question changed — and difference-first is the frame this whole site is built on.
Handle carefully Self-report from 127 people recruited through SPARK — a genetics research registry some of our community declines to join, which makes the sample selected in a way any use of it has to state. And the finding writes itself straight into the superpower framing if left alone. That trap is already named across the collection; a piece here would have to name it again.

Lag 36 d

The largest fibromyalgia study ever run found something — and much less than the headlines said

A multi-ancestry meta-analysis across 2,563,755 people — 54,629 cases, 87.7% of them female — reports 26 risk loci, heritability enriched exclusively in brain tissue and neural cell types, and genetic correlations above 0.7 with eighteen conditions including low back pain, PTSD and IBS. The strongest cell-type signal is the hippocampal dentate gyrus, which the authors connect to the symptom patients call brain fog. For a condition routinely met with there is nothing wrong with you, that is not a small thing.

And a second study of comparable size, published the same day in Nature Communications by a different group, reaches materially different conclusions on two of the headline questions. It is not a companion paper. It is a second opinion.

The star stuff frame The good part is real: pain that has been treated as fabrication has a genetic architecture, and it sits in the nervous system. But the finding worth the most here is the one nothing we read reported. The Nature Medicine group found the genetic architecture to be the same in men and women — inter-sex correlation 1.03, statistically indistinguishable from perfect — while 87.7% of the diagnosed are women. Their own conclusion: the prevalence gap is “unlikely to be driven by sex-specific genetic risk variants, and may instead reflect nongenetic biological factors, environmental exposures or diagnostic bias.” That is the authors, in Nature Medicine, putting who gets believed on the list of explanations. The other paper puts that correlation at 0.73, so even this is contested — but it is the question worth asking.

And the belonging claim was never waiting on any of it. If a finding is what makes disbelieved people credible, the next null result un-makes them. Down to is not because of.
The critique, now checked line by line — and its central charge is misattributed Five of the critic’s numbers hold, and every one of them is the paper’s own, stated plainly: the HTT variant carries OR 1.090 (“about 9% increased odds”), the polygenic score reaches AUC 0.59, and prevalence really does range sixfold across cohorts, 1.2% to 7.5% — disclosed in the second sentence of the Results. One number is wrong: the critique says heritability explains “less than 10%”; the paper reports 10.4% (95% CI 9.8–11.0). One is unresolved: we could not confirm the one-billing-code claim without the Methods supplement. And the central charge misses. The critique frames “a central nervous system disorder” as a headline exceeding the evidence. It is the paper’s own abstract — “This study provides robust genetic evidence defining fibromyalgia as a central nervous system disorder” — said twice, alongside two named therapeutic targets. That may still be over-reach, but it is a scientific disagreement rather than a science-communication failure, and the two need different treatment.
Two papers, one condition, one day, two answers Read side by side, the Nature Medicine study and Bright et al. in Nature Communications (85,139 cases; Yale and the VA Million Veteran Program) disagree where it matters most. On sex: an inter-sex genetic correlation of 1.03 and “no sex difference in the genetic architecture”, against 0.73 ± 0.064 — significantly short of identical. On autoimmunity: Nature Medicine finds no MHC signal and no immune-cell enrichment and concludes fibromyalgia is “not primarily an autoimmune disorder”; the Nature Communications structural model places it “mostly as a pain and autoimmune-related trait.” On heritability: 10.4% against 6.9%, measured on different scales. The coverage this item was filed from reported one of these papers and not the other.

It is no longer a Too Good to Check story about press over-reach, because the strong framing is the authors’ own. Two candidates remain: two studies, one condition, opposite answers, published the same day — or the quieter and harder one, identical genetics and a diagnosis rate that is not. Plausible Published 2026-07 Journal Neuron Read Columbia coverage

Lag ~37 d

Taking four to eight years to finish is the mechanism, not the delay

Microglia are the brain's resident immune cells, and they do developmental work: pruning synapses, tuning how circuits respond. In a mouse they reach maturity in about three weeks. In a human they take four to eight years. Columbia's group traces that to human-specific duplications of the gene SRGAP2 — reportedly around ten times more abundant in microglia than in neurons — which slow the maturation of both cell types. The slowing is synchronised: glia and neurons kept on the same long clock, and the group's reading is that the long clock is what human cognitive complexity is built on.

The star stuff frame One gene, doing the same thing to two different cell types so they stay in step, and the result of the delay is capacity. That is neoteny read as an achievement rather than a deficit, and it lands directly on a word our children get measured with. Developmental delay describes a schedule — and this is a species whose signature trick is being years behind the mouse on purpose.
Where it goes A numbered Star Stuff zine rather than a Glimmers entry: one settled mechanism, followed until the belonging claim is already in it. Neoteny is not unspent here — check what The Play You Keep and Thirteen Changes already argue. The unspent part is the synchrony: that being slow together is the point.

Plausible Published 2026-05-21 Journal Science Advances Read HKU release

Lag 104 d

Diamond was ruled non-piezoelectric for a century. Its irregularities turned out to be the mechanism

Squeeze a quartz crystal and it makes a voltage; that is piezoelectricity, and it requires a crystal structure without a centre of symmetry. Diamond has one, so for over a century diamond was simply not a piezoelectric material. A Hong Kong group used edge exfoliation to make diamond membranes thin enough to bend, bent them, and measured stable, repeatable voltages. Their calculations put the cause in the grain boundaries — the messy joins between crystal domains in a polycrystalline film. Charge polarisation gathers there as the bending increases, leaving a potential difference between the two faces. Named applications include self-powered implants and deformation sensors.

The star stuff frame This is the Yasuda line arriving from the opposite direction. Bone is piezoelectric — pressure into charge, the fact this whole collection stands on. Here the hardest material there is turns out to do the same thing, and it does it because it is not uniform. A perfect single crystal of diamond stays silent. The signal exists only where the structure is irregular. Difference is not tolerated in this system; difference is the transducer.
Numbers not confirmed A five-micrometre membrane holding output across more than 7,000 bending cycles at 0.35% strain: those figures came from a search summary. The HKU release carries the mechanism and no numbers, and Science Advances would not open. No piezoelectric coefficient was obtained at all. Don't print any of these until the paper is read — and mind the unit, because µm is micrometres and a stylesheet that uppercases it turns the figure into millimetres and a thousandfold error.
Where it goes The frame is too good to spend on a recirculated item without the paper. This is the companion to Bone Song — a zine whose one fact is that asymmetry is what makes the charge.

Plausible Published 2026, vol 12 no 17 Journal Science Advances Read Georgia Tech release

Lag ~130 d

There is no lizard inside your head, and 182 species say what is there instead

The triune brain — a reptilian core for survival, a limbic layer for emotion, a rational neocortex stacked on top — was proposed in the 1950s and has been contradicted by comparative neuroanatomy since the 1970s. It is also still in circulation, and appeared in introductory psychology textbooks as a real model as late as 2017. This analysis across 182 species finds the limbic system and neocortex do not stack; they trade. The two are wired on different principles — spatially organised circuits against distributed networks — and expand and contract together as an animal's life demands. When limbic regions grow, cortex tends to give ground.

The star stuff frame This is the How We Got Here motive in one finding. The triune brain is good science's clothes on a bad idea: it has a neuroanatomist's name on it, it produced a diagram everybody can draw, and pointed at people it does exactly the flattening that collection was built to answer — it ranks parts of a nervous system by how recently they arrived and calls the older ones primitive. Every appeal to somebody's lizard brain descends from it. And the replacement is not a ladder at all: it is a trade-off, with no top.
Where it goes The best chain candidate in this edition, and unspent — nothing on this site says triune or lizard brain. MacLean's proposal, the 1970s contradictions, the textbook survival to 2017, the pop-neuro descendants, and this paper: one link per spread, joints marked documented / contested / leap. It is an argument for the method and against a model that borrowed its authority — if a draft starts reading as anti-science it has gone wrong.

Field guide entry · More Than Human

Kanzi, who could hand you a cup of nothing

A bonobo tracked imaginary juice and imaginary grapes through pretend tea parties: he pointed to where the invisible grape was in 68.9% of trials, and when offered a cup of real juice against a cup of pretend juice he took the real one 77.8% of the time — so he knew which was which. Kanzi died in March 2025, aged 44. The authors put the capacity possibly six to nine million years back, to a common ancestor of humans and the other great apes.

Two limits belong on the card as much as the result does. The study shows Kanzi could follow a pretence a human set up; it does not show he could start one — comprehension, not production. And Daniel Povinelli (University of Louisiana at Lafayette) offers a simpler reading: that Kanzi may have been tracking which cup the experimenter had touched most recently. One bonobo, one of the most intensively language-trained animals who has ever lived, is also a sample of one.
