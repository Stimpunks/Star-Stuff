Almost every atom heavier than helium was forged in stars and their deaths. The calcium in your bones, the iron in your blood — all of it came from stars that lived and died before the Earth existed. “Star stuff” takes that settled science and reads it as an argument about belonging: if we’re all made of the same stellar matter, then neurodivergent and disabled people belong here fully — not despite our differences, but as part of the universe knowing itself in every possible way. Nothing here was arrived at in a straight line: these are cosmic connections, built across disciplines and out of each other's work.

Six Principles and a Room
Stimpunks × More Realms

An ethic you can only point at a person is an ethic that will keep finding the fault in the person.

A counsellor's code is addressed to two people. The distress is usually coming from a third thing, and the third thing cannot be struck off.

An essay→
Details

Helen Edgar came out of an evening on ethics in her BACP training and asked whether there was a Star Stuff version. The answer is read off one real, public document. The BACP Ethical Framework 2018 sets six core principles, and each is a duty running between a practitioner and a client — counted rather than asserted: in 6,825 words, client appears 173 times, environment once, and systemic, structural and institutional not at all. That is not an oversight, because a code's sanction is registration and you cannot strike off a building — but it means the lighting, the timetable and the eleven-month wait have nowhere to be recorded except inside the person who came in. Then the profession moved: the 2026 revision puts “Working ethically is working within relationships” on its cover, drops the Latin, goes from six principles to twelve, and adds anti-oppression as a named principle. The same count shows where the step stopped — environment falls to zero, and both uses of relational sit inside that new principle, locating oppression in power between people. The room does arrive, as a reason you could not do the ethical thing. Guattari's three ecologies name the missing band; the floor stays the Creed's; the practice at the end is Helen's own ethodivergent hearth.

An Essay
Rainbow Serpents
Foundations

entropy increases in an isolated system — and the qualifier keeps falling off

A criticism of our physics, arriving from outside our discipline, which turns out to be correct. Three of our own pieces walked up to this line and stopped.

essay→
Details

The second law says entropy tends to a maximum in an isolated system — one exchanging neither matter nor energy. A closed system exchanges energy but not matter and its entropy can decrease; conflating the two is a documented teaching error, and No. 43 made it on this site. In an open system the law is not suspended but restated: internal entropy production stays non-negative while total entropy can fall, because the system exports it. That is not an exception. It is how anything alive works, paid for by low-entropy sunlight arriving and high-entropy infrared leaving. Tyson Yunkaporta (Apalech clan, western Cape York) argues that applying the second law to open interconnected systems is a curse in a precise sense he defines: not a lie but “a true law or pattern applied where it doesn’t belong,” powered by something in constant motion. The essay checks the physics half and finds it right — and begins by admitting that three Star Stuff pieces each reached an edge of this and stopped: No. 43 states the domain restriction and marks crossing it as its leap; No. 56 uses the law to show a floor is compulsory; No. 69 shows the rainbow is anchored to the observer. Yunkaporta’s carved club is the argument as an object: an ouroboros etched round the head, which when rolled across clay prints “an endless procession of snakes, head to tail” — the closed figure and the open one, from one carving. Then the discipline the essay exists for: three infinities held apart rather than collapsed. It declines to explain the Rainbow Serpent, which is not one being and not ours, quoting it and stopping. And it features Kaya Oldaker’s Autism Infinity Dragon — public domain by the artist’s own release, and not a Rainbow Serpent, not descended from one. Its place here is geometric and checkable by looking: the head does not take the tail. The mouth is open and the plume passes beneath it — a lemniscate, which crosses itself and continues, where an ouroboros closes by eating. The one claim the essay makes about us is routed through the physics and our own symbol: a deficit assessment is an isolated-system model of a person, and people are not isolated. Refuses lineage between the three figures, refuses “ancient wisdom,” and refuses to un-refuse No. 43’s leap.

Zine No. 24
Shared Air
Stimpunks Zines

on breath, co-regulation, and the air we hold in common

9 spreads→
Details

The air we share, read through star stuff and solarpunk — a companion to Bodies of Water. There is only one air: a single, well-mixed body with no walls in it, so thoroughly circulated that your next breath very likely holds atoms once breathed by strangers and by trees across the world. It's a reciprocal breath — we exhale what plants inhale, and our oxygen is the exhaust of ancient cyanobacteria (the Great Oxidation Event, ~2.4 billion years ago), forged first in stars. Every room we share is a shared-air space (a term coined by Helen Edgar & Ryan Boren): in it we trade breath and co-regulate, nervous systems settling into a shared rhythm. The neurodiversity turn: many of us are the canaries of that room. Miners carried canaries because the birds felt the bad air first — not because they were weak, but because they were sensitive, and their alarm was information. Our sensitivity to fragrance, stale air, and fluorescent hum is an early-warning system, not a flaw: don't blame the bird, change the air — build it breathable for the canaries and it's breathable for all. Which raises the harder question: habitable, and for whom? The burden of shared air falls unevenly, so breathable air is a commons, not a private accommodation one person quietly manages. The response is solarpunk: clean air as infrastructure as resistance — open windows, plants, masks, DIY Corsi–Rosenthal boxes — mutual aid you can build in an afternoon. Move quietly and plant things. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Playlist & essay

The Rainbow Connection — forty songs, and the two uneasily cohabiting modes of thought they carry.

A song told a six-year-old that rainbows have nothing to hide, and he took it as permission to love the real thing more than any story about what it was concealing.

40 songs→
Details

“Rainbow Connection” — Paul Williams and Kenneth Ascher, sung by Jim Henson as Kermit, opening The Muppet Movie in 1979 — grants that rainbows are visions, downgrades them to illusions, and then lands the line that did the work for a science-loving kid: rainbows have nothing to hide. No pot of gold, no other side, nothing withheld. As a closeted queer and atheist child, Ryan found that observational reality better than any story about what a rainbow might be concealing — and the essay is honest that the song gets one word wrong. A rainbow is not an illusion. An illusion is a mistake perception makes; a rainbow is occurring, calculable to a fraction of a degree, and Roger Bacon measured its maximum elevation at 42° around 1267 with an astrolabe and no theory of refraction. What is true is stranger and better: it has no location — a direction rather than a place, centred on the shadow of your own head. Carl Sagan later supplied the vocabulary in The Demon-Haunted World (1995): his parents taught him “the two uneasily cohabiting modes of thought that are central to the scientific method.” Uneasily is the load-bearing word — not reconciled, sharing a house. NeuroTribes’ chapter “Princes of the Air” supplies the lineage, with Asperger forced to walk back his claim that his patients’ interests were remote from real concerns because their imaginations kept anticipating science by decades; and Dorion Sagan — son of Carl Sagan and Lynn Margulis, whose endosymbiosis this collection already runs on — calls detail and breadth “a kind of alembic, an antidote to both.” Then the fact that closes it. In March 2023 the School District of Waukesha, Wisconsin pulled “Rainbowland” from a first-grade concert as potentially controversial — and the song chosen to replace it was “Rainbow Connection,” which was initially banned too, reinstated only after parents pushed back; the teacher who spoke publicly was later terminated. A song whose entire argument is that rainbows have nothing hidden in them was, for a moment, too controversial for six-year-olds. Seven sections: eleven versions of the song; the Garfunkel and Oates joke that reaches the same thesis; eleven of “Over the Rainbow,” which wants the opposite thing; four of “What a Wonderful World,” which wants this one; the colours named as a list and as physics; the rainbow turned inward as identity; and finally Merle Haggard’s sour “Rainbow Stew” against a man on a mountain undone by a double rainbow, the two modes back to back with no attempt to reconcile them. All forty video ids were resolved through oEmbed before anything was written about them — five carried no artist in their link text, and four of those we would have got wrong. No lyric is reproduced anywhere on the page, which is stated rather than worked around.
