Almost every atom heavier than helium was forged in stars and their deaths. The calcium in your bones, the iron in your blood — all of it came from stars that lived and died before the Earth existed. “Star stuff” takes that settled science and reads it as an argument about belonging: if we’re all made of the same stellar matter, then neurodivergent and disabled people belong here fully — not despite our differences, but as part of the universe knowing itself in every possible way. Nothing here was arrived at in a straight line: these are cosmic connections, built across disciplines and out of each other's work.

Zine No. 19
No Universal Now
Stimpunks Zines

Crip time, read through relativity — with a guest coda by Helen Edgar. Physics says there is no universal “now”: simultaneity is relative and clocks run at different rates by speed and gravity (your phone's GPS corrects for it). So the one true clock is a human invention — railway-and-factory standard time, a convention we mistook for a law and then began grading bodies and minds against. Crip time (Zola & Gill, Kafer, Samuels, Price) bends the clock back to fit us instead of bending us to it — the same move as Minor Worlds, now applied to time. Monotropic spiral time and felt time (Helen Edgar, Bergson, Deleuze) are their own real clock, measured in intensity, not minutes; Samuels' time travel, grief time, and broken time hold the hard edges too. The reading: no clock is master, and being “off the clock” is closer to the truth than fitting it. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set, and a closing coda by Helen Edgar on spiral time.

Zine No. 20
The Same Water
Stimpunks Zines

Fluctuating capacity, read through the physics of phase transitions. Water doesn't get gradually stiffer as it cools — it holds, then flips to ice all at once at a threshold. Same H₂O, different state, and the conditions (not the substance) decide which. So the exhausting question — why could you do it yesterday and not today? — has a clean answer: capacity is a state, not a trait, and you were near a boundary. The hardest part is the crossing itself (threshold anxiety is sensitivity to a real edge — broken thresholds, not broken people); you can be supercooled, held past your limit until one small thing tips everything at once; and recovery slows near the edge, because that's how systems behave. Nothing is fixed or broken — fluid adaptation and weather-bodies, re-adapting to a changed climate. Extends Minor Worlds: change the conditions, not the substance. Grounded in phase-transition physics, Cynthia Kim, Helen Edgar, and Stimpunks' own threshold anxiety. Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 21
Not a Line
Stimpunks Zines

The spectrum was never a severity scale — after Helen Edgar & Patrick Dwyer. “Spectrum” is the most misread word in our vocabulary: people hear a line from mild to severe. But a real spectrum isn't a ladder — split a star's light and every element signs its own pattern of lines, a fingerprint, which is the only way we know what stars are made of. And the sky's figure was never a line: it's a constellation. The zine follows the escalation from Helen Edgar's brand-new essay — the line of neuronormativity, to the spiky profile (better, but still a grid measured from a baseline), to a constellation of intensities: no rank, no norm, no line. Patrick Dwyer's autism constellation anchors it; the functioning-labels critique shows how the line gatekeeps support (“not disabled enough” vs “too disabled”). Held with care: refusing the line doesn't erase that some of us need far more support — need is not a rank; name it instead. Cashes in the rainbow motif on every page and hands off to the Constellation Field Guide. Eight spreads, spectrum-forward diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Bodies of Water
Stimpunks Zines

Zine No. 24
Shared Air
Stimpunks Zines

The air we share, read through star stuff and solarpunk — a companion to Bodies of Water. There is only one air: a single, well-mixed body with no walls in it, so thoroughly circulated that your next breath very likely holds atoms once breathed by strangers and by trees across the world. It's a reciprocal breath — we exhale what plants inhale, and our oxygen is the exhaust of ancient cyanobacteria (the Great Oxidation Event, ~2.4 billion years ago), forged first in stars. Every room we share is a shared-air space (a term coined by Helen Edgar & Ryan Boren): in it we trade breath and co-regulate, nervous systems settling into a shared rhythm. The neurodiversity turn: many of us are the canaries of that room. Miners carried canaries because the birds felt the bad air first — not because they were weak, but because they were sensitive, and their alarm was information. Our sensitivity to fragrance, stale air, and fluorescent hum is an early-warning system, not a flaw: don't blame the bird, change the air — build it breathable for the canaries and it's breathable for all. Which raises the harder question: habitable, and for whom? The burden of shared air falls unevenly, so breathable air is a commons, not a private accommodation one person quietly manages. The response is solarpunk: clean air as infrastructure as resistance — open windows, plants, masks, DIY Corsi–Rosenthal boxes — mutual aid you can build in an afternoon. Move quietly and plant things. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 25
Shared Signal
Stimpunks Zines

Connection carried across distance, read through star stuff — a companion to Shared Air. The electromagnetic spectrum is one continuous field: radio waves and starlight are the same phenomenon at different wavelengths, and the same physics that carries a voice call carries a star's light. Shared-signal space — a term coined by Helen Edgar — is the counterpart to shared-air space: a call, a message, a voice note, a synthesised voice, carrying the rhythm on a signal instead of the air of a room. A voice note is breath, timestamped and sent; co-regulation crosses a screen too — thinner, but real (Porges; Streuber et al.). For many of us, text and AAC aren't a fallback from speech but the clearer channel — thinking time, structure, fewer sensory demands, a record to return to (Howard & Sedgewick) — and AAC is the real communication, not a workaround. Aversion to the phone is information, not fragility. Reachable, and for whom? Communication access is a commons; we build our own watering holes — the Autistic Rhizome (Edgar), Discords, AAC — sideways and underground, where lurking counts. The oldest signal of all is a dead star's light still reaching your eye: signal lets connection cross a gap touch can't, so no channel is the master channel (Milton). Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 22
No One Is the Leader
Stimpunks Zines

Emergence and the murmuration — a flock has no captain, and neither does a self. Hundreds of thousands of starlings make vast rolling shapes with no leader and no plan: each bird tracks only its six or seven nearest neighbours (Ballerini & the STARFLAG project), and the whole emerges from that one local rule. Philip Anderson's “More Is Different” (1972) is the spine — the founding statement of anti-reductionism: at each level of scale new laws appear that the parts don't contain, so knowing the parts never rebuilds the whole. The early universe did the same, self-organising faint density ripples into the cosmic web with no architect. Two payoffs land it home: the whole person is realer than any diagnostic checklist of parts (you are not a list of deficits — you are the murmuration), and our best neurodivergent collectives — NeurodiVentures — are leaderless murmurations, not org charts (competency networks and catalysts rather than leaders; decisions where the knowledge lives). A “the whole exceeds the parts” zine. Held with care: leaderless is not structureless. Eight spreads, flock-and-web diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 26
A Mycelium and a Rhizome
Stimpunks Zines

How neurodivergent knowledge and community grow, read through star stuff — no trunk, no center, many doors. A tree has one trunk and a chain of command; you climb it or you're lost. A rhizome (Deleuze & Guattari) has no root to ask permission of, so you can enter anywhere — Spiky Profiles → Monotropism → Deep Attention, or in from burnout, or a word that finally fit. No prerequisite, no gatekeeper. And a mycelium is the life in that shape: fungal threads carry nutrients and signals between trees that never touch (the “wood wide web,” Simard), thickening the paths that help and letting the rest fade; a brainless slime mold rebuilds the Tokyo rail network (Tero et al.). Zoom all the way out and the shape returns — the cosmic web of dark-matter filaments, the universe's largest structure, looks like a mycelium, like a neuron: not a pyramid, a web. We're already building it. Gray-Hammond and Munday name it neuro-anarchy; the Autistic Rhizome is “a network with no single point of origin,” members as equals held by a do-no-harm ethic, resilient because nothing depends on a center (with Helen Edgar's watering holes). Where the map meets soil is Cavendish Space. Decentralization isn't disorder — it's the oldest structure there is. Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 28 · Guest
Wyrd & Weird
Stimpunks Zines · Helen Edgar

On the old word for fate, and the newer word we made of it — a guest zine by Helen Edgar (Autistic Realms), rehoused in the Star Stuff style. Long before “weird” meant strange, wyrd meant becoming: Old English wyrd comes from weorþan, “to become,” and is twined with the root of “worth.” Fate was never drawn as a straight line from birth to death but as cloth — the Norns' weaving at the well beneath Yggdrasill, the “web of man” — many threads crossing, none load-bearing alone. The Weird Sisters carried the word down through Holinshed to Shakespeare's Macbeth; over centuries “weird” drifted into an insult, and Autistic and disabled communities have run that drift backward, taking it up again as a name, not a verdict — which is etymologically honest, not just cheek. Held with care: wyrd is not a script, not doom, not a synonym for wrong, not something done to you alone. Under the root, weorþan meant “to become,” never “to be”: nobody is finished, and becoming was never the flaw — it was the design. You were never written; you are still being woven. Nine spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set, a “threads already running” map of its kin in the collection, and Helen's own sources & notes.

Zine No. 29 · Guest
Only in Relation
Stimpunks Zines · after Helen Edgar

Relational quantum physics, and what it means to be Autistic — a guest zine developed after Helen Edgar (Autistic Realms) from her essay “We Are Made of Relations.” In Carlo Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics a particle has no standalone properties; what it is becomes definite only in relation to another system (Rovelli, 1996). The count gives it away: two distinct coins make four outcomes, but two identical bosons make only three — what's fundamental isn't the standalone particle but the symmetry, the relation (Webster, 2026). Karen Barad calls the relation-before-parts intra-action; the human version, intradependence — made of each other, deeper than interdependence — Helen draws from Barad too. Read through Deleuze, “objects emerge out of more fundamental processes of differentiation”: difference first, then relation, and the tidy standalone object is the thing that got built. The payoff lands on our own work — the double empathy problem (Milton, 2012) lives in the relation, not one mind, and Helen widens it into DEEP; and in the ARLES ladder Relational sits near the base with Regulation inside it, because regulation is relational, so you change the field, not the person. Held with care: not quantum mysticism, not “everything is connected” as a vibe, not dissolution into sameness — the spiky profile survives the merge. A rhyme, not a proof. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 30 · Guest
Always Migrating
Stimpunks Zines · Helen Edgar

Migration, monotropism, and the galaxy you're already made of — a guest zine by Helen Edgar (Autistic Realms), written after Brigit Anna McNeill's The Living Page migration workshop and rehoused here in the Star Stuff style. Every migrating body is made of matter that already migrated once, so migration was never a metaphor borrowed from animals: it's one of the oldest facts about star stuff. A bird crosses an ocean on a compass sense we still can't fully explain — light striking cryptochrome in the eye makes a pair of electrons whose spins stay linked, and that linked pair is thought to be part of how it reads the Earth's faint field (Hore & Mouritsen; Mouritsen). Entanglement's lesson: once two things have been in relation, they stay in relation — separateness was always the less accurate story, which is Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics in a wingbeat. Zoom out and the pattern holds: the Sagittarius dwarf is still arriving in our halo, and the Milky Way–Andromeda collision everyone called settled is now closer to a coin-flip (Sawala et al., 2025) — a tug, not a destination. Then the turn inward: the bodily restlessness that builds before anything is decided is monotropism's gravity, attention gathered into a few bright points rather than spread as an even glow — and masking teaches us to quieten it until we lose our own north. Neuroqueering is the refusal; Pernille Fraser's “Can we trust the space you offer?” asks it from the other side. Widened by ethodiversity and the double empathy problem, and by Barad's intra-action — the liminal crossing isn't empty transit, it's where the shaping happens. Closes past the spiky profile: not a chart, a constellation of intensities. Eleven spreads, eight diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set, and a threads-already-running map of its kin.

Zine No. 27
Every Nervous System
Stimpunks Zines

More-than-human neurodiversity, read through star stuff. There is no standard nervous system — not across species, and not within one: the octopus's mind distributed down its arms, the jellyfish's centreless nerve net, the worm's fully mapped 302 neurons. Read neurodiversity literally and it never stopped at the human. Following Ombre Tarragnat's antispeciesist–posthumanist philosophy: there's “no valid reason to be anthropocentric when you talk about neurodiversity,” and ethodiversity — behaviour reclaimed as “a subjective response to a bodymind's situation” — is a fundamentally animal phenomenon. Ethonormativity punishes the ethodivergent (the horse who refuses the rider, the cat who resists petting — abandoned or euthanised). And the core move: neurodivergent people are pathologised by being animalised, so the liberation is to reclaim animality — not bestialisation (the speciesist, neuronormative “less-than-human” split, after Butler 2020) but (hum)animality, kin. Distance is not disconnection: shutting down can be how we keep relation. Every nervous system is the universe wired up to feel itself, no two ever the same. “I would rather be a divergent animal than a typical human.” Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 31
Five Times a Crab, Seven Times Not
Stimpunks Zines

Convergent evolution and “attractors,” read against the primary literature — written after Brett D. Wick's essay When Everything Becomes a Crab. The crab-like body plan really did evolve at least five times in decapod crustaceans, which is the part everyone quotes. It was also lost at least seven times — decarcinization, the commoner event — and Wolfe, Luque & Bracken-Grissom (BioEssays, 2021) report that the reasons for the recurrence remain a mystery, with carcinized lineages running from millimetres to metres across nearly every habitat on Earth. So convergence produced no sameness, and the basin is not a destiny. Our eye and the octopus's converged on the same camera and were built backwards from each other, which is why a system that grades the route instead of the outcome will fail an eye. Waddington's canalized landscape gives the picture; “attractor” is named as a metaphor borrowed from dynamical systems. Then the turn: nature's basin permits the millions who never roll in, while engineered systems write the attractor into the rules — engagement chosen as the objective and everything downhill of it becoming vertical video, one route to the answer and a diagnosis for the others. Cavendish monoculture against Cavendish Space, on the ARLES Environment and Systems rungs. Ideas ripen the same way: Ogburn & Thomas's 148 multiples, Merton's singletons-are-the-exception, Tao's “maths at internet speed,” and Brian Eno's scenius quoted from the 1996 primary rather than the altered version in circulation. Closes on our own history — neurodiversity was itself developed collectively. Gould and Conway Morris are left unresolved on purpose. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 32
The Skeleton That Skipped Bone
Stimpunks Zines

A companion and a counter-song to Bone Song, written for a young person in our community whose special interest is sharks. This collection opens on a skeleton that is piezoelectric — press it and it makes voltage. Sharks, rays and chimaeras are vertebrates with no true bone at all: a cartilage skeleton stiffened by thousands of mineral tiles called tesserae, which Dean & Summers (Zoology, 2006) describe as tuned species by species and even to how the animal feeds. Then the finding that changes the story. Sharks were taught for a century as the before picture — cartilage first, bone later, primitive, not there yet. But Brazeau and colleagues (Nature Ecology & Evolution, 2020) describe endochondral bone in Minjinia turgenensis, an Early Devonian fish near the common ancestry of jawed vertebrates. If the shared ancestor had bone, the shark's bonelessness is not a failure to arrive — it is a loss, derived rather than primitive, and the same shape of fact as the crab body plan being lost more often than gained. Which exposes the verdict buried in the word primitive, the same one hiding in low-functioning and delayed. Then the other half: because sharks were said not to get cancer, their cartilage was ground into pills. Ostrander et al. (Cancer Research, 2004) documented 42 chondrichthyan tumours across at least 21 species, at least 15 malignant; Loprinzi et al. (Cancer, 2005) ran the trial and found no benefit to survival or quality of life. A body read as a cure gets harvested — and extraction always starts as appreciation. The skeleton is for the animal. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 33
The Ick Is a Border
Stimpunks Zines

Disgust feels like a fact about the animal. It is a fact about the frame. Rozin & Fallon (Psychological Review, 1987) treat disgust as a food emotion at root, whose objects are largely learned — arriving across a child's first eight years and running on the magical law of contagion, where one touch renders a whole plate inedible forever. A reaction that far out of proportion to any physical fact is a rule about categories, and rules about categories are taught. Then three arrangements that read as gross and are, in frame, simply competent. Osedax, the bone-eating worm (Rouse, Goffredi & Vrijenhoek, Science, 2004): no mouth, no gut, a vascularised root system dissolving whale bone, dozens of dwarf males inside the female's tube. The deep-sea anglerfishes, whose fused males cost them their immunity — Swann, Holland, Petersen, Pietsch & Boehm (Science, 2020) found loss of functional aicda, and in permanent attachment loss of rag, abolishing the hallmark of adaptive immunity. To stay joined to another body they gave up the machinery that tells self from not-self. And the whale fall (Smith & Baco, 2003): a pulse of food worth roughly 2,000 years of background supply, a sulphophilic stage lasting decades, a mean of 185 macrofaunal species — richer than any other hard surface down there. Then the turn. The same reflex fires at feeding tubes, drooling, stimming, incontinence and AAC; Nussbaum calls it projective disgust, where a group of people becomes a buffer against the animal nature we would rather deny. Two frames to widen past — the neuronormative and the anthronormative. A house companion to Helen Edgar's Wyrd & Weird: weird was reclaimed, gross gets disarmed. Nobody is guilty of a flinch; everyone is answerable for the two seconds after.

Zine No. 34
Two Hundred Years Before You're Grown
Stimpunks Zines

The Greenland shark is the longest-lived vertebrate known, and it does not reach maturity until it is about 156 years old. Nielsen et al. (Science, 2016) radiocarbon-dated the eye lenses of 28 females — the lens core is laid down before birth and never replaced, so they read each animal's age against the atmospheric bomb pulse from 1950s nuclear testing — and found a life span of at least 272 years, the largest animal at 392 ± 120. It is also the slowest fish for its size: 0.34 m/s, one tail-beat every seven seconds (Watanabe et al., 2012), and it eats seals anyway, apparently by arriving quietly while they sleep — the strategy needs the trait. Nearly all of them carry a copepod clamped to the cornea, and it costs them almost nothing, because vision is not what the Arctic deep asks for. So late turns out to be a comparison, not a measurement; the ruler was always the variable. But this is not a zine about slow being beautiful: Nielsen's own paper raises the conservation alarm, because an animal needing a century and a half to reproduce cannot recover from pressure arriving in decades, and the species is IUCN Vulnerable. Slow is survivable or it isn't, and the environment decides — which is the social model of disability, stated in fish. Names the standard defence of the clock too: Alfie Kohn's BGUTI, "Better Get Used To It," which almost by definition ignores developmental differences — and cannot survive noticing them. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 35

Tyson Yunkaporta writes in Sand Talk that a Star Dreaming Oldman Juma Fejo of the Larrakia People wants to share with all peoples "goes everywhere that turtles go — and there are turtles all over the world, even in desert country, so it connects everybody." Zine No. 36
The Layer We Call Essential
Stimpunks Zines

Mortensen et al. (Frontiers in Neuroanatomy, 2014) counted the long-finned pilot whale's neocortex and found roughly 37.2 billion neurons — "almost twice as many neocortical neurons as humans," the first animal ever counted above us. Then, in the same paper, they conclude that absolute neuron number is "not correlated with the superior cognitive abilities of humans." The yardstick came back against us and the ranking survived, because it was never resting on the count. Underneath sits the architecture: the cetacean neocortex is agranular, with layer IV — the input layer our textbooks call canonical — absent or underdeveloped, and layer I apparently taking the job instead, giving "a very different connectivity scheme compared to more commonly studied species, like rats and macaque monkeys." Manger (2006) read that missing layer as evidence there is no neural basis for complex cognition in cetaceans; sixteen authors replied in Biological Reviews that these features are "a non-primate route to neuroanatomical (and cognitive) complexity." Difference read as deficit, conducted by neuroscientists, about whales. Held with care: thermogenesis is a serious hypothesis and a brain can do heat work and thought work at once — the claim here is only about which reading you get for free. On neurodiversity, the honest answer: there is no cetacean diagnostic literature and this zine does not invent one. What exists is variation inside single populations — sponging in 15 of 141 known Shark Bay mothers, down one matriline (Krützen et al., 2005), spongers more solitary and foraging longer (Mann et al., 2008) — read through ethodiversity (Ombre Tarragnat), which needs no clinic. Plus 8,719 sperm whale codas resolving into ~143 combinations where 21 had been described, because someone borrowed rubato from music. And eight days off Pico with an S-spined bottlenose dolphin among sperm whales, the rubs returned — a paper titled "Repeated Non-Agonistic Interactions," because "adopted" was the press's word. Douglas Adams got the joke in 1979, ranking us third behind the mice. Then he went to see the baiji. A rhyme, not a proof.

Field Guide No. 6
A Field Guide to Symbioses
Star Stuff Field Guides

Mutual aid, written into biology — twelve partnerships across the tree of life, read as mutual aid. Fungi and roots trading underground, bacteria making soil, a cleaner fish trusted by predators who could swallow it, algae building reefs that shelter a quarter of ocean life, the fig and its wasp that cannot live without each other. Interdependence is the rule, not the exception: Kropotkin showed mutual aid is a factor of evolution; Margulis, that life “took over the globe not by combat, but by networking.” This isn't “nature is nice” — parasites are real too, and the ghost pipe (Monotropa uniflora) is the honest one-way case, a myco-heterotroph that only takes, modelling receiving without reciprocating — and a bridge to monotropism (with Helen Edgar's tribute to Dinah Murray). Field Guide No. 8
A Field Guide to the Ways of Migrating
Star Stuff Field Guides

Fifteen ways of migrating — the companion catalogue to Always Migrating (Zine No. 30). Say “migration” and most of us picture one shape: a long line, north in spring, back in autumn. Almost nothing does that. The Arctic tern flies ~71,000 km a year and refuses the straight line, riding wind in vast S-shaped detours because the long way is the cheap way. A juvenile godwit crossed 13,560 km in 11 days without landing — held with care, since it shrinks its own organs to do it and a migrant who rests is not a lesser migrant. No monarch finishes the round trip: several short-lived generations work north and one “super” generation, living 8–9 months instead of weeks, flies the whole way back. Salmon find one stream by the magnetic field they imprinted on, then die and carry the ocean up into the forest. The largest migration on Earth is a few hundred metres straight up, every night, in the dark — distance was never the measure. Spiderlings rig silk and let atmospheric electricity carry them; trees migrate at metres a year, seed by seed; the Sahara crosses an ocean as dust and fertilises the Amazon; a star gets handed to a wider orbit and the Sun is probably not from here. The centre of the guide is partial migration — populations holding both migrants and residents, described in the literature as the commonest form, the one all others derive from. Variation within a population isn't the anomaly; it's the mechanism. Closes on us: the crossing made in place, when the word arrives and every year behind you rearranges. Held with care throughout — this reads migration as a way of being, never as a frame for forced human displacement. Fifteen entries, route-shape diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Field Guide No. 9
A Field Guide to Being a Shark
Star Stuff Field Guides

Twelve ways to be a shark — the companion catalogue to the shark set (Zines 32, 33 and 34), written for a young person in our community whose special interest is sharks. These are not distant relatives: one clade, one body plan, four hundred million years. And inside it, almost nothing holds still. The whale shark reaches ~18.8 m and lives on plankton; the dwarf lanternshark tops out near 20 cm and erases its own silhouette by lighting its belly to match the light coming down. The goblin shark cannot give chase in the dark, so it doesn't — the jaws fire forward at up to 3.1 m/s, the fastest protrusion known in any shark. The thresher hunts with the far end of itself, whipping its tail overhead to stun several fish at once. The epaulette shark walks between tide pools on its fins and rides out near-anoxia by suppressing its own metabolism. The cookiecutter, at fifty centimetres, takes a neat plug out of animals hundreds of times its size and everything swims off alive. The bonnethead eats seagrass and — tested in 2018 — digests it, which made "sharks are carnivores" a category nobody had checked rather than a finding. Two entries are here because of a phrase rather than an animal: the great hammerhead's head does not sharpen its electrosense (tested directly, it widens the search area instead), and the frilled shark is not a living fossil, because nothing alive is. One points up, one points down, and both skip what the animal is doing. Twelve entries, schematic diagrams, a "Not:" refusals set. There is no standard shark.

Field Guide No. 10
A Field Guide to Being a Turtle
Star Stuff Field Guides

Twelve ways to be a turtle, and five turtles people made — the companion catalogue to Everywhere That Turtles Go (No. 35). Almost every entry is a thing a turtle is supposed to have and doesn't. The leatherback has no bony carapace at all, just hundreds of interlocking ossicles in oily skin — and it dives to 1,280 m and holds its body up to 18 °C above the sea around it, which gives it the largest range of any reptile alive. The mata mata has weak jaw muscles and almost no tongue and is a superb predator, because it never bites: the mouth is a pump, and every "weakness" on the list is a component of it. The Fitzroy River turtle takes about 70% of its oxygen in through two enlarged sacs at the far end of itself. The Chinese softshell excretes urea through the lining of its mouth rather than its kidneys — the head-dipping that looks like drinking is the opposite of drinking. Painted turtle hatchlings spend their first winter in a nest below −10 °C by supercooling, and what decides whether they live is not how tough they are but what touches them. And the Arrau was filed as a silent animal until somebody brought a hydrophone: 2,122 recorded sounds in eleven types, hatchlings calling from inside the egg, and females coming and answering — the first evidence of post-hatching parental care in any turtle. The silence was ours. The last entry is the bill: Rafetus swinhoei, two or three animals left, the last known female dead in April 2023. The five turtles people made — Turtle Island, ao, Kurma (a pivot for churning, not a world-bearer), Great A'Tuin, and a Larrakia Star Dreaming we cite rather than retell — get a separate section and a deliberately different card, because they are not five drafts of one story. Field Guide No. 11
A Field Guide to Being a Tortoise
Star Stuff Field Guides

Twelve land tortoises — Testudinidae — and an arc that runs from the Moon to a graveyard to a beginning. It opens where this collection has to open: on 15 September 1968, Zond 5 carried two Soviet steppe tortoises around the far side of the Moon and brought them back alive, two months before Apollo 8 carried any human. They lost about a tenth of their body weight and were otherwise fine. Then the ordinary astonishments of a slow land animal: the gopher tortoise digs burrows over fourteen metres long that more than 350 other species live in, which is the argument for building rooms that fit whoever needs them most, stated in reptile. The pancake tortoise has a shell whose bone is full of holes and is the fastest and best-climbing tortoise there is — and the beloved story that it inflates itself inside a rock crevice is now believed to be wrong, so the guide says so instead of repeating it. In July 2020 an Aldabra giant tortoise was filmed hunting and swallowing a tern chick, the first deliberate hunt ever recorded in a wild tortoise, which turned "tortoises are herbivores" from a law back into a summary of what we had happened to watch. Jonathan hatched around 1832 and is still on the lawn. The speckled padloper is six centimetres and complete. And then four entries marked as unfinished, because their endings are decisions rather than facts: the star tortoise, most trafficked tortoise on Earth and trafficked because it is beautiful; the ploughshare, whose shell conservationists engrave to spoil it for the market, and it may not be working; Lonesome George, who died in 2012 and took Pinta with him; and Fernanda, found alive in 2019 after 113 years of "extinct." It ends on Île aux Aigrettes, where an Aldabra tortoise is doing an extinct Mauritian tortoise's job — 529 hatchlings in eleven years. Not restoring what was lost. Finding another way for the function to happen.

The loud companion to the L★S Playlist, and the one that argues first. In July 1977 Bob Marley recorded Punky Reggae Party with Lee Perry after hearing The Clash cover a Jamaican record — a guest list for an alliance that did not exist until it was read out, and the following April roughly 100,000 people marched six miles through National Front territory to hear The Clash, Steel Pulse and X-Ray Spex on one Rock Against Racism bill. Stimming and Belonging with AURORA
Stimpunks × More Realms

The companion rack to Warriors and Weirdos (No. 13), weighted toward live performances because the argument is the body. It opens on AURORA's own account of seeing herself on video for the first time — she was shocked, said she couldn't do anything about it, and kept it: I have to use everything I got. Then the physics of the light she shares a name with, which turns out to be about exactly this. The solar wind delivers energy and nothing else; the colour is the atom's own — that famous green is oxygen's 557.7 nm line. And that line is a forbidden transition: it breaks the electric-dipole selection rules, so instead of the nanoseconds an allowed transition takes, the atom waits about 0.7 seconds before letting go, and the red line waits 110. Which is why altitude decides the colour — lower down the air is crowded and a collision takes the energy first. The most famous light in the sky is a slow release the rules call forbidden, and it only completes where nothing crowds it. Twenty-eight performances across six movements, plus stim-listening (Jim Irion's coinage) and the reason Ryan's own playlist is nearly all live footage. The page is careful about whose word is whose: she calls it emotion; we are the ones calling it stimming, and it says so rather than putting the term in her mouth. The sharpest refusal is saved for last — her movement is celebrated because it is on a stage and lovely, while the same body doing the same thing in a classroom gets a behaviour plan.
