Almost every atom heavier than helium was forged in stars and their deaths. The calcium in your bones, the iron in your blood — all of it came from stars that lived and died before the Earth existed. “Star stuff” takes that settled science and reads it as an argument about belonging: if we’re all made of the same stellar matter, then neurodivergent and disabled people belong here fully — not despite our differences, but as part of the universe knowing itself in every possible way. Nothing here was arrived at in a straight line: these are cosmic connections, built across disciplines and out of each other's work.

An Essay
A Promise, Not a Finding
Stimpunks × More Realms

For forty-nine issues this project has signed off Love you down to your star stuff, and the grammar of that phrase has been doing the hardest work on the site. The subject is us, not the cosmos — and “down to” measures how far the love reaches, not why it is given. The star stuff is the extent of the claim, not its warrant. Which matters, because grinding the universe down and sieving it turns up no atom of justice in the powder: a belonging claim resting on “variation persists in nature” would be revoked by any paper finding that it doesn't, and the same move run backwards is how eugenics argued. Ours never rested there. Includes the sentence we did write once, in the colophon of Zine No. 1, and repaired in the same breath. Two rules follow. Every piece here is a lie-to-children in Jack Cohen and Ian Stewart's sense — honest only where it marks the rung it stops at, which is what “a rhyme, not a proof” has been doing unnamed — and the ladder has a moral limit as well as an epistemic one. And First Sight and Second Thoughts, from Pratchett's Wintersmith, names what the six gates and the public changelog implement, with the catch that only the second half can ever be automated. Zine No. 19
No Universal Now
Stimpunks Zines

Crip time, read through relativity — with a guest coda by Helen Edgar. Physics says there is no universal “now”: simultaneity is relative and clocks run at different rates by speed and gravity (your phone's GPS corrects for it). So the one true clock is a human invention — railway-and-factory standard time, a convention we mistook for a law and then began grading bodies and minds against. Crip time (Zola & Gill, Kafer, Samuels, Price) bends the clock back to fit us instead of bending us to it — the same move as Minor Worlds, now applied to time. Monotropic spiral time and felt time (Helen Edgar, Bergson, Deleuze) are their own real clock, measured in intensity, not minutes; Samuels' time travel, grief time, and broken time hold the hard edges too. The reading: no clock is master, and being “off the clock” is closer to the truth than fitting it. Zine No. 20
The Same Water
Stimpunks Zines

Fluctuating capacity, read through the physics of phase transitions. Water doesn't get gradually stiffer as it cools — it holds, then flips to ice all at once at a threshold. Same H₂O, different state, and the conditions (not the substance) decide which. So the exhausting question — why could you do it yesterday and not today? — has a clean answer: capacity is a state, not a trait, and you were near a boundary. The hardest part is the crossing itself (threshold anxiety is sensitivity to a real edge — broken thresholds, not broken people); you can be supercooled, held past your limit until one small thing tips everything at once; and recovery slows near the edge, because that's how systems behave. Nothing is fixed or broken — fluid adaptation and weather-bodies, re-adapting to a changed climate. Extends Minor Worlds: change the conditions, not the substance. Grounded in phase-transition physics, Cynthia Kim, Helen Edgar, and Stimpunks' own threshold anxiety. Eight spreads, six diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 24
Shared Air
Stimpunks Zines

The air we share, read through star stuff and solarpunk — a companion to Bodies of Water. There is only one air: a single, well-mixed body with no walls in it, so thoroughly circulated that your next breath very likely holds atoms once breathed by strangers and by trees across the world. It's a reciprocal breath — we exhale what plants inhale, and our oxygen is the exhaust of ancient cyanobacteria (the Great Oxidation Event, ~2.4 billion years ago), forged first in stars. Every room we share is a shared-air space (a term coined by Helen Edgar & Ryan Boren): in it we trade breath and co-regulate, nervous systems settling into a shared rhythm. The neurodiversity turn: many of us are the canaries of that room. Miners carried canaries because the birds felt the bad air first — not because they were weak, but because they were sensitive, and their alarm was information. Our sensitivity to fragrance, stale air, and fluorescent hum is an early-warning system, not a flaw: don't blame the bird, change the air — build it breathable for the canaries and it's breathable for all. Which raises the harder question: habitable, and for whom? The burden of shared air falls unevenly, so breathable air is a commons, not a private accommodation one person quietly manages. The response is solarpunk: clean air as infrastructure as resistance — open windows, plants, masks, DIY Corsi–Rosenthal boxes — mutual aid you can build in an afternoon. Move quietly and plant things. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 44
You Cannot Flatten a Sphere
Stimpunks Zines

Try wrapping a sheet of paper smoothly around an orange. It creases, always, and that is not clumsiness — it is forbidden. Gauss proved it in 1827 and liked the result enough to call it the Theorema Egregium, the remarkable theorem: a surface’s curvature is intrinsic, so bending that doesn’t stretch can never change it. Paper rolls into a tube or a cone because those are still flat in the sense that matters; a sphere is not, and no care gets you there. You have used this today without noticing — fold a pizza slice and the tip stops drooping, because curving it one way forces it to stay straight the other. The consequence: no flat map of the world can preserve everything, and every projection must sacrifice area, angle, distance or direction. Mercator chose angle, in 1569, so a constant compass bearing would be a straight line — superb for sailors, and it swells Greenland to the apparent size of an Africa roughly fourteen times larger. The map never says what it chose, and that silence is the scandal rather than the distortion. Then Tissot’s answer from 1859, which is the honest one: you cannot remove the distortion, so draw it — a grid of ellipses showing exactly what this map did to this place. And then the turn. An IQ score, a DSM-5 support level, a functioning label: each takes a many-axis person and returns one value, which is not a metaphor but literally a projection — so it must destroy information, the choice of what to destroy is somebody’s, and the number never records it. Worse than a map, in fact: a Mercator keeps every place badly, while a single number doesn’t distort the other dimensions, it deletes them. Ask what a summary was optimised for and you learn whose journey it serves — Mercator for sailors, a support level for a budget, a functioning label for a gate. If you must summarise, publish the indicatrix: the axes it came from, the range it moves across, what it was built for, reviewed by the person measured. Closes on the gentlest thing the theorem gives — a globe has no edges and no corners, so being at the margin was never information about you, only about where somebody cut. Companion to No. 21 Not a Line, which asks what shape the thing is; this asks what a reduction costs. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 45
The Cloud Phase
Stimpunks Zines

The story we get told about a dead star ends about one step past the explosion: the shell expands, thins, cools, and disperses into the interstellar medium. Fade out. But the physics runs further than the sentence does, and there is another word waiting at the end of it. Romano, Behrendt & Burkert (2024) followed remnants through their long cold decline and found the radiative phase divides into four stages — and the last one they call the cloud phase. Not the end phase. Not the dispersal phase. The cloud phase: once nothing is driving the shell and the pressure inside has fallen to match the pressure outside, nothing holds the cavity open, so the remnant falls back into itself and settles as a chemically enriched cloud. That is a 2024 simulation and it is held here as proposed, flagged in place — because the argument's weight sits somewhere firmer. Murray (2011) measured what clouds actually do: in the Milky Way's most luminous star-forming complexes, the star formation efficiency averages 0.08, so ninety-two percent of the cloud never becomes a star — and that is the flattering number, since the Galactic average of 0.005 puts it nearer ninety-nine and a half. A giant molecular cloud lasts 27 ± 12 million years, and the bright episode inside it is short, late, and the thing that tears the cloud apart. Which turns the story the other way round: the in-between is not the wait between the real states. It is the majority state, by mass and by duration — and that is a measurement, offered as fact rather than as comfort. Along the way, SN 1987A: at least 0.2 solar masses of dust condensed inside its own wreckage, grains that did not exist anywhere before the star died. The debris field is a foundry. Written after Helen Edgar, whose Supernova Remnant and Emergence Point map the same stage from the inside — burnout as “remnants of my old self entangled in new ways of being,” recovery as re-assembly rather than return, and deterritorialisation as “a soft loosening of pressure.” A cloud never decides to collapse; it is collapsed upon, by gravity and radiation and its neighbours, so the honest question when one of us reorganises is what the pressure is and where it comes from. Held with care throughout: gestation is not a promise of a star, most of the mass never lights, and the claim is only that the cloud is a real state, not a waiting room. Closes on aluminium-26 in the Allende meteorite — something nearby and freshly dead was in our own birth cloud, and which star is openly contested fifty years on, so we name the contest instead of picking a winner. Eleven spreads, nine diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set.

Zine No. 46
Companion Stars
Stimpunks Zines

A star that shares a system with another star already has a name, and it is astronomy’s own working word: a companion star. Helen Edgar’s thirteenth constellation carries it — “the people who orbit close … who don’t need me to perform and who just ‘get it’.” But before any of that, a correction, because this collection exists partly to take science back from the version that travels furthest. You have met the line most stars are in binaries — the Sun is the odd one out. It isn’t so. Charles Lada put the binary statistics beside the stellar mass function in 2006 and published the answer as his title: most stars are single — two-thirds of main-sequence systems in the Galactic disk. Multiplicity climbs steeply with mass (~26% of M dwarfs, 44 ± 2% of Sun-like stars, near-total for the most massive), and the galaxy is overwhelmingly made of the small end. So this zine will not tell you that everybody has their people. The claim it makes instead is smaller and holds all the way down: where the binding is real, here is exactly what it is. Sirius A and its white-dwarf companion trace a 50.13-year orbit about a barycentre lying in neither star — and equal binding is not equal motion, since the heavier body is displaced less, which is worth saying out loud for anyone who has been told they’re imagining who does more of the moving. Then the hardest spread: bring two stars close and they do deform each other — stretched to ellipsoids, drawn to teardrops, finally sharing one dumbbell envelope with matter crossing between them. Proximity is not automatically good. It is the wide pairs — bound out to a parsec, one orbit longer than the whole history of flowering plants — where both keep their shape. And then the turn, which is theorems rather than reading: with two bodies you can write the solution down, but Bruns (1887) proved no further algebraic integrals exist, Poincaré (1890) closed off the single-valued analytic ones, and Sundman’s 1912 series converges far too slowly to use. So these systems are stepped forward numerically, together, and no one body’s path can be solved on its own and the others added afterward. That same work is where chaos was found — and the part the popular word destroys is that nothing is random: every rule is exact and known, and the system is still unpredictable far out. Read beside the language this community built first — David Gray-Hammond’s Chaotic Self (“you can’t unqueer a queer mind”) and Autistic Rhizome, with Helen’s Rhizome Array and Mycelial Stars — and holding the tension honestly rather than smoothing it: the rhizome describes resilience, no node depending on another to exist; the three-body physics describes non-separability while bodies are bound. Both true, neither cancelling. Closes on what actually holds a bound system: no agreement, no shared origin, no similarity, no upkeep, no performance — mutual gravity and nothing else, which means there is no version of a companion star that has to earn it. Nine spreads, seven diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 48
The Shadow Is Bigger Than the Thing
Stimpunks Zines

Nobody has photographed a black hole, and nobody will. A camera works by catching light that left the subject, and the event horizon is the surface where every outward direction has stopped existing — so the ordinary way of knowing a thing is simply unavailable, and no better lens fixes it. What the Event Horizon Telescope released in April 2019 and May 2022 is a shadow: the set of directions in which light that might have reached you went into the hole instead, ringed by the light that passed far enough out to be bent around and escape. Then the fact this zine is built on, which is exact geometry rather than an impression. For a non-spinning black hole the event horizon sits at 2 GM/c² and the edge of the shadow at 3√3 GM/c² — so the dark circle is about 2.6 times wider than the object casting it. Gravity does not merely hide the thing; it magnifies the hiding. And that magnification is nearly constant across spins and viewing angles (5 ± 0.2 GM/Dc², a spread of about four percent), which is the only reason anyone can work backwards from it. The instrument was the planet: eight radio telescopes at six sites from Spain to the South Pole, observing at 1.3 mm, writing ~3.5 petabytes to helium-filled hard drives that had to be flown to two correlators — the Antarctic share stranded until the winter ended. No site saw the ring; no site could have. Four imaging teams then worked blind to one another, on two different families of algorithm, because everyone involved knew what they hoped to find. M87*’s ring came out at 42 ± 3 microarcseconds — an orange, on the Moon. Sagittarius A* was harder despite being nearer, because its innermost orbit takes about thirty minutes against M87*’s thirty days: the subject rearranges itself while the shutter is open. Then the turn, in two halves. Some things are known only by their effect on everything around them — monotropism, pain, a sensory environment, an access need — and the absence of a photograph was never evidence of an absent thing. But the geometry has a second half, and this piece does not stop before it: what people see of an unmet need is the disruption, not the need, and the disruption is the larger object, inflated by every year of bending and masking and quiet accommodation that got folded into it. In physics you can divide the factor out. In a life there is none, which is exactly why you ask. Closes on the guardrail rather than the warm note: unseeable is not unreal, and it is not a blank cheque either — the collaboration pinned Sagittarius A* to 51.8 ± 2.3 microarcseconds without ever seeing it. Twelve spreads, ten diagrams, a “Not:” refusals set that turns down the obvious reading first. A rhyme, not a proof.

Zine No. 51
Almost Shadow
Stimpunks Zines

In 1604 Johannes Kepler was drawing the geometry of an eclipse and ran out of language. The Sun is not a point of light but a disc, so every part of it throws a shadow in a slightly different direction and the result comes out layered: a core where the whole disc is hidden, an outer region where some of it still shines through, and between them no edge at all — just a handover. He had umbra for the core. For the handover he had nothing. So in the middle of a diagram in Ad Vitellionem Paralipomena he made one: quod est inter KE, NM penumbra dicatur — let what lies between be called penumbra. The verb is dicatur, let it be called; that is not a man using a word, it is the word being made because a drawing demanded it. Paene plus umbra: almost-shadow, and what gets named is neither the dark nor the light but the thing between them. Astronomy keeps three zones now, and the third is the one nobody has heard of: the antumbra, past the shadow cone’s apex, where the Moon sits entirely in front of the Sun — nothing sticking out, nothing off to one side — and it is still not a total eclipse, because the Moon is simply too small in your sky to cover what is behind it. Complete obstruction, and the darkness never comes. Two words would have forced that case into one of the other two and both would have been wrong. Then the pair of facts this zine refuses to separate. First, the partial zone is the enormous one: the umbral track lands as a stripe a hundred-odd kilometres wide while the penumbra covers thousands, so the overwhelming majority of everyone who has ever seen a solar eclipse saw a partial one. The penumbra is where we live; totality is the exception. Second, and cutting the other way, 99 percent obscured is still daylight. The corona is only about as bright as a full Moon, and the surviving sliver of photosphere is hundreds to thousands of times brighter than that — so it obliterates the corona and keeps the sky blue. Illuminance falls roughly a hundred-fold in the last two minutes, another order of magnitude in the final half minute, more than four orders of magnitude in all. Zine No. 50
The Sky Was Not Regular Enough
Stimpunks Zines

Until 1960 a second was a piece of a day — one 86,400th of it — and the definition pointed at something. Not a prototype in a vault, but the sky: you could check it against a star. Then quartz clocks arrived and, for the first time in the history of measurement, the instrument was steadier than the Earth. Scheibe and Adelsberger reported the seasonal wobble in 1936; Stoyko put it at about two milliseconds in 1937. The Earth was never irregular to itself — it became irregular the moment we had something steadier to compare it to. So the tie was cut, twice. In 1956 the second became a fraction of the tropical year 1900, a year that had already ended and could never be re-measured; in 1967 it became 9,192,631,770 caesium transitions, and after that the definition points at no day, no year, no sky at all. Every one of those moves was an improvement, and this zine says so — the collection exists to reclaim science, not to indict it. Since 1972 the gap between the atomic standard and the turning planet has been closed in public by leap seconds: 27 of them, first on 30 June 1972, last on 31 December 2016, and none since, because the Earth started spinning faster and the correction now due is a negative leap second that has never been tested. Duncan Agnew showed in Nature in 2024 that melting Greenland and Antarctic ice slowed the spin enough to postpone it from 2026 to 2029 — climate change bought the world's timekeepers three years, and he refuses to call that good news. Resolution 4 of the 27th CGPM decided in 2022 to widen the tolerance by 2035, and the implementing vote is at the 28th CGPM this October; the proposal is a leap hour. The argument: a leap second is not a bug but an honesty mechanism — a standards body saying in public, on a named date, with a number attached, that its standard and the world disagree by exactly this much. Abolishing it is defensible engineering. What it costs is the announcement. And that is the one thing carried across to us, marked leap because caesium licenses nothing about a person: an accommodation is a leap second, and the objections to it arrive in the same order — it's irregular, it breaks the system, everybody handles it differently — with the same proposed remedy, widen the tolerance and stop announcing. Ends at processing speed and crip time: the Earth is not slow, the second left. Eleven documented joints, one contested, one leap. The reverse is a blank Constellation Index of ten rows — ten rather than twenty, since twenty is her number and printing hers would quietly turn a personal map into a quota. The stars are SVG circles rather than the site's usual CSS starfield, which matters more here than anywhere else on this site: browsers leave background graphics off by default, so a gradient field would have printed as an empty white box, and a blank sheet with no stars on it is not a blank sheet, it is a mistake. Verified by printing it — two sides on A4 and US Letter, with the dots landing on paper. There is no wrong way to fill it in, and no score at the end.
