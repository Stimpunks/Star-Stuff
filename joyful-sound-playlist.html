It Take a Joyful Sound

New wave, new phrase, neurodiversity. Thirty-six songs from the Stimpunks public service announcement — and the argument underneath them, which is about physics before it is about music.

Playlist & essay · Sound
One

Somebody had to name the party

In July 1977, in London, Bob Marley recorded a song with Lee “Scratch” Perry about a party that did not exist yet. He wrote it after hearing what The Clash had done to Junior Murvin's Police and Thieves — a Jamaican record, played back at him by white kids in torn clothes — and rather than take offence he took the hint.

Punky Reggae Party is a guest list. It names The Wailers, The Damned, The Jam, The Clash, Toots & the Maytals and Dr. Feelgood, and by naming them it made a thing out of a coincidence. Two groups of outsiders, sharing a city and a set of enemies, were told they were at the same party. Then they behaved as if they were.

They were not imagining the alliance. On 30 April 1978, roughly 100,000 people walked six miles from Trafalgar Square through Cable Street to Victoria Park in Hackney — deliberately, through National Front territory — for the Rock Against Racism carnival. The Clash, Steel Pulse, X-Ray Spex and the Tom Robinson Band played the same bill, and Misty in Roots led the march from the back of a lorry. Rock Against Racism had been founded in 1976, after Eric Clapton used a stage in Birmingham to endorse Enoch Powell. The mixed bill was the entire point: reggae and punk on one stage was the argument, and the music was how you made it in public.

That is the move this whole page is about. An underground phenomenon gets named, and the naming helps it happen. Which is the same thing neurodiversity did, and roughly as recently.

And the guest list had a hole in it

We are not going to tell this as a fable. The Slits were reportedly in the namecheck and were cut — the reason given, in the accounts that record it at all, was that they were women. The song that named the alliance shipped with a woman-shaped gap where a band should have been, on a bill everyone remembers as the moment punk got principled.

We flag it as reported rather than settled, because we could not trace it past secondary sources. But it points at something that is not in dispute at all, and that runs through the rest of this page: punk's inclusiveness was never automatic. It was fought for from inside, repeatedly, by the people it kept leaving out.

Two

A joyful sound needs something to happen in

Sound is not a thing that travels through the world. Sound is the world, briefly pushed. A mechanical wave is a pattern of compression and rarefaction passing through matter, which means it requires matter — a medium — and gets no exemption. In a vacuum there is no sound at all. Not a quiet sound. None.

So every note anyone has ever heard was carried by atoms. Air is about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, and neither of those was made here: nitrogen comes out of the CNO cycle running in stellar interiors, oxygen out of the fusion shells of massive stars and the supernovae that scatter them. The room is star stuff. The air between the amp and your ear is star stuff. So is the amp, and the ear, and the floor.

A gig is star stuff, arranged into a body, pushing on star stuff, arranged into air, until it reaches star stuff, arranged into someone else.

The whole mechanism. There isn't a step where it stops being true.

This is not a metaphor we are stretching. It is the plain physical description, and it happens to be unreasonably moving: the universe cannot make a sound without lending you its own material to make it out of.

Three

A pure tone has almost no identity

Play a sine wave at 220 Hz. It has a pitch, and beyond the pitch it has nearly nothing. You cannot say what it is. You cannot say who it is.

Now play the same note on a guitar, a cello and a human throat. Same fundamental, three unmistakable things. Hermann von Helmholtz worked out why in 1863, in On the Sensations of Tone: alongside the fundamental frequency, a real sound carries upper partials — harmonics sitting above it at multiples of the base frequency — and the particular recipe of those extras is what your ear reads as identity. The fundamental tells you the note. The overtones tell you who is playing it.

Character is the surplus. It lives in everything above the note you asked for.

The sound punk is named by is difference, deliberately added. That is not a poetic reading of distortion; it is what distortion is. A scene built an entire aesthetic on running the signal past the point where the system could keep it clean, and what came out the other side had more in it than what went in.

A rhyme, not a proof

Physics does not have opinions about people, and an amplifier is not an argument for anybody's belonging. Nothing about harmonic distortion obliges a scene to let you in. We are noting that the sound and the politics happen to be the same shape — surplus is where the character is — and that noticing it is worth something, while proving anything from it is not available.

Four

It reaches you at the skeleton

You already hear through bone. It is why your recorded voice sounds wrong to you and normal to everyone else.

When you speak, part of the sound reaches your inner ear the ordinary way, through the air, and part of it arrives through the bones of your skull. Skull bone passes low frequencies far more efficiently than high ones — work by Tobias Reichenbach's group at Imperial College has mapped the mechanics in detail — so the voice in your head is bassier and fuller than the one anyone else has ever heard. A recording captures only the airborne half. The stranger on the tape is the version everybody else has always known.

In a loud room the same channel opens outward. Low frequencies arrive as pressure on the chest and through the soles of your feet; you are not only listening, you are being pushed on, at a scale your body registers as an event. This is the ordinary, unmysterious reason a gig feels like something happening to you.

And this collection has a standing interest in what bone does under pressure. Iwao Yasuda showed in the 1950s that bone is piezoelectric — put mechanical stress on it and it produces a small electrical charge. That is the founding fact of Bone Song and it is why the phrase is down to your star stuff rather than down to your feelings.

What we are not claiming

Nobody has shown that the sound pressure at a gig produces a piezoelectric signal in your skeleton that matters to you in any way. Yasuda's finding is about stress on bone; a PA is not a load-bearing force. The bone conduction is real and measured. The piezoelectricity is real and measured. The line drawn between them here is ours, and it is a rhyme. We would rather say so than let a nice sentence do the work of a mechanism.

Five

The door is the argument

What punk actually contributed was not a sound. It was a claim about who is allowed to make one.

Marianne Elliott-Said went to see a band on her nineteenth birthday. They were badly rehearsed and playing to almost nobody, and what she took from the evening was not that they were good — it was that this was clearly doable. She formed X-Ray Spex and became Poly Styrene, and within a year a generation of women watching her on television were running the same calculation about themselves. The mechanism has a name in the education literature we work in: you build the thing because you have seen someone build the thing, and the someone has to be near enough to you to count.

Which is also why the exclusions mattered so much, and why the corrections came from inside. The Riot Grrrl manifesto, published in Bikini Kill Zine 2 in 1991, is not a document written by people who found punk welcoming. It is a list of grievances against a subculture that told girls they could not play, and it says so in its own vocabulary — the manifesto's word for the thing in its way is beergutboyrock. Cripple punk arrived for the same reason a decade later: disabled punks were still being handed the inspirational role and refusing it, and the refusal had to be stated because the scene was not going to state it for them.

When it works, the door test is very simple and very literal. In May 2021, four teenagers played a set in the Los Angeles Public Library for AAPI Heritage Month; the recording of Racist, Sexist Boy went everywhere, and The Linda Lindas had a record deal within days. It was a library. There was no stage worth the name. Somebody said yes and pointed a camera.

What is a punk band, after all, but an affinity group with guitars?

CrimethInc., Punk — Dangerous Utopia: Revisiting the Relationship between Punk and Anarchism

That is the whole apparatus. A room, a few people who have agreed to be in it together, and no audition.

Six

Everything brought to the front

There is a sentence that has organised a great deal of our work: everything that was normally supposed to be hidden was brought to the front. It names a whole method in fifteen words. It is also, it turns out, a good lesson in how a phrase travels.

We traced it. It circulates almost everywhere as a quotation credited to “punk subculture” with no author, and the trail leads to the Wikipedia article on punk subculture, where the sentence appears as unquoted prose footnoted to LeBlanc, 1999 — that is Lauraine LeBlanc, Pretty in Punk: Girls' Gender Resistance in a Boys' Subculture (Rutgers University Press, 1999), a study built on interviews with forty punk girls and women.

So the idea has a real scholarly source, and the wording may not be hers. An encyclopaedia sentence summarising a book is not the same object as a line from the book, and we could not confirm which one this is. We are keeping the phrase, because it is ours by use and it is true; we are crediting LeBlanc for the argument; and we are recording plainly that we could not verify it as a verbatim quotation. See Too Good to Check for why this is exactly the shape of thing that gets past people.

There is a second reason to be glad the trail led there. The sentence that names punk's central gesture comes out of a book about a boys' subculture, written to document the women who had to fight it for room. Bringing everything to the front was never a courtesy the scene extended. It was a demand made on it.

Seven

Accepted down to your star stuff

Here is the version of belonging this collection keeps arriving at, and punk states it better than we do, because punk states it as a door policy rather than a sentiment.

You are not admitted on the condition that you turn out to be fine. Nobody checks first. The weird thing about you is not something the room agrees to overlook — it is roughly what the room is for. Come in stimming. Come in with the wheelchair, the cane, the AAC device, the ear defenders, the meltdown you had in the car park. Come in without the money for the door. Rejected by society is not a disqualification here; on the night the party got its name it was practically the entry requirement.

That is what Love You Down To Your Star Stuff means when you take the words literally instead of tenderly. Not despite the difference and not because of it either — which is the other trap, the one where you get admired for your spikes and still not fed. All the way down, past the diagnosis and the file and the behaviour plan and the phenotype, into the elements, which were manufactured in stars, which do not sort their output into acceptable and unacceptable atoms.

The universe doesn't pathologize its own variation. Neither should the door.

The house line, in punk register.

And then the honest part, which we would rather say than have someone discover: a loud room is itself an access barrier. For a great many of us the gig described above is unsurvivable — sensory load, crowds, no exit, no quiet room, no seat. A scene that will let anybody in and then builds the only entrance out of volume has not finished the job. Acceptance is not the nature of any scene. It is a practice, re-won in every room, by whoever notices who is missing.

Not:

  • Not punk as an aesthetic with the politics removed. The clothes without the door policy is a costume, and costumes are cheap.
  • Not the mosh pit as a model of access. Getting in free is one barrier. Volume, crowding, stairs, no seating and no quiet space are five more, and they are the ones nobody puts on the flyer.
  • Not inspiration. We are not here to be anyone's evidence that attitude conquers all. Cripple punk exists specifically to refuse the good-cripple role, and we refuse it too.
  • Not reclamation on someone else's behalf. Crip, mad, weird and stimpunk belong to the people doing the reclaiming. Reclaimed words are not handed out to bystanders.
  • Not a golden age. Punk was racist and sexist and ableist in plenty of rooms, from the start, and the people who fixed it were the ones being excluded. Telling it as a fable would erase exactly the labour that makes it worth citing.
  • Not physics as permission. Harmonics do not vote. The acoustics here are a rhyme with the argument, never a substitute for making it.
One — The party gets its name

Two outsider musics told each other they were at the same party, and then behaved as if it were true.

The naming
Punky Reggae Party
Bob Marley & The Wailers · 1977

Recorded in London with Lee “Scratch” Perry after Marley heard The Clash cover Police and Thieves. A guest list for an alliance that did not exist until it was read out.

The workings
Punky Reggae Party — Perry sessions
Bob Marley & Lee Perry · 1977

The dub-and-vocal version from the Black Ark sessions. Useful for hearing a song being assembled rather than delivered — the alliance is still in pieces on the desk.

Music as jurisdiction
Reggae Music
Jimmy Cliff · 2012

Cliff stating plainly what the form is for: wherever injustice turns up, this shows up too. A late-career song that reads as a job description.

Rights, with conditions attached
Know Your Rights
The Clash · 1982

The public service announcement this whole strand is named after. Rights read out like a charter, each one immediately qualified into something smaller. Anyone who has applied for support recognises the format.

The two musics, joined
Revolution Rock
The Clash · 1979

From London Calling — a cover of a Jamaican record played by an English punk band, which is the punky reggae party working exactly as advertised.

Two — Everything brought to the front

The gesture itself: what you were told to tuck away, moved to the outside where it can be seen.

The founding shout
Oh Bondage! Up Yours!
X-Ray Spex · 1977

Poly Styrene, braces on her teeth, refusing the shape the industry had ready for her. Dick Hebdige used her title as punk's epitaph; a generation of women used her existence as a permission slip.

Turn and face it
Changes
David Bowie · 1971

The verse about the children being spat on as they try to change their world is one of the earliest pop statements that the adults doing the assessing are the ones out of their depth.

Compliance training, from inside
Neurodivergent
Rabbit Junk · 2020

A song written from the chair in the room where you are asked to push the buttons in the right order and told the failure is yours. It ends by naming the construct as a thing that was built, and can therefore be dismantled.

Non-compliance
Standing in the Way of Control
Gossip · 2006

Written against a specific legislative attack on queer people, and general enough to have become the standard anthem for obstructing a control system by continuing to exist inside it.

Against the mould
Spare Me From the Mold
Gossip · 2009

The monoculture objection, sung. Cavendish logic applied to people: uniformity is efficient right up until the blight arrives.

Nothing about us
Nothing About Us Without Us
Tommy Concrete

The self-advocacy motto set to metal, including the part most songs leave out: who did the identifying, what was done with the findings, and who is rewriting the history now.

Three — The door is the argument

DIY is not a style. It is a claim about who is allowed to make the thing, tested at the entrance.

We'll get in anyway
Punk Rawk Show
MxPx · 1996

Two and a half minutes about having nowhere to be and no money for the door, and going anyway. The most cheerful statement of door policy in the genre.

Do it yourself
DIY
Dream Nails · 2017

A list of ordinary competences — bikes, salad, shelves, a broken heart — building to the flat assertion that you are good enough, strong enough, smart enough. Affirmation delivered as inventory rather than as compliment.

Space, taken
Take Up Space
Dream Nails

The companion instruction. Occupying room is not rudeness; being asked to fold yourself smaller is not politeness. Both halves of that need saying out loud.

Riot Grrrl
Rebel Girl
Bikini Kill · 1993

The song from the movement whose manifesto had to be written because punk told girls they could not play. Admiration for another girl, stated without competition — which was itself the argument.

A library, May 2021
Racist, Sexist Boy
The Linda Lindas · live at Los Angeles Public Library, 2021

Four teenagers, a library floor, an AAPI Heritage Month booking, and a record deal within days. Written after a boy backed away from the drummer on learning she was Chinese. The venue is the point.

Owing nobody a performance
No Obligation
The Linda Lindas · 2024

A catalogue of the conditions under which you would be liked better, followed by a refusal to meet any of them. Masking, itemised and declined.

Four — The body in the room

Where the sensory reality lives. Loud rooms are not free, and the songs about that are written from inside the overload.

Overload
Overwhelmed
Royal & the Serpent · 2020

Sensory overwhelm described from the inside — crowds, faces, exits — and then the second injury, which is being told you are too sensitive by someone who has never had to find the exits.

Just relax, they said
Overwhelmed (Ryan Mack Remix)
Ryan Mack

The remix leans on the dismissal rather than the panic — the calm-down-and-take-it-easy that arrives instead of an accommodation.

Unpredictable, and harmless
Wildcard
Slothrust · 2025

Staying home to avoid being the wildcard, and the promise offered anyway that moving strangely is not the same as being a danger. Leah Wellbaum's art appears across the Stimpunks site.

Unmet need
Doom 84
Screaming Females · 2012

Marissa Paternoster on being pinned at home and forgotten, ending on the only request that matters: if you can't heal me, find a way to meet the need instead.

The file, read aloud
Diagnoses
Kae Tempest · 2025

A list of acronyms delivered as a roll call rather than a chart, arriving at the reversal this whole collection is built on: the disturbance is a reasonable response to the conditions.

Bent, not broken
Roots
Grumpster · 2019

Months stuck inside your own head, unable to connect, and the reframe at the end that does the entire job: what happened to you was not a break.

Five — Care is punk

The unglamorous half. Mutual aid, rest, and holding the door open are not the soft version of the politics; they are the politics.

Esmx's theme song
Self Care Is Punk
pink suits

The theme tune of the Stimpunks mascot, and the clearest three-word statement of the position: healthcare, welfare and well-share are the radical acts, not the compromise with them.

Interdependence
I Need Someone To Lean On
Noun

Marissa Paternoster's solo project, saying the quiet thing. Needing to lean is the ordinary human condition, not a failure state to be reported.

If there was ever a time
If There Was Ever a Time
Armstrongs

Tim and Billie Joe Armstrong on standing together, tonight, with whoever is here. Tim Armstrong elsewhere: a community making decisions rather than one person is a beautiful thing.

Breathe
Breathe, You Are Alive / Metsäkukkia
Gaelynn Lea · Paste Studios, 2017

Loop pedal, violin, and an instruction rather than an anthem. Lea is a musician and a disability rights advocate, and the two are one job.

Find your people
All Hail Open Doors
Swamburger & Scarlet Monk

Opening doors as a stated vocation. The song Stimpunks reaches for at the point in the argument where someone realises they have found the house.

Love, translated into action
Evening Prayer aka Justice
Ezra Furman · 2019

Furman wrote it as a rallying cry, citing Abraham Joshua Heschel's line that prayer is meaningless unless it is subversive. Transcendence with a work order attached.

Six — Keep on livin'

Survival, which is the floor and not the ceiling. The Stimpunks mission song sits in here.

The mission song
Keep On Livin'
Le Tigre · 2001

Written by Kathleen Hanna and JD Samson about both kinds of coming out — as a survivor and as a queer person — and adopted by Stimpunks as the theme for the whole endeavour. A song by and for survivors, addressed to whoever is still here.

Standing anyway
I Stand On The Line
Kae Tempest · 2025

Walking yourself home in the rain, being looked at strangely, and telling the younger version of you to keep drawing breath. Resilience without any of the varnish usually applied to it.

The norm is a construction
Statue in the Square
Kae Tempest · 2025

The clearest statement in the playlist that normal is not a fact about people but a device for raising output — and that the people it was built against were here first.

For the outcasts
Imaginary Numbers
Feral the Earthworm

An argument against putting every student through one system, addressed directly to the ones it did not fit, and stated as a dedication rather than a policy note.

Say you're sorry
Shave the Pride
Le Butcherettes · 2015

Take a step back, shave the pride off, stop this madness. Stimpunks files it under the researchers and institutions who have spent a generation not apologising.

A good day for it
It's a good day (to fight the system)
Shungudzo

Fighting the system arranged as a rota: when one of us is tired out, the other one holds it down. Stamina described as a shared resource rather than a personal virtue.

One more
One More
Jimmy Cliff · 2012

One more song, one more life, one more cliff to climb. The right place to stop, because it does not claim the climbing is over.
