A Field Guide to
the Ways of Migrating

Fifteen ways of migrating — the companion catalogue to Always Migrating (Zine No. 30). Say "migration" and most of us picture one shape: a long line, north in spring, back in autumn. Almost nothing does that. A relay no individual finishes. A trip with no return. The biggest migration on Earth, a few hundred metres straight up, every night. And the ones who stay — which turns out to be the commonest form of migration there is.

How to use this guide Each entry gives a way of migrating, the shape its route makes, how a one-shape reading misreads it ("sounds like / actually"), and full field notes. Click any entry to open its notes.
There is no standard migration We inherited one picture — a straight line, on a schedule, completed by the individual who set out — and every crossing that doesn't match it gets read as lesser, or as not really migration. The catalogue says otherwise. Variation isn't the exception here; it's the mechanism.
On the diagrams Each schematic draws the shape of the route — a line, a spiral, a relay of segments, a nightly rise and fall, a scatter on the wind, a population splitting in two. Same question every time: what shape does the crossing make?
Held with care This guide reads migration as a way of being in the world. It is not a reading of human displacement. People forced from home by war, persecution, poverty, or a heating planet are not following a tug or an inner compass — they are surviving decisions made by others. Nothing here naturalises that, excuses it, or softens it.
Not: Not one shape — a line, a spiral, a relay, a nightly rise, a scatter, a stay. Not a schedule — irruptive migrants move by conditions, and they are not late. Not a distance contest — the largest migration on Earth is a few hundred metres. Not a solo achievement — the monarch's route is held by a lineage, not a life. Not a failure to stay — and not a failure to go. One population, both answers. Not unreal because unwitnessed — the eel crossed for a century before anyone saw it.
Held with care

Repeating the note above, because it matters more than the catalogue does: this is a guide to migration as a way of being, and not a frame for human displacement. Forced migration is a political fact with named causes and responsible parties. Reading it as natural, seasonal, or instinctive would launder it — so we don't. The one human entry here is our own, and it is about a crossing nobody is driven into.

Star stuff, migrating

Every traveller in this catalogue is made of matter that already crossed once — forged in stars, scattered by their deaths, gathered by gravity into a tern, a spore, a body reading these words. This is the catalogue behind Always Migrating (No. 30), Helen Edgar's guest zine: migration isn't a metaphor borrowed from animals — it's one of the oldest facts about star stuff. Her essay version, Migration & Trusting Our Inner Monotropic Compass, is at More Realms.

Distances, durations, and rates are approximate, and several are single-individual records rather than species norms; figures follow the sources named in each entry — among them Egevang et al. (2010) on the Arctic tern, the USGS/Max Planck tracking of godwit 234684 (2022), Putman et al. (2013) on geomagnetic imprinting in salmon, Morley & Robert (2018) on ballooning in electric fields, Wright et al. (2022) on the first direct tracking of European eels to the Sargasso, Yu et al. (2015) on Saharan dust and Amazon phosphorus, and Sellwood & Binney (2002) on radial migration in the galactic disc. The reframe — that "migration" was handed to us as one shape, and that a population containing both migrants and residents is the norm rather than the anomaly — is ours. Companion to Always Migrating (No. 30) and No Universal Now (No. 19). Migration was never one shape.
