A Field Guide to
Nests

Thirteen homes among our Terran kin, sorted by where the labour actually goes — and the catalogue turns out to disagree with the word. Most nests are not built. They are borrowed from whoever left, inherited from whoever died, induced in somebody else's body, secreted out of your own, or they are simply two feet and a fold of skin. Building is one option. It was never the only legitimate one.

Where this came from The companion to What Looks Like Chaos (No. 55), which grew from the Stimpunks glossary entry Nesting and David Gray-Hammond's writing about his own room. That zine asks how does a nest get built, and answers that the textbook instinct is learned. This one asks the other question — what counts as one — and finds the answer is much wider than the zine had room for.
Why the last entry is dashed Because the apparatus stops there. Thirteen entries get a reframe table because the popular verdict on them misreads an animal. The rooms people build get none: running sounds like / actually over somebody's own room would repeat the exact failure No. 55 indicts.

Five places the labour goes

The sort is ours, and it came out of the entries rather than ahead of them. We started meaning to sort by material — grass, mud, silk, spit — and the material sort kept failing, because a hermit crab supplies no material at all and a gall wasp supplies none either. What actually varies is who does the work. So the sections below run from the case the word nest assumes, through four that it does not: labour spent on acquiring rather than assembling, labour outsourced into another organism's body, labour paid out of your own tissue, and the case where there is no structure to speak of. The fifth section holds a thing that is not a nest at all and gets mistaken for the best one on Earth. None of these ranks above another. That is the whole reason it is a catalogue and not a ladder.

One · Built

The case the word assumes

Four animals that do assemble a structure out of parts — and even here the word misleads twice. A termite mound is not where the termites live. And the most elaborate structure in this section was recorded by divers for years as an unexplained pattern on the seabed, because nobody could believe the builder was that small.

Two · Borrowed & inherited

The work goes into getting one

Three animals whose housing effort is spent almost entirely on acquisition. A crab that owns nothing but must find everything. A nest so old and so heavy it eventually kills the tree holding it. And a bird whose real ecological job is not its own nest but the vacancies it leaves behind — a landlord that never collects rent.

Three · Induced in somebody else

Making another body do it

Two strategies that sound like cheating and are nothing of the kind — they are among the most-evolved solutions here. One insect makes an oak tree grow its house to specification. And not building a nest at all turns out to have been invented, independently, at least seven separate times in birds alone. A thing discovered seven times over is not a mistake.

Four · Secreted, or worn

Paid out of the body

Three animals for whom the home and the body are not clearly separable. One builds a nest out of nothing but its own saliva. One has no structure at all and is still an excellent parent. And one lined a single burrow with silk and stayed in it for forty-three years — the last of which is really a story about the person who kept coming back to look.

Five · Not a nest

The famous one that does not qualify

If you asked a room to name the greatest nest-builder alive, a good number would say the bowerbird. A bower is not a nest. Nothing lives in it, no egg is ever laid in it, and the actual nest is built somewhere else entirely, by the female, alone. The bower is a theatre — and the deception in it is more sophisticated than the mistake we make about it.

Where the apparatus stops

We are not going to grade your room

This one gets no reframe table, and the absence is the argument. Everywhere above, "sounds like / actually" corrects a verdict that got an animal wrong. Turning that instrument on a person's own room would make us the observer in the doorway with the clipboard — which is precisely the figure No. 55 spends ten spreads indicting. You cannot borrow a method to attack a thing and then use the method on the thing.

Not: Not permission from nature. Nothing here licenses anything about how we live. A universe that could approve could also decline, and we would not take the verdict either way. Down to is not because of. Not a ranking. The sections are five places the labour goes, not five rungs. The gall wasp is not lazier than the weaver, and the hermit crab is not a freeloader — vacancy chains are among the most efficient housing systems documented anywhere. Not mascots. No animal here is Autistic, and none is a stand-in for us. They are kin with their own business. Not a claim that every acquisition is benign. The swiftlet's nest is harvested at industrial scale for soup, and saying so is part of the entry rather than a footnote to it. Not settled. Termite mound ventilation is measured in two species and generalised; the vacancy-chain literature is largely two genera; and the gall-inducer effector mechanism is actively being worked out.
Star stuff, keeping house

Every builder here is spending calcium, carbon and silicon forged in dying stars on the same problem — somewhere to be — and no two of them solved it the same way. The chitin in a caddisfly case, the calcium in an eagle's egg, the silicon in the sand a pufferfish moves one mouthful at a time: all of it assembled in stellar interiors and in the deaths that followed.

What the thirteen have in common is not a method. It is that each of them has somewhere. The methods do not converge, they do not rank, and the ones we would have called failures — owning nothing, building nothing, making someone else do it — are stable, ancient, repeatedly-reinvented solutions. There is no standard nest.

Figures follow the primary and authoritative sources. Pufferfish — Kawase, H., Okata, Y. & Ito, K., "Role of huge geometric circular structures in the reproduction of a marine pufferfish," Scientific Reports 3, 2106 (2013); the builder is a male Torquigener albomaculosus of roughly 10 cm, the structure roughly 2 m across, and the fine sand gathered in the nest centre matters to female choice. A follow-up on the construction rules is Kawase et al., Scientific Reports 8 (2018). Termite mounds — Ocko, S. A., King, H., Andreen, D., Bardunias, P., Turner, J. S., Soar, R. & Mahadevan, L., "Solar-powered ventilation of African termite mounds," Journal of Experimental Biology 220(18), 3260–3269 (2017), measuring air velocity and temperature inside mounds of Macrotermes michaelseni; the paper's own description is that these fungus-farming termites build uninhabited mound structures protruding above their subterranean nests, and that diurnal heating drives the convective flow. The south Asian comparison is Odontotermes obesus. Hermit crabs — Chase, I. D., Weissburg, M. & DeWitt, T. H., "The vacancy chain process: a new mechanism of resource distribution in animals with application to hermit crabs," Animal Behaviour (1988); Lewis, S. M. & Rotjan, R. D., "Vacancy chains provide aggregate benefits to Coenobita clypeatus hermit crabs," Ethology 115 (2009), which verified the prediction that benefits are distributed across many chain participants; synchronous chains involve crabs queuing beside a vacant shell in descending size order, asynchronous chains do not. Bald eagle nest — the largest bird's nest on record, built near St Petersburg, Florida, measured 2.9 m (9 ft 6 in) wide and 6 m (20 ft) deep, examined in 1963 and estimated at over two tonnes (Guinness World Records). Reuse and annual enlargement over decades is standard for the species. Galls — gall tissue is plant tissue whose development is directed by the inducer, which injects effectors including phytohormones; distinct insect species produce distinct gall morphologies on the same host, which is why the literature describes a gall as an extended phenotype of the insect, most explicitly in Hearn et al., "Genomic dissection of an extended phenotype: oak galling by a cynipid gall wasp," PLOS Genetics (2019). Roughly 13,000 gall-inducing insect species are described, about 2% of insects; global richness estimates range from 21,000 to 211,000 and are given here as a range rather than a number. Brood parasitism — interspecific obligate brood parasitism evolved independently at least seven times in birds (Sorenson, M. D. & Payne, R. B., 2002): three times in the cuckoos, once in the honeyguides, once in waterfowl (Heteronetta atricapilla), and twice in songbirds (Viduidae and Molothrus). Roughly 99% of bird species provide parental care. Trapdoor spider — Mason, L. D., Wardell-Johnson, G. & Main, B. Y., Pacific Conservation Biology (2018), reporting a female Gaius villosus known as Number 16 at North Bungulla Reserve near Tammin, Western Australia, at an estimated 43 years the longest-lived spider on record; the population study was begun by Barbara York Main in 1974 and ran over four decades. She died after a parasitic wasp attack, not of age. Bowerbirds — a bower is a display structure, not a nest; the female builds a separate nest and rears the young alone. Forced perspective in great bowerbird courts is Endler, J. A., Endler, L. C. & Doerr, N. R., "Great bowerbirds create theaters with forced perspective when seen by their audience," Current Biology 20, 1679–1684 (2010). Weavers — stated in one line and handed off: the full argument is What Looks Like Chaos (No. 55), from Walsh, P. T., Hansell, M., Borello, W. D. & Healy, S. D., Behavioural Processes 88(1), 1–6 (2011) and Tello-Ramos, M. C. et al., Science 385(6712), 1004–1009 (2024). Held honestly: caddisfly case-building, tailorbird stitching, paper wasp fibre, woodpecker cavity supply and emperor penguin brooding are given as well-established natural history rather than from papers read for this guide, and no precise figures are printed for them. The termite result is measured in two species and generalised to a habit shared by many; the vacancy-chain literature is concentrated in a few genera; gall effector mechanisms are actively contested in detail. The Science paper behind the weaver line was read at abstract level only, as No. 55's colophon also states. The sort into five kinds of labour is ours, arrived at after the entries and not before them. Companion to What Looks Like Chaos (No. 55) and to A Field Guide to Symbioses (No. 6). The beaver is deliberately absent: it is already the niche-construction case in Minor Worlds, and re-carding it here would be a third telling. There is no standard nest.
