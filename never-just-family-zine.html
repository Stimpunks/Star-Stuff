The default

Offered to us as
nature's own unit

Two parents and their biological children. It gets called the natural family, and the word natural is doing something specific: it is claiming that the arrangement was not decided by anybody. That it is simply how living things raise the young.

Everything else then arrives as a departure. A family you assembled out of the people who actually showed up gets called chosen, and chosen carries a quiet demotion — as though a tie you had to select is a tie that could have gone either way, and therefore counts for less than one you were issued at birth.

But notice what has happened. A claim about biology got smuggled in as a claim about worth. And the claim about biology is checkable. Somebody has counted. There is a literature on who actually raises the young across the animals we are related to, and it has been accumulating for fifty years.

So let us read it. Not to find permission — nothing in it can license how anyone ought to be treated — but because a claim this load-bearing should have to be true.

The question is not whether nature approves of chosen family. It is whether nature ever supplied the default that chosen family is supposedly departing from.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 2
The word already exists

Alloparenting

Biologists needed a term for care given to young that are not yours, because they kept running into it. The term is alloparenting — allo-, other. When it is organised enough that a pair cannot raise a brood without help, the arrangement is called cooperative breeding.

How common is it? Here the interesting part is not the number but the correction. Reviewing parental care across the birds, Cockburn assigned 852 species — nine per cent — as cooperative breeders. Previous studies had recognised two and a half to three per cent.

So the count roughly tripled. Not because the birds changed. Because somebody looked properly at a category that had been treated as a curiosity, a handful of odd species you mention at the end of the chapter. It was never a handful. It was hundreds of species, mis-sorted as exceptions.

Keep that shape in mind, because it will recur. A pattern gets called rare for as long as the people counting expect it to be rare.

What the groups are made of

Never just family

Cooperative breeding is usually explained through relatives. Help your siblings and you propagate the genes you share, so a helper at the nest is often described as a young adult investing in the family firm.

Christina Riehl went and checked who is actually in the nest. Of the species where cooperative breeding is a regular occurrence, group composition had been described for 213 of them — and the split is this: fifty-five per cent nest primarily with kin. Fifteen per cent nest primarily with non-kin. Thirty per cent nest in groups that regularly contain both.

Read the first number honestly, because it is the majority: family is really in there. More cooperative breeders organise around relatives than around anything else. Anyone claiming the animals abolish kinship is misreading this as badly as the textbook did.

But read the other two. Forty-five per cent of these species regularly raise young in groups that include birds they are not related to. Which means kinship is one common way to build a nest full of helpers. It is not the rule, and it never was.

Not never family. Never just family — and the difference between those two sentences is the whole reason to read the table instead of the headline.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 4
The part that inverts it

The ones who need each other most
are the least related

Split those species by how much they depend on helping. Some are facultative cooperative breeders — a pair can raise young alone, and helpers turn up when conditions push that way. Others are obligate: without helpers, the brood does not fledge.

If cooperation runs on family, the dependence should make family more important. The stakes are higher, so the loyalty had better be thicker.

It goes the other way. Seventy-seven per cent of obligately cooperative species nest in groups that regularly contain non-kin — 24 of 31. Among the facultative species, only thirty-eight per cent do: 68 of 181. Riehl reports the difference at χ² = 17.1, p < 0.0001.

So when helping is optional, kinship tends to organise it. When helping is not optional — when the young genuinely cannot be raised without a village — kinship mostly stops being what the village is made of. Riehl's reading is that direct benefits substitute for kinship benefits as breeding constraints get severe. Where cooperation is load-bearing, it is not built out of relatives.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 5
The ledger

Eight and a half times

A common vampire bat dies after about seventy hours without blood. A bat that fails to feed can be fed by a roost-mate, who brings up part of her own meal to do it. This is not a small favour, and it is exactly the kind of act kin selection was built to explain.

Carter and Wilkinson tested it under conditions that strip out the usual confound. Over two years they individually fasted twenty bats and induced sharing on forty-eight days, with relatedness mixed and familiarity held equal — so nobody could be helped merely for being nearby.

The best predictor of food given was food previously received, and it was 8.5 times more important than relatedness. Sixty-four per cent of sharing pairs were unrelated — against the sixty-seven per cent you would expect if nepotism were absent altogether.

Sit with that comparison. The network of who feeds whom in that roost looks very nearly like a network with no family in it at all. And two details close off the cynical readings: donors initiated more often than recipients, so this is not harassment or extortion; and the sharing network tracked mutual grooming. The bond is maintained, not inherited.

Relatedness is a fact about the past. Who fed me is a fact about the relationship. The bats keep the second ledger, and it is the one that predicts what happens next.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 6
Adoption, at cost

Half of them were males.
One of those was the father.

For a while the tidy version of chimpanzees said they were indifferent to unrelated group members. It came largely from captive experiments, where individuals shared and cooperated only narrowly, and it got used to mark a boundary: extended altruism toward non-relatives is the human thing.

In the Taï forest of Côte d'Ivoire, across twenty-seven years of observation, Boesch and colleagues recorded eighteen adoptions of orphaned youngsters by other members of the group. Adoption here is not a gesture. It means carrying, sharing food, waiting, years of it.

Half the adopters were male — and of those, only one turned out to be the orphan's father. Genetic testing confirmed three of the males were unrelated to the youngster they took on.

The authors' conclusion is narrow and worth keeping narrow: under the right socio-ecological conditions, chimpanzees do care about the welfare of unrelated group members, and altruism in the wild is more extensive than captivity suggested. The boundary was an artefact of where we had been looking.

A behaviour was called absent because it does not appear in a cage. Twenty-seven years of watching a forest found it eighteen times.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 7
The honest part

Some of them stayed because
there was nowhere to go

Everything so far could be assembled into a warm story: the animals build families out of care rather than blood, so chosen family is the older and truer arrangement. That story would be dishonest, and the literature says so plainly.

Emlen's ecological constraints model — the framework the whole field still argues with — proposes that helping often begins with a housing problem. In a saturated habitat, where territories are scarce and there is no opening to breed into, grown offspring have little option but to remain in the natal group as helpers. Selection favours delayed dispersal not because staying is loving but because leaving does not work.

That is not a chosen family. That is a housing shortage with a nest in it. The birds in some of those groups are cooperating the way anyone cooperates when the alternative is unavailable, and reading their arrangement as devotion flatters us rather than describing them.

Which cuts toward us as well, in the direction nobody likes. People stay inside families that harm them because leaving costs more than staying: housing, money, immigration status, care for a disabled sibling, a religion that would take the rest of the community with it. Constraint is not the opposite of a family. It is one of the things families are made of.

So we are not claiming the animals chose. We are claiming something narrower and harder to dismiss: the arrangement being sold as nature's default is not what nature mostly does — and where cooperation is genuinely required, it is mostly not relatives doing it.

Constraint and care can occupy the same nest. Telling which is which is not a detail of the argument. It is the argument.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 8
Too good to check

The proverb has
no village either

"It takes a village to raise a child." It is the sentence everyone reaches for here, and it is almost always introduced as an African proverb.

In 2016 NPR went looking for where it came from and could not pin it down. Academics they asked disagreed with each other — some pointing to Kijita or Kiswahili sayings with a similar sense, others calling the English phrasing something closer to a modern invention. The Dictionary of Modern Proverbs found no evidence of African origin beyond general sayings about communal cooperation. The African studies scholar Lawrence Mbogoni made the sharpest version of the point: that the proverb may describe a social reality he grew up inside without that making it an ancient saying.

So: the sentence we quote to mean kinship is communal has no traceable kin of its own. Nobody can say whose village it came from.

We are not retiring it — it says something true, and this zine spends fourteen spreads agreeing with it. We are declining to launder it. A claim that flatters the argument is exactly the claim to check first, and an unsourced proverb dressed as ancestral testimony is a small version of the thing we came here to refuse: a story that travels because it is satisfying rather than because it is true.

The irony is doing real work. A phrase about belonging, orphaned. We can hold it anyway. That is rather the point.

Attribution is a form of care. Saying "we don't know where this came from" costs the sentence nothing and costs us nothing — and it is the difference between quoting a people and invoking them.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 9
Us, in the same literature

We were never
the pair either

Humans are in this literature too, and not as the species that transcended it. In Mothers and Others, Sarah Blaffer Hrdy argues that we are cooperative breeders — that alloparents are not a supplement to human childrearing but a condition of it.

Her comparison is a small and startling one. A human forager mother will let others hold her newborn almost immediately. Other great ape mothers keep near-continuous contact with an infant for months and do not hand it over. Hrdy's proposal is that being raised by several people put a premium on reading other minds — and that our particular kind of intersubjectivity grew out of that arrangement rather than producing it.

We should mark the epistemic grade honestly. This is an argued position in evolutionary anthropology, well-supported and contested in parts, and it is not the same kind of claim as 64% of sharing dyads were unrelated. The bat number is a measurement. Hrdy's is a synthesis, and we are not going to promote it to a fact because it suits us.

But it does reverse the usual direction of the appeal. If anything about human childrearing is deep, the village is the better candidate than the pair. The nuclear household — two adults, alone, doing all of it — is a recent and strange arrangement, and people keep failing at it because it is a hard thing to do, not because they are bad at families.

The exhaustion of the private household is not a personal failing. It is what happens when an arrangement that assumed a village gets asked to run on two adults.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 10
Next of kin

The form has
one box

None of the birds needed paperwork. We do, and the paperwork is where the default stops being a description and starts being an instruction.

Who may sit with you in intensive care. Who is told first. Who can make a decision when you cannot. Who gets bereavement leave, a survivor's pension, a tenancy, a visa, a place at the funeral. Every one of those is keyed to a relation, and the relations the form recognises are the ones you did not choose. The person who has fed you for fifteen years may be, administratively, a stranger.

Sophie K. Rosa puts it hard: "The nuclear family is a scab." She means it twice over — "an injury crusted over by blood, and counter-revolutionary." Her argument is not that biological love is fake; it is that a small unit organised around private property and inward-looking priorities is a poor engine for the wider solidarities people actually need. She quotes Silvia Federici: "The denuclearisation of the family is the path to the construction of communities of resistance."

And on the ballroom houses, whose members walk together and call each other family, she quotes Judith Butler's phrase for what those houses did to the idea of a household — "a great subversive rearticulation of houseness" — because "A 'house' is the people you 'walk' with."

Which is where Kath Weston's ethnography arrived, decades earlier, and gave the thing its name. Weston treated family "not so much as an institution, but as a contested concept, implicated in relations of power that permeate societies." Not a natural fact with edges. A claim about who counts, kept in force by paperwork.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 11
In their own space and time

Whānau

Some languages did not need to invent a word for this, because they never split the family off from the people who show up. These are not ours; we are citing them, and the borders matter.

In te reo Māori, whānau is extended family, a family group — and the Māori Dictionary notes that in modern use the term is sometimes used to include friends who may not have any kinship ties to other members. Whanaungatanga is kinship and a sense of family connection: a relationship through shared experiences and working together which provides people with a sense of belonging — which is, almost word for word, what Carter and Wilkinson measured in a roost.

And takiwātanga — Autistic ways of being, "in their own space and time" — was made deliberately. It is Keri Opai's, built from tōku/tōna anō takiwā and published in Te Reo Hāpai, his glossary for the mental health, addiction and disability sectors: a term chosen to sit outside the deficit tone of the English diagnostic language.

Writing at Autistic Collaboration, Jorn Bettin and Ulku Mazlum make the turn this whole zine has been walking toward: "There is the saying that 'It takes a village to raise a child.' The Autistic translation of this saying is 'For an Autistic person it takes an Autistic whānau to feel loved and alive.'"

And the cost of not having one, stated without ornament: "Without the support of an Autistic whānau, Autistic life feels like a life in continuous emergency mode."

Most of us were not born into an Autistic whānau. Bettin and Mazlum's answer is that we co-create it — in our own space and time, which is what takiwātanga was made to say.

The Village Was Never Just Family · Stimpunks 12
Strawberry people

A ledger, kept
in a phone

In Unmasking Autism, Devon Price tells a small story about a man called Samuel, who started marking his phone contacts with emoji. "I put strawberries next to people who were super loving. I put seedling emojis by folks who taught me things that made me think or grow."

Within a year, Price writes, many of Samuel's strawberry people had become his found family. They stood by him through therapy for PTSD and eating disorder recovery. They became friends with each other. They talk in one group chat.

Read that against spread six and it is the same instrument. A strawberry beside a name is a ledger of who fed you. Not who you are related to. Who showed up, repeatedly, in a way you noticed and recorded. Eli Clare, decades earlier, described building chosen families and homes "not rooted in geography, but in shared passion, imagination, and values." Anna Stenning calls this kinship as doing and becoming rather than being — future-facing, mobile, and not bounded by a place.

So here is the turn, and it is narrower than the one you might expect. The animals do not license chosen family. Nothing a bat does can make anybody's household legitimate; difference and belonging would deserve their welcome in a universe that produced exactly one arrangement and no variation at all.

What the literature does is remove a warrant. The claim that biological kinship is nature's default — the claim doing the actual damage, on the actual forms, in the actual hospital corridor — turns out not to survive its own evidence. Nobody has to argue their family into existence against a natural order that was never there.

You were not issued your people. You were fed by them, and you kept count, and that is what kinship has mostly been the whole time.

Not: nature says so. That a village is mostly not relatives licenses no conclusion about how anyone ought to be treated. The ethics are an ethical argument. The birds are why we went and checked.
Not: the animal as mascot. A vampire bat is not a poster for mutual aid. She is an animal with a literature, and the reading has to survive her being uninterested in us.
Not: everyone has their people. Companion Stars already corrected us on exactly this: most stars are single. A great many people are alone and did not choose it, and a zine that promises otherwise is lying to the reader it most needs to be honest with.
Not: constraint is the same as care. Some helpers stay because the habitat is full. Some people stay because leaving costs more than staying. Naming that is not cynicism; refusing to name it is.
Not: biological family is fake. Fifty-five per cent of those species nest primarily with kin. Plenty of people are loved well by the relatives they were born to. The argument is against a ranking, not against anybody's mother.
And: choosing does not make it lesser — and not choosing does not make it fake. Both halves, or the sentence quietly rebuilds the hierarchy it was meant to take apart.

Held honestly. Four cautions. One: the bird figures describe species in which cooperative breeding is already a regular occurrence — they are not a claim about birds in general, still less about animals in general, and 91% of bird species are not cooperative breeders at all. Two: "non-kin" in Riehl is a statement about relatedness within a breeding group, not about affection; a bird that tolerates an unrelated helper has not thereby chosen a family, and we are reading a demography, not a feeling. Three: Emlen's constraints model is the reason spread eight exists, and it genuinely cuts against the warm reading — some of these groups are held together by the absence of alternatives, and the honest version of this zine has to carry that rather than bury it in a footnote. Four: the direction of inference. That nature is various is not why difference deserves welcome; difference would deserve welcome in a universe that made only one shape. As about.html puts it, we are noticing a rhyme, not collecting a proof — and as A Promise Not a Finding insists, the loving in love you down to your star stuff is a promise we are making, not a property of the cosmos. Down to measures how far it reaches. It is not because of.
