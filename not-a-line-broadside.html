Side A of B

Not a Line

A spectrum was never a severity scale. Split a star's light and you get a fingerprint — the pattern of lines that tells you what it is made of, with no high end and no low end. Before anyone reaches for mild, high-functioning, or low support needs: here is what those words actually do.

This is the spiky profile — Damian Milton's, building on Francesca Happé — and it already beats what most people mean by spectrum: nobody is uniformly mild or uniformly severe. But look at what it keeps. A better chart is still a chart, drawn against a flat expected baseline, frozen at one moment of assessment. The reverse drops the grid.

“Mild”
Reports how much your difference costs the person describing it — and is usually written down on a good day, in a quiet room, about someone who was masking hard to be there at all.
“High-functioning”
Heard as a compliment, used as a refusal. It reads coping in one domain as proof that no support is needed in any other, and then withdraws it. The people it describes are the ones told they are not disabled enough.
“Low-functioning”
“severe”
Moves you from the person a decision is about to the object it is made on. It presumes incompetence everywhere on the strength of the places you struggle — and it is how someone becomes too disabled to be trusted with their own choices.
“Low support needs”
“Level 1, 2, 3”
Newer words on the same single axis. Renaming the rungs does not remove the ladder. Support needs are plural and specific, and they move by day, room and domain — one number cannot carry them, and the number is what gets budgeted.
“A little bit on
the spectrum”
Treats the spectrum as a quantity of a thing. It never was one. A fingerprint has no volume knob — and neither does anyone at this table.

Side B of B
Say this instead
instead of mild / severe Name the domain, and the day. “Speech is harder after two o'clock.” “Crowds cost more than deadlines.” Both are checkable, and both can change. A severity level is neither.
instead of high-functioning Name the support — and keep it. “Needs instructions in writing, and a door that closes.” Managing in one room is not evidence against need in another; it is often the cost of the room.
instead of low-functioning Ask the person. Not speaking is not not thinking. Presume competence, find the channel, and wait longer than feels comfortable before deciding there was no answer.
instead of levels & support tiers A list, not a level. What support, where, when, how much, and who decides when it changes. Every one of those is answerable. “Level 2” answers none of them, and it is the thing that gets budgeted.
instead of a little bit on the spectrum Autistic. Or not. The spectrum is what you are made of, not how much of it you have. And if what you meant was “everyone has traits” — everyone does, and that is not what a diagnosis names.
Where the word came from
a spectrum is a fingerprint Split white light and you get every wavelength at once — none higher, none lower. Now split a star's light: threaded through it are fine dark lines, and every element signs its own exact pattern of them. Fraunhofer mapped the lines; Kirchhoff and Bunsen worked out what they meant. It is still the only way we know what the stars are made of. A spectrum does not rank a star — it tells you what the star is made of. The word meant fingerprint. We heard ladder.
a constellation, not a chart Patrick Dwyer offers a different figure — an autism constellation: not a point on a scale but many stars in many positions, each of us a different arrangement. Helen Edgar carries it further, to a constellation of intensities: an interdependent, monotropic galaxy of sensory, cognitive and relational ways of being, in ever-shifting relation. No axis. No baseline. No norm star. A constellation coheres into a figure only from where you happen to stand.

“always in motion, always connecting, always becoming — never fixed, never finished.”

Helen Edgar · The Constellation of Intensities: Neuroqueering the Spiky Profile · Autistic Realms, 2026
Held with care

Refusing the line is not refusing that some of us need far more support than others. Functioning labels do harm precisely because they gatekeep it — sorting people into not disabled enough to qualify, or too disabled to be consulted. The honest replacement for a label was never a better label. It is naming the specific support each of us actually needs, and then providing it. No rank, and all the care.

You are not a point on a line. You are a whole spectrum — and a constellation no one has ever seen before.

The spine of this sheet — line → spiky profile → constellation — is Patrick Dwyer's “The Autism Constellation” (Autistic Scholar, 2020) and Helen Edgar's “The Constellation of Intensities: Neuroqueering the Spiky Profile” (Autistic Realms, 2026); the quoted line is Helen's own. The metaphor is older than either essay and belongs to the Autistic community: Dwyer notes in a 2021 update that he did not coin it — Caroline Hearst had built a physical autism-constellation model and shown it at events, and in correspondence with Helen Edgar, Damian Milton credits Larry Arnold as the first he knows of to use “constellation” in place of “spectrum,” with Milton and the late Dinah Murray discussing similar concepts. A sheet against ranking people should not erase the people whose idea this is. The spiky profile is Milton's, building on Francesca Happé; monotropism is Murray, Lesser & Lawson (2005). The functioning-labels critique is the community's — Ollibean, the Autistic Self Advocacy Network, “Words That Wound” (NeuroHub Community). The science: dispersion (Newton) and spectral lines (Fraunhofer; Kirchhoff & Bunsen). Compresses Not a Line, Zine No. 21, where every claim here is argued at length with its sources attached.
