The credit

Already load-bearing, never named

We have cited Terry Pratchett three times, always as fiction, always as one turtle among several. What we never said is that his witches taught us how to work.

The visible debt is small and correctly filed. No. 35 puts Great A'Tuin beside Turtle Island, ao and Kurma and says plainly they are not the same turtle; Field Guide No. 10 keeps A'Tuin in the turtles people made section with no reframe table, because you do not run a “sounds like / actually” column over somebody's cosmology. That is all sound. It is also not the debt.

The real one is at Stimpunks, and it has been sitting there in plain sight:

First Sight and Second Thoughts, that's what a witch had to rely on: First Sight to see what's really there, and Second Thoughts to watch the First Thoughts to check that they were thinking right.Terry Pratchett, Wintersmith (2006) — quoted on stimpunks.org since 2024

That is this project's method. Not a resemblance to it — the thing itself, described better than we have ever managed. Spread six takes it apart properly. And it is not alone: Miss Level's “I store it in other people” carries our mutual-aid page, the neurodiversity-punk hedgehog mascot is named for Esmeralda Weatherwax, and the Librarian is in the Library. Four load-bearing borrowings, none of them ever credited as where the method came from.

The citation habit points at claims. It does not point at the book that taught you how to hold a claim.the same fault No. 47 found, in a different room

So this page is the credit. And in the course of writing it we went to check the quotations, the way we always do, and two of the three ideas turned out to have the wrong name on them. That is the next spread, and it is not a gotcha — it is the ordinary result of doing what this site says it does.

The fact-check this page owes

Two of the three have the wrong name on them

The Science of Discworld books are the ones this site actually learned from. They are also the ones whose authorship almost everybody, including us, gets wrong.

They are co-authored: Terry Pratchett with the mathematician Ian Stewart and the reproductive biologist Jack Cohen. And they are not co-authored in the blurred way that phrase usually means. The chapters alternate. The odd-numbered chapters are Pratchett's Discworld narrative — the wizards of Unseen University, who have accidentally made a universe in a jar. The even-numbered chapters are Stewart and Cohen's science, addressed straight to the reader about our universe.

Now look at where the ideas we took actually sit.

The quote you have seen shared is “We are the storytelling chimpanzee” — attributed, everywhere, to Terry Pratchett. It comes from the science half of The Globe. So does the passage about thinking critically and not accepting the word of authority. The aggregators file both under the name that sells.

contested We could not reach either passage in the book. Every route we tried ended at a quote aggregator, which this house treats as a lead and never as a citation — the rule we wrote after shipping a Baldwin misattribution that was really Martin Luther King Jr. So: the structure is verified and the inference from it is ours. Which sentence sits on which page stays open until somebody opens the book. See the colophon.

None of this is a complaint about Pratchett, who put both names on both covers. It is about what happens to a citation once it leaves the book — and it is the same mechanism Too Good to Check catalogues. A collaborator gets sanded off because one name travels further.

Two lineages, one book

Narrativium is his. Lies-to-children is not.

Both coinages arrive in the same 1999 book. They come from opposite directions and have completely different parentage, and keeping them straight is the whole job of this spread.

Narrativium is Pratchett's, and it is a joke with a knife in it. On the Disc it is an actual element — the substance that makes stories happen, so that the millionth-to-one chance works and the third son succeeds. It is built as a parody of phlogiston, the imaginary fire-stuff that eighteenth-century chemistry invented to explain burning and then had to abandon. The joke: we invent a substance to explain a pattern we have noticed, and sometimes the substance is fictional and the pattern is real.

Lies-to-children is not his at all. Jack Cohen and Ian Stewart had been working the idea for years before Discworld carried it anywhere:

where lies-to-children actually comes from
yearbookwhat happens
1994The Collapse of ChaosCohen & Stewart discuss the thing: cultural lore passed on in a form that is sufficient but not completely true
1997Figments of Realityelaborated, and the term used for the difficulty of reducing complex ideas for teaching
1999The Science of Discworldreaches a mass audience — with a novelist's name on the spine

A lie-to-children is the thing your physics teacher does when they tell you electrons orbit the nucleus like planets. It is false. It is also the only way to get you to the next rung, and the honest version of the practice says so out loud: this is a ladder, and you are not at the top of it.

Every one of these zines is a lie-to-children. That is not the confession it sounds like — it is the job. The question is only whether we admit the next rung exists.which is the next spread

Turned around

Every zine here is a ladder

If a lie-to-children is a simplification that gets you to the next rung, then this entire site is made of them, and pretending otherwise would be the actual dishonesty.

We say the atoms in your body were forged in dying stars. That is a lie-to-children. Hydrogen is primordial; a good deal of the rest arrives from stellar winds, neutron star mergers and cosmic ray spallation, and “dying stars” is a serviceable rung that is not the top one. We say bone is piezoelectric — and had to correct ourselves twice, because it is the collagen and not the mineral. We drew a spiky profile and then refused our own chart.

So what separates a lie-to-children from the thing this site was built to fight? One property, and it is the only one.

same simplification, two practices
lie-to-childrensmoothed retelling
says it is a rungpresents itself as the top

survives being climbed pastcollapses when you check

Which is why the apparatus around here looks the way it does. “A rhyme, not a proof” appears at the foot of piece after piece, and it is doing exactly this work: it marks the rung. So does the wood-wide-web hedge, where the mycorrhizal symbiosis is ours to use and the forest-wide story is not. So does Field Guide No. 10 refusing to put a reframe table over somebody's cosmology.

The ladder is not the problem. The ladder with the top rung painted on is the problem.what How We Got Here is for

Cohen and Stewart's word for the next stage is the one we keep failing to earn: after lies-to-children come lies-to-adults, which are the simplifications you are handed once you can be trusted with the fact that they are simplifications. That is the register this site is trying to write in.

The method, credited

First Sight and Second Thoughts

Here is the debt paid properly. Everything this site does procedurally is a witch's two-part discipline, and Pratchett named both halves better than our documentation does.

First Sight is seeing what is actually in front of you — not what you expected, not what the story says should be there. It is harder than it sounds, because the expectation arrives first and feels like observation. Our whole Field Guides collection is First Sight as a form: seven of the twelve arrive independently at there is no standard one of these, which is what you find when you go and look instead of consulting the type specimen in your head.

It is also what the corrections look like. The mantis shrimp has sixteen photoreceptor classes and the famous claim collapses on contact with the literature — they discriminate wavelengths worse than we do. The wood wide web rhymes so beautifully with mutual aid that it got past us in three pieces before Karst, Jones and Hoeksema's 2023 review made us go back. First Sight is not scepticism. It is the refusal to let a lovely pattern do your looking for you.

Second Thoughts are the thoughts that watch the first ones. Not doubt — supervision. And this is the half that machinery can actually carry, which is the only reason this site has any:

Second Thoughts, as installed

“the page looks fine”five gates that measure instead of glancing
“we got that right, surely”a changelog that publishes our errors

That last one is the Second Thought that costs something. Publishing your own corrections is not a virtue display; it is the only way the first thoughts ever get supervised in public. A witch checks her own thinking where somebody can see her do it.

The Industrial Revolution books

A technology arrives. Who pays?

There is a run of Discworld novels that all do the same trick: drop one modern innovation into a medieval city and then refuse to look away from the bill.

The pattern is consistent enough to be a method. Moving Pictures gets cinema. The Truth gets the printing press and the newspaper. Going Postal gets the post — stamps, a network, a man made to run it. Making Money gets paper currency and modern banking. Raising Steam gets the railway. In each, the innovation genuinely works, genuinely delights, and genuinely costs somebody something, and the book stays in the room for the third part.

That third part is rare. The default technology story has two acts — the invention, and the wonder — and stops before the bill arrives. Pratchett's have three, and the third is always about who was standing where the thing landed: the clacks workers on the towers, the golems who are property, the engravers whose craft the press makes worthless, the people the railway timetable now owns.

The question is never “does it work?” It works. That is what makes the question interesting.the shape of an Industrial Revolution book

Which lands squarely on techno-solutionism and on the Luddite Sensibilities Stimpunks argues for — and it is worth being precise, because the word gets used as an insult by people who do not know what it means. The Luddites were not against machines. They were skilled workers objecting to a specific arrangement in which the machine's gains went one way and its costs went another. They broke the frames of the employers cutting wages and left the others alone. That is not technophobia. That is a question about distribution, asked with a hammer.

It is also the question this site keeps asking about a different set of machines. An assessment instrument works. A behaviour protocol works, for some value of works. Who pays? is the question How We Got Here exists to keep open, and Pratchett was running the experiment in a fantasy city for thirty years.

The clacks

GNU

In Going Postal there is a semaphore network across the continent — towers, shutters, a code, and crews who live up there. Early in the book a young man named John Dearheart dies on one.

His father puts his name into the Overhead — the working channel the towers use to talk to each other, which no customer ever sees — with three letters in front of it. G: send it on. N: do not log it. U: at the end of the line, turn it around and send it back. GNU John Dearheart, up and down the towers, indefinitely, for as long as the network stands.

A man's not dead while his name is still spoken.Terry Pratchett, Going Postal (2004) — see the colophon on wording

Pratchett died on 12 March 2015. Within days, readers on the Discworld subreddit proposed doing the obvious thing to the network we actually have: put him in the Overhead. An HTTP response header, invisible in the page, carried on every request, logged by nobody, meaning nothing to any browser:

X-Clacks-Overhead: GNU Terry Pratchett

As of today, this site sends it. Every page you load from starstuff.earth carries that header. You can see it in your browser's network panel, under response headers, on this page or any other. It does nothing. That is the point — it is the exact structural joke, an unlogged message that costs one line of config and turns around at the end of the line.

stated plainly This is a fan convention, not Pratchett's instruction. He did not ask for it, his estate did not originate it, and nobody should read the header as endorsement by anyone. It is a thing readers started doing in March 2015 because the book gave them a shape for grief and no better one has turned up since.

It also belongs in this collection more exactly than anything else here. An easter egg on this site is a real piece behind a small door — and the smallest door we have is a header nobody has to read.

Posterior cortical atrophy

The embuggerance

In December 2007, at fifty-nine, Pratchett announced that he had posterior cortical atrophy. He gave it a word of his own almost immediately, and the word was not battle or journey.

PCA is a rare presentation of Alzheimer's disease that attacks from the back of the brain forward. Because those posterior regions do visual and spatial processing, it takes the seeing before it takes the remembering — reading, judging where things are, recognising what your eyes are pointed at. It typically arrives earlier than typical Alzheimer's, in the mid-fifties or early sixties, which is exactly where it found him.

He called it an embuggerance. A nuisance of an unusually total kind. Something in the way.

Not a battle he was losing. Not a lesson. A thing that was in the way, named accurately, out loud, by the person it was in the way of.why the word matters here

Then he kept writing, by changing the method rather than the ambition: dictating to his assistant Rob Wilkins, and using speech-recognition software. He was knighted in 2009. He went on publishing until he died on 12 March 2015.

We are putting this in because Stimpunks is a disability organisation and this is what accommodation actually looks like from inside: the output was never the thing that had to change. The interface did. Dictation is not a lesser way of writing a book; it is a way of writing a book. That is the entire argument of the disability rights movement, demonstrated at length by a man producing novels while his occipital lobe went.

What we are not doing with this. Not treating his diagnosis as inspiration, not describing him as brave for having a disease, and not diagnosing him with anything he did not name himself — including anything on our own side of the neurodivergence line. He told us what he had. That is the only source we need or should want.

Death's challenge

One atom of justice

Now the objection. In Hogfather, Death is explaining to his granddaughter why humans need to believe in things like the Hogfather. Susan pushes back — and he says the hardest thing anyone has said to this website.

YOU THINK SO? THEN TAKE THE UNIVERSE AND GRIND IT DOWN TO THE FINEST POWDER AND SIEVE IT THROUGH THE FINEST SIEVE AND THEN SHOW ME ONE ATOM OF JUSTICE, ONE MOLECULE OF MERCY. AND YET—Death waved a hand. AND YET YOU ACT AS IF THERE IS SOME IDEAL ORDER IN THE WORLD, AS IF THERE IS SOME . . . SOME RIGHTNESS IN THE UNIVERSE BY WHICH IT MAY BE JUDGED.Death, in Hogfather (1996), verbatim — the capitals and the two italics are the novel's own. Checked against the book, not an aggregator.

Read our masthead against that. You are made of star stuff. The universe loves you for it. The first sentence is nucleosynthesis and it is true. The second sentence is not in the atoms. You can grind this entire site down — every citation, every field guide, every carefully sourced figure — and sieve it, and there will be no molecule of and therefore you belong in the powder.

We know. It is written into the house rules: no naturalistic fallacy, and the line that closes No. 35 — that variation persists in nature is not why difference deserves welcome. The physics does not entail the love. It never did. Anyone who tells you the universe validates your existence because carbon is common is selling you the same trick the eugenicists sold, with a nicer conclusion attached.

So what is the second sentence doing there? Pratchett's answer arrives a few lines earlier in the same conversation, and it is the ladder from spread four in the novelist's half of the shop:

YES. AS PRACTICE. YOU HAVE TO START OUT LEARNING TO BELIEVE THE LITTLE LIES.
“So we can believe the big ones?”
YES. JUSTICE. MERCY. DUTY. THAT SORT OF THING.Death and Susan, Hogfather (1996), verbatim

That is lies-to-children, arrived at from the fiction side. Cohen and Stewart's ladder gets you from planetary electrons to quantum mechanics. Death's gets you from the tooth fairy to justice, mercy, duty — the big lies, which are not found in the powder either, and which exist anyway because enough people practised on the small ones and then acted as if the big ones were real.

Susan makes exactly our objection — “people have got to believe that, or what's the point—” — and Death's reply is three words long and closes the argument:

MY POINT EXACTLY.Death, Hogfather (1996)

The point is not that it is out there. The point is that it has got to be believed. And a few lines later he says the other half, helping her onto the horse:

NO. YOU NEED TO BELIEVE IN THINGS THAT AREN'T TRUE. HOW ELSE CAN THEY BECOME?Death, Hogfather (1996) — quoted at Stimpunks under Becoming, where the capitals and the italic on become had been lost

How else can they become. Not a consolation — a construction schedule. Justice is not a discovery, it is a thing being built, and belief is the scaffolding you stand on while you build it. That is the same grammar as the falling angel meeting the rising ape: we are the animal that is on its way somewhere, in both directions at once, and the meeting place is made of things we decided to mean.

And there is one more beat, which could have been written for this site specifically. Death points out that stars explode and worlds collide and there is almost nowhere in the universe a human can survive — and yet you believe that a bed is a normal thing. He calls it the most amazing talent, and then, being Death, calls it a very special kind of stupidity. Both of those are correct at once, which is the register this entire project is written in.

So: the tagline is a promise we make, not a fact we found. That is a stronger thing to be, not a weaker one. A fact you found could turn out otherwise. A promise is only broken if we break it.what this whole site is, said accurately

Love you down to your star stuff is therefore an act, not an observation. The star stuff is the checkable half and it is genuinely, gorgeously true. The love is ours, and we are putting it there on purpose, and it will hold exactly as long as we keep holding it. Which is the only kind of justice there has ever been.

Not:authorised, endorsed, or anything to do with the Pratchett estate. They have no involvement in this page, were not asked, and have not seen it. Every reading here is ours and so is every error.
Not:a claim that Pratchett was neurodivergent. He named one condition and we take him at his word on it. Diagnosing the dead in order to recruit them is a thing done to us often enough that we are not going to do it to anybody else.
Not:an argument that fiction beats evidence. The opposite. This whole collection exists to reclaim science from the things that wear its clothes, and Pratchett is on that side — the Science of Discworld books are a sustained argument for the practice, not against it.
Not:“believe whatever you like.” The little lies are practice for justice and mercy, not for anything you fancy. A belief that has to become true is under an obligation the moment you hold it, and most comfortable beliefs are not.
Not:a retreat from the citations. Everything checkable on this site is still checked, and this page found three attribution errors while being written. Saying the love is a promise does not soften a single figure.
Not:a claim on grief that isn't ours. Millions of people loved this writer. We are one small project saying where four of our habits came from.

What this page is, and is not

It is a credit and an appreciation, written entirely by us. Terry Pratchett's estate has no involvement, was not consulted, and has not seen it. Nothing here should be read as authorisation or endorsement — including the header on spread eight, which is a reader convention this site has chosen to join.

One thing we have deliberately left out. Pratchett spent his last years as a prominent advocate for assisted dying, including the 2011 documentary Choosing to Die. That is a genuinely contested matter within disability communities — including ours, including among people who love his books — and it deserves either its own carefully sourced piece or nothing at all. It does not deserve a paragraph on the way past. So it has one here, saying that, and no more.

It sits in Easter Eggs because it is a page about our own furniture: where our method came from, and what our own tagline can and cannot claim. An egg here is not a lower standard — same sourcing, same grading, same ledger row, same gates. It is just not in the grid.

A rhyme, not a proof

A comic fantasy novel is not evidence for a paradigm of human variation, and Death is a character. Where this page sets his challenge against our masthead, it is because the challenge is correct and we would rather answer it in public than hope nobody asks. The star stuff is real and checked. The love is ours, deliberately added, and held on purpose. GNU Terry Pratchett.
