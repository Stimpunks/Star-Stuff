Proportioned
to the Groove

four lines of Dickinson, and why a narrow channel is a fit rather than a shortage

Words: Helen Edgar
With Ryan Boren
Scroll ↓  the groove opens as you go
01 · The Poem

Four lines, nothing left over

That Love is all there is,
Is all we know of Love;
It is enough, the freight should be
Proportioned to the groove.

— Emily Dickinson, The Single Hound (1915), poem CX

A groove, in the sense Dickinson is using, is the channel a load runs along — a rut, a track, a rail. The freight is what the channel carries. She is not saying love is small. She is saying it is matched: enough is a relation between a load and the thing that carries it, not a quantity you could weigh on its own.

On the number

This poem is usually cited as 1765, which is Thomas H. Johnson's number from his 1955 edition — the numbering this collection already uses, as J1765, in No. 67. Dickinson titled and dated almost nothing, so a number is an editor's decision rather than hers. We could not confirm the corresponding Franklin number and so do not give one. The text above is quoted from the poem's first printing, where it appears untitled as poem CX on p. 118.

02 · The Groove

A channel, not a limit

Dickinson isn't describing a shortage. She's describing a fit. Love doesn't need to be vast to be complete — it needs to match the channel it moves through. A groove that carries everything it was built to carry is not a smaller love. It's a finished one.

Monotropism describes attention working the same way: interest pulled into a small number of deep channels rather than spread thinly across many at once (Murray, Lesser & Lawson, 2005). Read against that, proportioned to the groove stops sounding like a consolation and starts sounding like a description — of how focused, monotropic love already works.

The freight was never supposed to spread thin. It was supposed to fit.

03 · The Refrain

A child in the dark

Deleuze and Guattari open their plateau on the refrain — 1837: Of the Refrain — with exactly this kind of groove. A child in the dark, gripped by fear, comforts himself by singing under his breath. Lost, he takes shelter, or orients himself with the little song as best he can.

“a rough sketch of a calming and stabilizing, calm and stable, center in the heart of chaos”

Deleuze & Guattari, A Thousand Plateaus, trans. Massumi (1987), on the child's song

That's the groove doing its first job. Not confinement — shelter. A pattern repeated until it becomes somewhere safe to be. They are careful to add that the song is itself a skip: it jumps from chaos to the beginnings of order, and it can come apart at any moment. The shelter is real and it is not guaranteed.

A reader's own refrain

I was that child humming in the dark. Fear was the prevailing feeling of my childhood. I was scared of everything and felt tossed about by forces I didn't understand and had no hope of controlling.

I spent my nights sitting in the hallway outside my parents' bedroom door, staring at the light in the ceiling and humming to myself as I feared the world at either end of the corridor. The hum in my chest and the hum in the light offered a comforting groove, carved from unknowable chaos.

— Ryan Boren

04 · The Circle

Home does not preexist

The second movement of the refrain is the one that gets skipped in the retelling, and it is the one that matters most here. The song stops being a point in the dark and starts being a space. Deleuze and Guattari put it flatly:

“Now we are at home. But home does not preexist”

Deleuze & Guattari, A Thousand Plateaus, trans. Massumi (1987)

It has to be drawn. A circle around an uncertain centre, marked out and held, so that the forces of chaos stay outside and something fragile inside gets room to work. Their own second example is a hallway light away from the one above: a child hums to summon the strength for the schoolwork she has to hand in.

This is the part worth saying plainly to anyone who has been told their coping is a symptom. The repetition is not the residue of the fear. It is the construction of the room.

05 · The Opening

One opens the circle a crack

Then the third movement. The circle that was built to keep chaos out is opened — not on the side where the old forces press against it, but somewhere the circle itself made possible. One opens it, they write, “in order to join with the forces of the future, cosmic forces.”

“One ventures from home on the thread of a tune.”

Deleuze & Guattari, A Thousand Plateaus, trans. Massumi (1987)

And then the line that keeps this from being a ladder: these are not three successive moments in an evolution. They are three aspects of one thing. The shelter, the room and the opening are not stages you graduate through, leaving the humming behind. They are happening at once, in the same small repeated tune.

Nothing about the groove has to widen for that to happen. It only has to open. The channel that made the shelter possible is the channel that makes the cosmic connection possible — not despite its narrowness, but through it.

A correction we made in the building

This spread was drafted around a line about a cowbell being “promoted to the status of celestial noise” through sheer repetition, cited to A Thousand Plateaus. It is not in that book. Searching the Massumi translation, cow bells appear once, in a list making a different argument, and celestial appears elsewhere again. The image is genuinely Deleuze's — but he says it aloud, alone, in the filmed interviews L'Abécédaire, in the letter R comme Ritournelle. The only English we could reach is one scholar's own translation of spoken French, so we give the image as a paraphrase and let the verbatim quotations above, which we read at the primary, carry the argument instead.

06 · Star Stuff

The freight is literal

“Because the cosmos is also within us. We're made of star-stuff. We are a way for the cosmos to know itself.”

Carl Sagan, Cosmos: A Personal Voyage, ep. 1, “The Shores of the Cosmic Ocean” (1980)

Not a metaphor, and not a flourish. The calcium in the bones doing the humming, the iron in the blood, the carbon in every cell — forged in the interiors of stars that finished burning before the Earth existed, and scattered when those stars came apart.

So the freight in Dickinson's four lines is not only a figure of speech either. Something actual is being carried, and it has to take the shape of whatever carries it: a body, a relationship, a hallway at night, a line of a poem.

07 · LYDTYSS

Down to is not because of

This is where the groove, the refrain and the star stuff meet. Love You Down To Your Star Stuff is this house's central phrase, and its grammar is doing careful work.

Down to measures how far the loving reaches. It does not say the star stuff is the reason for it. The elements are the extent of the claim, not its warrant — which is exactly the distinction Dickinson's line is already making. The freight is proportioned to the groove; the groove is not what earns the freight.

So: to love someone down to their star stuff is not to love them despite the narrowness of their channel. It is to love the specific, elemental material that channel is carrying, without asking it to widen first.

08 · The Freight

What the channel was carrying all along

A monotropic love often looks like one interest returned to again and again; one person's whole attention offered rather than divided; an info-dump handed over like a gift, because it is one. None of that is love running low. It's the freight, exactly proportioned, moving through the only channel it was ever going to fit.

It does not need translating into a broader, more diffuse shape to count. It was already enough.

A reader's own refrain

I've felt the freight of limerence, and of requited love. The attention tunneling, furrowing, following its groove.

— Ryan Boren

A companion reading runs through No. 66, which arrives at the same place from the other direction: a groove is a loop with a body attached. Dickinson's groove is a channel; Byrne's is a repetition you can dance to. Both are patterns that hold a person up.

09 · An Invitation

What's your groove carrying?

Think of the channel your own love travels through — the single interest, the one person, the particular way you show up for someone.

Has it been met at the size it actually is? Or has it been asked to widen before it was allowed to count?

Sit with that for a moment before you scroll on.

10 · Colophon L★S

Love You Down To Your Star Stuff

Refusals

  • monotropic attention as a deficit to be broadened into something more respectable.
  • “narrow but deep” offered as a consolation prize for not being the other thing.
  • a poem as evidence. Dickinson's four lines are a rhyme with monotropism, not a proof of it, and she is not a witness we get to call.
  • retroactively diagnosing Emily Dickinson. Reclusiveness is not a diagnosis, we have no standing to hand her an identity she never claimed, and the argument does not need her to be one of us to work.
  • the naturalistic fallacy. That the universe is built out of difference does not by itself make difference good; we love people down to their star stuff because we choose to, not because physics instructs us.
