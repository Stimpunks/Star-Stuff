Skepticism and Wonder

The Rainbow Connection — forty songs, and the two uneasily cohabiting modes of thought they carry. A song told a six-year-old that rainbows have nothing to hide, and he took it as permission to love the real thing more than any story about what it might be concealing.

A Sound rack · companion to Zines No. 69 and No. 70
One

1979, and a six-year-old

I grew up with The Muppet Show. The Muppet Movie came out in 1979, when I was six, and I was captivated by its opening song.

“Rainbow Connection” was written by Paul Williams and Kenneth Ascher and sung by Jim Henson as Kermit, sitting on a log in a swamp with a banjo. It was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Original Song, the American Film Institute later placed it 74th among movie songs, and in 2021 the Library of Congress added it to the National Recording Registry.

None of which is why it mattered to me. It mattered because of what its first verse does in the space of six lines.

The verse opens by asking why there are so many songs about rainbows and what is on the other side. Then it grants that rainbows are visions — and immediately downgrades them, calling them only illusions. And then it lands the line that did the work: rainbows have nothing to hide.

On the lyrics, which are not printed here This page discusses “Rainbow Connection” closely and quotes almost none of it, because reproducing a copyrighted lyric is not ours to do. The verses are characterised rather than transcribed, individual words appear where the argument turns on that exact word, and the full lyric is one link away at Genius. If a sentence here seems to be circling a line rather than quoting it, that is why.

Two

Nothing to hide

To a science-loving kid, nothing to hide was the whole song.

It recognises that a rainbow is an artifact of observation. There is no pot of gold in its reaches. There is no other side, because there is no side. Rainbows are not withholding anything from you, and they are not keeping a secret at the end that you might reach if you walked far enough. Rainbows just are.

As a closeted queer kid and a closeted atheist, I found the observational reality of rainbows better than any story of what they might hold if they were fixed and finite. The science of them filled my head with more wonder and awe than the stories told about them ever did. That is an unusual thing for a song aimed at children to allow, and it is why this one stuck.

A thing with nothing to hide is not a thing with nothing in it.

Three

The one word the song gets wrong

And here the rack has to be honest about its own opening track, because the verse contains a word that our own Zine No. 69 spends twelve spreads refusing.

“Illusions.” A rainbow is not one. An illusion is a mistake your perception makes — something you seem to see that is not occurring. A rainbow is occurring. It is light, refracted and reflected in falling water, arriving at your eye along a path that is calculable to a fraction of a degree from a refractive index of 1.3315. Roger Bacon measured its maximum elevation at 42 degrees around 1267, with an astrolabe and no theory of refraction at all, and the number has not moved.

What is true is stranger and better than illusion: a rainbow has no location. It possesses, in Les Cowley's words, “neither distance nor linear dimension.” It is a direction rather than a place, centred on the shadow of your own head, and no two people have ever seen the same one. That is not a thing failing to be real. That is a real thing that is not an object.

So I read the chorus — so we've been told, and some choose to believe it — not as negating the verse but as refusing the reduction in it. The true nature of rainbows is more wonderful than any of the flattenings, and it opens far more metaphorical room, not less. They are perfect circles, centred on the observer, made of solar light and falling water and a working eye, all three in real-time collaboration. How connected and wonderful is that?

Four

Two uneasily cohabiting modes

The next verse asks who decided that a wish on a star would be heard, notes that somebody thought of it and somebody believed it, and then says, flatly: look what it's done so far.

I was a skeptical kid and a closet atheist. Fascinated by stars; unconvinced by wishing on them. What I liked was the ambiguity — that line can be read as praise or as indictment and the song does not tell you which. It holds the good and the bad of belief in the same hand. I chose to hold both the skepticism of science and the wonder of dreaming, and the song let me.

Sixteen years later, Carl Sagan gave me the words for what I had been doing. In The Demon-Haunted World: Science as a Candle in the Dark (1995), writing about his parents:

But in introducing me simultaneously to skepticism and to wonder, they taught me the two uneasily cohabiting modes of thought that are central to the scientific method.Carl Sagan, The Demon-Haunted World, 1995

Uneasily cohabiting. Not balanced, not reconciled — sharing a house and not always comfortably. That is the correct description, and it is what “Rainbow Connection” had already modelled for me before I had a name for it. The song is skeptical in the verse and hopeful in the chorus and does not resolve the tension, because the tension is the point.

Five

Princes of the air

Autistic, skeptical minds have been looking at the stars and inventing the future out of their dreams for a long time.

NeuroTribes has a chapter about it — “Princes of the Air” — documenting the stargazing visionaries who turned science fiction into working technology and connected the world through the air. Steve Silberman notes that Hans Asperger may have been the first clinician to see that his patients' imaginations sometimes anticipated developments in science by decades, which forced him to walk back his own claim that their interests were remote from real-world concerns:

But his joking suggestion that the designers of spaceships themselves must be autistic also turned out to be prescient.Steve Silberman, NeuroTribes — ch. “Princes of the Air”

The joke was that we were unworldly. The record is that we were early.

Six

The alembic

There is a third voice worth adding, and the lineage is not a coincidence.

Dorion Sagan — son of Carl Sagan and of Lynn Margulis, whose endosymbiosis this whole collection already runs on — puts the same two modes together as a piece of laboratory glassware:

Science's eye for detail, buttressed by philosophy's broad view, makes for a kind of alembic, an antidote to both.Dorion Sagan, Cosmic Apprentice: Dispatches from the Edges of Science

An alembic is a still: two vessels joined, one heating and one condensing, and neither of them useful alone. An antidote to both is the sharp part — detail without breadth curdles into scientism, breadth without detail into vapour, and each is the cure for the other's excess. Which is the same claim Sagan the elder made about skepticism and wonder, and the same one a frog made on a log in 1979.

Seven

What the song was later found to cost

In March 2023, first-graders at Heyer Elementary in Waukesha, Wisconsin, were preparing a spring concert on a theme of world unity and peace. Their teachers picked “Rainbowland,” the Miley Cyrus and Dolly Parton duet on this rack.

The district vetoed it, reviewing the song against a board policy on controversial issues in the classroom. The music teacher was asked to find something else, and what she landed on was “Rainbow Connection.”

That was initially banned too. It was reinstated only after parents pushed back. The teacher who spoke publicly about the first ban, Melissa Tempel, was later terminated.

Sit with the shape of it. A song whose entire argument is that rainbows are ordinary optical phenomena with nothing hidden in them — written for a puppet, nominated for an Oscar, entered in the National Recording Registry — was, for a moment, too controversial for six-year-olds. Not because of anything in the song. Because of what the word in its title had come to be suspected of.

Which is the skepticism half of this rack arriving from a direction I did not expect when I was six. The wonder half is easier and it is still true: the light is real, the water is real, your eye is real, and the three of them are collaborating right now. Nothing is being hidden from you. That was always the good news.

What this rack is not saying

  • Not that “Rainbow Connection” is a queer anthem or an Autistic one. It is a song about wanting to believe things, written by two songwriters for a film, and it did not have me in mind. What it did was leave room, and room is what a closeted kid can use.
  • Not that skepticism and wonder resolve into one comfortable thing. Sagan's word was uneasily, and the discomfort is doing work — drop the skepticism and you get credulity, drop the wonder and you get the flattening this collection spends most of its time arguing against.
  • Not that every song here agrees with the others. “Over the Rainbow” wants somewhere else; “What a Wonderful World” wants this one; “Rainbow Stew” thinks you are being sold something. They are on the same rack because the argument is between them, not inside any one of them.
  • Not a claim on the Pride flag. It is a chosen set of colours, not the physical spectrum, and the Philly stripes were added precisely because a “natural” spectrum was being used to leave Black and brown queer people out. No. 70 holds that line and this rack does not cross it.
One — The song itself · eleven ways through it

Paul Williams and Kenneth Ascher, 1979. The verse is skeptical, the chorus is not, and nobody has ever resolved that — they just pick which half to lean on.

The original, handed over
Rainbow Connection (Sing Along)
The Muppets · Jim Henson as Kermit

The Muppets’ own sing-along cut, which is the right way to meet it: the song was written to be joined, and Henson performs it as a small voice thinking out loud rather than a big voice declaring.

The song as a room
A Special Performance of “Rainbow Connection”
The Muppets

Kermit alone with the banjo. Strip the film away and what is left is a character admitting he keeps being drawn to something he has just finished calling an illusion.

Sung by a crowd
“Rainbow Connection” with Choir! Choir! Choir!
Kermit the Frog & New Yorkers · Lincoln Center

A room of strangers taught the song on the spot. The lyric is about lovers and dreamers finding each other; this is the only version where you can watch that happen in real time.

The outlaw reading
Rainbow Connection
Willie Nelson

Nelson sings it as a plain statement of fact from someone who has had a long time to consider the question, which turns the wistfulness into something closer to patience.

The unreleased one
The Rainbow Connection (Outtake)
Carpenters

An outtake, surfaced on the Carpenters’ catalogue channel rather than issued at the time. Karen Carpenter’s voice does something specific to the sceptical verse: she declines to be arch about it.

The full-throated one
Rainbow Connection
Barbra Streisand

The maximalist reading, and on the rack precisely because it is the opposite of Henson’s — proof that the song survives being sung as though it were certain.

The soft-rock reading
Rainbow Connection
Kenny Loggins

Loggins takes it at face value and lets it be pretty. A legitimate choice and a useful control: this is what the song sounds like with the scepticism sanded off.

The one you would not guess from the link
Rainbow Connection
Weezer feat. Hayley Williams

From Muppets: The Green Album (2011). The link text carries no artist at all; oEmbed returned Weezer. Two voices trading a song about not being sure, which suits it.

The punk one
Rainbow Connection
Me First and the Gimme Gimmes

A covers band who play everything fast and mean it anyway. Speeding it up removes the room to be wistful and leaves only the questions, which is a reading rather than a joke.

The lullaby
Rainbow Connection
JJ Heller

Sung to a child, which returns the song to its actual audience and raises the stakes of the verse: this is the version where somebody is being told rainbows have nothing to hide for the first time.

The duet nobody expected
Rainbow Connection
Debbie Harry & Kermit the Frog

Blondie’s singer and a frog, on television. The Muppets’ standing method — put the icon next to the puppet and let neither condescend — is the move this collection keeps making with facts.

Two — The joke that agrees with the song

One track, because it earns its own section: a comedy duo arriving at the essay’s thesis by the shortest possible route.

The pun that is also the point
Rainbow Connections
Garfunkel and Oates

A song about the connections you make with people, hung on the rainbow of the title. The joke depends on the listener already knowing a rainbow is about relation rather than location — which is the argument of No. 69, reached by comedians first.

Three — Somewhere over · the wish, eleven times

Harold Arlen and E. Y. Harburg, 1939. The other great rainbow song, and it wants the opposite thing: not that rainbows have nothing to hide, but that there is somewhere on the far side worth reaching.

The original wish
Over the Rainbow
Judy Garland

The Wizard of Oz, 1939; it won Best Original Song. The rainbow here is explicitly a threshold to somewhere else — the exact reading “Rainbow Connection” would push back on forty years later.

The one carrying two songs at once
Somewhere over the Rainbow
Israel “IZ” Kamakawiwoʻole

His medley folds “What a Wonderful World” into it, which is why this rack has a section for each: IZ had already noticed the two songs answer the same question. Also carded on the L★S rack — one of the few songs held in two racks here.

Recorded in a small room
Over The Rainbow (Blues Alley)
Eva Cassidy

Live at Blues Alley in 1996, the year she died at thirty-three, and almost unknown at the time. The recording found its audience long afterwards, which is its own comment on arrival and delay.

The quiet one
somewhere over the rainbow
Christina Perri

Taken slowly and small — the wish read as something you say to yourself rather than announce, which is closer to how wishes actually get made.

The two songs spliced on purpose
“Somewhere Over the Rainbow / I’m Yours”
GENTRI

An arrangement that follows IZ’s instinct and pairs the wish with a song about being present. The seam is the interesting part.

The young voice
Over The Rainbow (RAWsession)
Nikki Yanofsky

A jazz reading that refuses the sentimental default and treats the melody as a set of intervals to be worked, which is a form of respect.

No words at all
Over the Rainbow (Tokyo 1984)
Keith Jarrett

Solo piano. Strip the lyric and the wish is still legible in the shape of the tune — that famous octave leap is the reaching, and it is there without anybody saying so.

The one that slows down to look
Over the Rainbow (Live at Jazz at Lincoln Center)
Cécile McLorin Salvant

Salvant treats the lyric as a text to be read closely, holding words long enough that you hear what they are actually asking for. Skepticism applied to a song, and it survives.

Two voices, no hurry
Over The Rainbow
Ray Charles feat. Johnny Mathis

Charles brings the gospel weight, Mathis the clean line. The wish sung by people who sound like they have wished before and know the odds.

Live, 1958
Somewhere Over The Rainbow
Sarah Vaughan

From Holland, 1958. Vaughan’s control is the argument: she could place the note anywhere and chooses restraint, so the longing reads as decided rather than helpless.

The last of the eleven
Over The Rainbow
Ella Fitzgerald

Another link with no artist in its text; oEmbed returned Fitzgerald. Her reading is unusually plain, and the plainness is what makes the wish sound reasonable.

Four — The world as it is · the counterweight

The answer song, whether or not it was written as one. Not somewhere over anything — here, and already sufficient.

Here, not elsewhere
What A Wonderful World
Louis Armstrong

1967. Armstrong lists ordinary things and declines to wish for others. Beside “Over the Rainbow” it is a rebuttal; beside “Rainbow Connection” it is agreement.

Played across borders
What a Wonderful World
Playing For Change · Song Around The World

Musicians in many places recorded separately and assembled into one take. The method is the message: the wonderful world is demonstrated rather than described.

The reclaiming
What A Wonderful World
Jon Batiste

Batiste takes a song worn smooth by advertising and finds the grain again, largely by refusing to smooth the piano.

With an orchestra behind it
“What A Wonderful World” w/ National Symphony Orchestra
Jon Batiste · The Kennedy Center

The same reading at scale — useful next to the previous track for what does and does not change when you add ninety people.

Five — Naming the colours

Two songs that treat a rainbow as a thing with parts, which is what the skepticism half of this rack is for.

The colours as a list
You Can Sing a Rainbow
Nina Simone

A children’s song about naming colours, sung by one of the most formally serious musicians of the century. A third unlabelled link; oEmbed returned Simone’s catalogue channel. The song itself is Arthur Hamilton’s, 1955.

The colours as physics
Roy G Biv
They Might Be Giants

From Here Comes Science (2009), and the most literal track here: the spectrum as a mnemonic, sung to children, with no wishing in it at all. The band who made a career of being silly and exact, being both.

Six — I am a rainbow · the metaphor turned inward

At some point the rainbow stopped being in the sky and became a description of a person. These are the songs that made that move — and the reason a word in a song title can now get a first-grade concert cancelled.

The one that became a promise
true colors (acoustic cover ft. Kenton Chen)
stories · Cyndi Lauper cover

Lauper’s 1986 song, covered here small and close. She has spent decades since attaching it to actual provision for queer young people with nowhere to sleep, which is what turns a metaphor into an argument.

Addressed to somebody having a bad year
Rainbow
Meghan Trainor

Pop consolation, unembarrassed. The rack needs at least one song that simply tells you it gets better without hedging.

The metaphor stated flatly
I Am a Rainbow
Dolly Parton

Written in the 1980s, released decades later on a children’s record. Parton takes the whole spectrum as a description of one person’s moods and refuses to rank any of them — the thesis of No. 70 in a nursery register.

The one that got banned
Rainbowland
Miley Cyrus feat. Dolly Parton

A duet about a world where everybody is allowed. In March 2023 a Wisconsin district pulled it from a first-grade concert as potentially controversial under a policy on classroom controversy — and the song chosen to replace it was “Rainbow Connection,” which was initially banned too. See section seven. Also carded on the Dolly rack — the second of two songs this rack shares with the shelf.

After the storm, not instead of it
Rainbow
Kacey Musgraves

The closing track of Golden Hour, and careful in a way the genre usually is not: it does not say the weather was fine. It says the rainbow is over you now and you are still holding an umbrella.

Written during a legal fight
Rainbow
Kesha

Made while she was in litigation with her producer. The rainbow here is not decorative — it is what she chose to name the record and the recovery, and the timing is the context.

The rainbow as a person
She’s A Rainbow
The Rolling Stones

Their Satanic Majesties Request, 1967. A fourth unlabelled link; oEmbed returned the Stones. The conceit — a person who comes in colours — is where much of the later identity writing on this rack ultimately comes from.

Taken to the bluegrass side
She’s A Rainbow (Rolling Stones cover)
Molly Tuttle

Tuttle is a flatpicker of ferocious precision, and moving the song onto acoustic strings exposes how much of it was always melody rather than production.

Two songs at once, live
“She’s A Rainbow” x “Over The Line”
Molly Tuttle

A live splice of the cover with one of her own — worth having beside the studio version for the same reason the IZ medley is: musicians keep noticing these songs want to be joined to other songs.

Seven — Skepticism and wonder, back to back

The two modes, one after the other, with no attempt to reconcile them. That is the method.

The skepticism
Rainbow Stew
Merle Haggard

1981, and the sourest rainbow song here. Haggard lists the utopia he is being promised — free energy, free everything — and does not believe a word of it. On a rack about wonder this is the necessary corrective: somebody is usually selling something.

The wonder
Yosemitebear Mountain Double Rainbow
Paul “Yosemitebear” Vasquez · 2010

A man alone with a camcorder, undone by the sky, asking what it means. Mocked for a decade, and simply correct: he is looking at two arcs made by one drop of water and one beam of light, and his is the appropriate response. “It’s a double rainbow all the way” has been our phrase ever since.
