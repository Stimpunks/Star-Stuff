Rainbow Serpents

Entropy increases in an isolated system. Not a closed one, and not an open one — and the qualifier keeps falling off. On a law applied where it does not belong, three of our own pieces that walked up to that line and stopped, and three infinities that are not the same infinity.

You’re never too far from a snake.

— Tyson Yunkaporta, Snake Talk

This essay is about a qualifier. Six words long, taught in every thermodynamics course, and almost always dropped by the time the idea reaches daylight. Dropping it produces a picture of the universe as a thing running down — and that picture has been doing work far outside physics for a very long time.

The person who put it to us most sharply is Tyson Yunkaporta, of the Apalech clan, western Cape York, a Wik Mungkan speaker who writes about knowledge systems. His charge is not that our physics is wrong. It is that we took a true law and sang it somewhere it does not belong, and that the consequences have been enormous.

Two things this essay will not do. It will not explain the Rainbow Serpent, which is not one being, not one nation’s, and not ours to describe. And it will not claim that Aboriginal Law is validated by thermodynamics — that would make our instruments the judge of somebody else’s jurisprudence, which is the argument in Already an Entity (No. 81) and we are not going to undo it a week later. What it will do is take a criticism of our physics, from outside our discipline, and check it.

Three times we walked up to this and stopped

Before anything else, the confession, because it is what gives this essay its subject.

The Elimination of Waste (No. 43) follows the word waste from Carnot’s heat engine into scientific management and then into the school. On its last spread it stops dead and argues against itself: “you are not a heat engine. The second law governs closed systems and cyclic processes; a person is neither. Fatigue is not waste heat, spoons are not joules.” It marks that step as its leap and declines to take it.

The Noise Floor (No. 56) uses the same law in the other direction — Nyquist deduced the thermal noise floor from the second law, because a silent resistor would be a perpetual-motion machine. The hiss is not a shortfall. It is required.

Your Shadow Is the Centre (No. 69) establishes that a rainbow is a cone of directions with its apex in your own eye: there are as many bows as there are observers, and Roger Bacon reasoned his way to it around 1267 with an astrolabe and a measured 42°.

Three pieces, three edges of one argument, and each of them stops just short of it.

Yunkaporta does not stop. He puts the three together and says the arrangement is a curse.

The qualifier, stated properly

Thermodynamics distinguishes three kinds of system, and the distinction is not pedantry — it decides which sentence is true.

SystemExchanges with its surroundingsWhat the second law says about its entropy
Isolatedneither matter nor energytends to a maximum, and does not decrease
Closedenergy but not mattercan decrease, given work or energy input
Openboth matter and energycan decrease, while entropy production inside stays non-negative

The familiar sentence — entropy always increases — is true of the first row only. Conflating closed with isolated is a well-documented teaching error, and it is the one that matters here, because almost nothing anybody cares about is isolated.

And we made the same slip

Read the No. 43 sentence again: “the second law governs closed systems and cyclic processes.” The precise word is isolated. Yunkaporta writes closed too. So does a great deal of physics writing.

The argument survives the imprecision in both cases — a person is neither isolated nor closed, and neither is the Earth. But we are not in a position to clarify anybody’s terminology without fixing our own in the same breath, so: ours was loose, and this is the correction.

What happens in an open system is the part that gets lost. The law is not suspended out here. It is restated: the entropy a system produces internally is never negative, but a system can export entropy to its surroundings faster than it makes it, and its own entropy can fall. That is not a loophole and it is not an exception.

It is how anything alive works. A seed does it. A forest does it. You did it this morning.

The Earth is not isolated. It receives a stream of low-entropy sunlight and radiates high-entropy infrared to space, and it is that gradient — not any exemption from physics — that pays for every ordered thing on the planet.

The tellSomebody says “everything runs down — second law” about a thing that is plainly exchanging matter and energy with its surroundings. Ask which system they mean, and where its boundary is.

A true law, sung where it does not belong

Here is the argument as Yunkaporta makes it. It is his, it is in print, and we are reporting rather than extending it.

He describes how a curse works in an Aboriginal worldview: you take part of a system, observe the pattern of the whole in that fragment, “then you sing a false pattern… into the whole from the part, powering it with another system in constant motion.” And then:

The curse is a deception made real—either an outright lie or a true law or pattern applied where it doesn’t belong. It is like a computer virus, a sneaky line of code that ends up crashing the whole system. The application of the second law of thermodynamics to open, interconnected systems does the same thing. It is a curse.Tyson Yunkaporta, Sand Talk (2019), pp. 48–49

Note the precision of the definition. A curse is not superstition here and it is not a lie, necessarily. It is a true pattern, correctly observed, deliberately applied outside its domain, and powered by something that keeps moving. That is a description of a category error with a delivery mechanism — and as a description of what happened to the second law between the textbook and the culture, it is uncomfortably exact.

He goes further, and this is the part that is his interpretation rather than a physics claim: that the appetite for closed systems is why the built world is so full of “fences and walls, borders, great divides, and great barriers.” If your model of time requires a closed system, you will find yourself manufacturing them.

We are not in a position to adjudicate that. We can say that the physics half is right, that our own No. 43 half-said it and stopped, and that the question he ends on is a fair one to leave standing:

In reality we do not inhabit closed systems, so why choose the second law of thermodynamics to create your model of time?Yunkaporta, Sand Talk, p. 47

A club that says two things depending on how you hold it

The argument has an object attached to it, and the object is the best short explanation of it.

Yunkaporta carved a club. Around its head he etched an ouroboros — the snake swallowing its own tail — to stand for the second law and a world that must end. But the club is three-dimensional, and when you roll it across clay the etching releases something the flat image could not hold: “an endless procession of snakes, head to tail.”

One carving. Rolled, it prints a line with no last snake in it.

The ouroboros closes because something is being consumed. The procession does not close, and nothing is eaten.

That is the first law and the second law rendered as a tool you can hold, and it is worth saying plainly that this is a better teaching object than most of the diagrams in a physics textbook. It also carries his sharpest line about the figure itself, from earlier in the same book: the ouroboros was supposed to be a metaphor for infinity, “However, it contained the same curse, the same contradiction: How can this serpent be a symbol of infinity if it will eventually eat itself?”

The bow was always a circle

One piece of pure physics, which belongs to nobody, and which this site has already worked over twice.

A rainbow is not an arc. It is a cone of directions, roughly 42° from the antisolar point, with its apex at your eye. You see an arc because the ground cuts the cone. From an aircraft, with nothing in the way, the same bow is a complete circle — and it always was.

Which means the bow moves when you move, and two people standing on two hills are not looking at one rainbow from two angles. They are looking at two rainbows. Yunkaporta makes exactly this point, and reaches the same conclusion an optics textbook does:

When we see that arc in the sky… we are only seeing one part of it, and it is subjective, just for us. If we move the rainbow also moves, only appearing in relation to our standpoint. A man on the next hill will see it in a different position from where we are seeing it.Yunkaporta, “A rainbow serpent theory of time,” Garland Magazine

We are not claiming a convergence here, and we are certainly not claiming that physics confirms anything. It is one fact about light, available to anyone who looks, and two traditions noticed it. That is what a fact about light is like.

The full argument, with Bacon’s astrolabe and the reason there are as many rainbows as there are observers, is Your Shadow Is the Centre. The catalogue of every other kind of bow is A Field Guide to Rainbows.

Not one serpent

This is the section where the essay stops.

The Rainbow Serpent is not a single figure with regional variants, and treating it as one is the specific error this site has undertaken not to make. There are many beings, many nations, many names, and some of the knowledge is restricted. Yunkaporta says so himself, of the serpent he is describing: “There are infinite variations of him in all shapes and sizes throughout the world—wyrm, dragon, uraeus, and many different names in different regions, taking the shape of the spirit of those places.”

So: quoted, credited, and not explained. We are not going to summarise a Law we do not hold, or produce the tidy paragraph that would let a reader think they now know what the Rainbow Serpent is.

Why the restraint, specifically

Not squeamishness. The Rainbow Serpent has one of the heaviest histories of commodification in Australian art — reproduced on tea towels and airport canvases for decades, usually without a nation attached and usually without payment. A settler-run project adding one more explanation to that pile is not a contribution.

What we can do instead is the boring thing: cite the person, name the clan, quote rather than paraphrase, and link out.

The dragon that is not a serpent

“The Autism Infinity Dragon” by Kaya Oldaker, released by the artist into the public domain: “This is the autism symbol as a dragon. I did this to represent the influence my autism has on my art. This is a public domain drawing and anyone can use it for any reason.” — Kaya’s Kosmos · kayaskosmos.com. Credited here because attribution is courtesy even where it is not required.

This is not a Rainbow Serpent, it is not descended from one, and it is not in conversation with one. It is the autistic community’s own infinity symbol, drawn as a dragon by an autistic artist for whom dragons are a long-standing special interest. Kaya Oldaker also drew Esmx, the Stimpunks mascot.

It belongs in this essay for a reason that is about geometry, and that you can check by looking at the picture.

The head does not take the tail. Look at the crossing point: the mouth is open, and the violet plume of the tail passes beneath and past it. This is a lemniscate — a single continuous curve that crosses itself and returns, with no end and nothing consumed. It is not an ouroboros. An ouroboros closes by eating.

The ouroboros is a closed system. The infinity loop is an open one. The difference is whether anything has to be eaten for the figure to continue.

We are not going to claim Kaya drew a thermodynamics diagram. She drew a dragon, for a reason she has stated, and it is a better dragon than the argument needs. But the autistic community chose an unclosed figure for itself, and the distinction between the figure that closes and the figure that does not is exactly the distinction on the club. Those two things rhyme, and rhyming is not the same as descent.

A deficit assessment is a closed-system model of a person

Here is the one claim in this essay that is ours, about us, made with our own symbol, and routed nowhere near anybody’s Law.

An assessment measures a person alone. It scores them against a standard, in a room, on a day, with the environment held constant and unrecorded. Whatever comes out is treated as a property of the person — something they have, or lack, or are losing.

That is an isolated-system model, and people are not isolated systems. The support in the room, the noise, the lighting, whether anyone believed you last time, whether you are allowed to move — all of these cross the boundary constantly, and none of them appear in the score. When the number comes back low, the model has nowhere to write down that half the apparatus was outside the box.

Nothing in thermodynamics licenses that observation. The physics does not say how anybody should be treated, here or anywhere — that argument is A Promise, Not a Finding, and it holds. Down to is not because of.

What the physics does is take away an excuse. If you are going to model a person as a system, you have to say which kind, and if you say isolated you are wrong before you have measured anything.

What this essay is not

  • Not an explanation of the Rainbow Serpent. Quoted from a named author of a named clan, and left there. It is not one being and we do not hold it.
  • Not Aboriginal Law validated by physics. The traffic here runs the other way: a criticism of our physics, arriving from outside our discipline, which we checked and found correct. See No. 81 for why the reverse would be a colonial move with better manners.
  • Not a claim that the second law is wrong or optional. It is exactly right, in its domain, and No. 56 is built on it holding. Entropy production in an open system is still never negative.
  • Not “the second law proves rest is mandatory.” No. 43 refused that on its own last spread and we are not un-refusing it. You are not a heat engine, and spoons are not joules.
  • Not a lineage. The ouroboros, the Rainbow Serpent and the autism infinity loop are three different things that happen to be snakes bent into curves. They rhyme. Rhyming is not descent, and noticing a rhyme is not discovering a connection.
  • Not ancient wisdom. Yunkaporta is a working academic who published a book in 2019 and an essay in a magazine. Filing living argument under timeless folklore is another way of filing it under not-current.
