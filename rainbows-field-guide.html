A Field Guide to Rainbows

Sixteen rainbows, sorted by what changed — the drop, the path inside it, the light arriving, the surface it lands on, the instrument at the point of the cone. Every listicle sorts these by how they look and then ranks them by how rare they are. Both sorts fail, and they fail in the same direction: rarity is a fact about where you were standing.

L★S
How to use this guide Each entry gives a bow, what is physically different about it, how the popular telling misreads it ("sounds like / actually"), and full field notes with sources. Click any entry to open its notes.
Where this came from The companion to two zines rather than one. Your Shadow Is the Centre (No. 69) establishes that a rainbow is a cone with its apex in your own pupil, and Both Arcs, One Drop (No. 70) argues that a double rainbow is one drop doing one thing, with neither arc secondary. Between them they take a single bow apart. This guide asks the next question: if the mechanism is one mechanism, what are all these "kinds"? Both zines' own subjects are handed back to them rather than told twice.
Why the last three are dashed Because the apparatus stops there. Sixteen entries get a reframe table because a confident popular telling misreads a bow. The ice halos get none — they are not rainbows, and correcting a listicle is not the same as correcting a phenomenon. Neither do the two arguments we are handing to the pieces that make them properly.

Five things that change

The sort is ours, and it came out of the entries rather than ahead of them. We started meaning to sort the way the genre does — by how the bow looks, from the common ones to the rare ones — and that sort collapsed almost immediately, twice over. It collapsed once because a fogbow and a rainbow look nothing alike and differ only in the size of the drop, while a sea spray bow and a rainbow look identical and differ in the chemistry of the water: appearance sorts them exactly wrong. It collapsed again because "rare" turned out not to be a property of any bow at all. A circumhorizontal arc needs the sun above 58°, which happens for about 140 hours a year in London and about 670 in Los Angeles, and never at all north of 55°N. What actually varies is one parameter at a time, and the physics is the same physics throughout. So the sections below run from changes to the drop, through changes to the light's path inside it, to changes in the light arriving, the surface the cone meets, and the observer standing at the cone's point. None of these ranks above another, which is the whole reason it is a catalogue and not a countdown.

One · The drop

When the water is a different water

Five bows that differ from the everyday one in nothing but the drop: how big it is, what shape the fall has beaten it into, and — in the last card — whether it is water at all. Two of these look nothing like a rainbow and one is indistinguishable from one, which is the first sign that sorting by appearance was never going to work.

Two · The path inside

When the light takes a longer way round

The tidy schoolroom rainbow — in one face, off the back, out again — counts the bounces, and this section is what happens as you keep counting. Two gives the famous double, one drop making both arcs at once. Three and four put bows on the wrong side of the sky, where they waited until 2011 for a photograph. Then the counting stops working: one entry here is a bow geometric optics cannot produce at all and never could, and the last is still not fully explained.

Three · The light

When what arrives has already been changed

The shortest section, and the count is information rather than an omission: the drop has far more ways to vary than the light does. Both entries here are the same move — something about the beam was settled before it ever reached the water. In the first, the atmosphere edited it on the way in. In the second, the limit was built into the lamp, and it applies to every other bow in this guide.

Four · The surface

When the cone meets something

A rainbow is not an arc. It is a cone of directions with your eye at the point, and arc is only what you get when the ground cuts it. Change what the cone meets and the shape changes to a circle, or a hyperbola, or an ellipse — none of which is the rainbow's shape, and all of which get named as though they were.

Five · The observer

The instrument at the point of the cone

The other four sections change something in the sky. This one changes the instrument receiving it, and both entries make the same correction: a bow reported as lacking something is not necessarily a bow that lacks anything. A moonbow carries every colour to an eye that cannot report colour that dim. A polarising lens can delete a rainbow outright. That a rainbow is anchored to your own shadow in the first place is not carded here — it is the whole subject of No. 69, and it gets a dashed card below instead of a worse second telling.

And · Not carded

Three things we are not going to reframe

These three appear in almost every list of rainbow kinds. They get a card here because leaving them out would look like an oversight — and a dashed one with no reframe table, because the table exists to correct a misreading of a bow, and none of these three is a bow we have misread. One is not a rainbow at all. The other two are arguments rather than mechanisms, and both are made in full elsewhere. The double rainbow was briefly filed here and has been moved into section two, because its mechanism is exactly the kind of thing a catalogue is for — and the handoff survives inside the card.

Not: Not permission from nature. The sky does not sort by conditions in order to instruct us, and nothing here licenses anything about how anybody lives. A universe that could approve could also decline, and we would not take the verdict either way. Down to is not because of. Not a ranking. Five things that change, not five rungs, and no rarity countdown. A fogbow is not a rainbow that failed to develop colours, and a moonbow is not a dim rainbow — each is the same mechanism met under different conditions, and the conditions are not scored. Not the word illusion, and this one is worth being exact about. Nearly every popular account of the observer-anchored cone reaches for "the ultimate optical illusion," and it is the wrong word. An illusion is a mistake perception makes. A rainbow is occurring — calculable to a fraction of a degree, photographable, about 96% polarised, measurable by instruments with no perception at all. Unique to each observer is not the same as unreal, and collapsing the two hands away the whole argument to anyone who wants to call a private experience a false one. Roger Bacon went the other way in about 1267 and reasoned from the un-shareability to the mechanism — that is No. 69 — and the word itself is argued on Skepticism and Wonder. Not a claim that everything is relative. The sort here is a sort by conditions, and conditions are objective: drop diameter, refractive index, solar elevation, retinal thresholds. Two observers see different rainbows for reasons that are entirely public and entirely calculable. This is the opposite of "everybody has their own truth." Not a metaphor for us. No bow here is Autistic, and none stands in for a person. The rhyme between "sorted by conditions rather than by deviance" and how we would like to be met is a rhyme, noticed after the physics rather than imposed on it, and a rhyme is not a proof. Not settled. The glory is the honest one: after van de Hulst's 1947 surface-wave account the consensus holds, but the precise mechanism is still described in the literature as obscure. The twinned-bow explanation is simulation-led. And the third- and fourth-order photographs are a handful of confirmed images, not a survey.
Star stuff, bending light

Every drop in this guide is doing one thing: slowing light down, and slowing the short wavelengths a little more than the long ones. The oxygen in the water was forged in dying stars, the hydrogen has been here since the first few minutes of everything, and the calcium in the eye reading the result came from the same furnaces. The light is old and the water is old and the arrangement is about four milliseconds old.

What the sixteen have in common is not appearance. It is that none of them is a deviation. There is no master rainbow that these fall short of — the everyday one is simply the set of conditions we happen to stand in most often, and the moment you move the drop size, or the liquid, or the receptor, the same rule produces something the genre has to give a separate name and a rarity score. The naming is ours. The rule never changed.

There is no standard rainbow. And there is no standard place to stand, which is the same sentence said from the other end — and the one this guide hands to Your Shadow Is the Centre (No. 69) rather than argue twice. The argument about a single bow having two arcs is in Both Arcs, One Drop (No. 70). This guide is the catalogue for both.

Figures follow the primary and authoritative sources, and where we did not reach the primary this note says so. Fogbows — the broadening is diffraction, and it runs on a slope rather than a switch: cloud and fog droplets rarely exceed 100 µm, a fogbow requires droplets around 0.05 mm and below, the bow narrows and regains colour at the edges as droplet diameter rises, and drops of roughly 100–500 µm — waterfall spray — give intermediate forms. The primary fogbow radius is given as a range, about 30–45°, because it varies with droplet size; printing one figure would contradict the card. Taken from Les Cowley's Atmospheric Optics (atoptics.co.uk), which is an expert secondary source and is treated as one. Cloudbows — scattering angles of approximately 135°–165°; the polarised cloudbow as a remote-sensing signal for droplet size is an established technique, cited rather than read in full. Sea spray bows — dissolved salt raises seawater's refractive index above rainwater's, giving a radius roughly 0.8° smaller, with both bows reported and photographed standing side by side and visibly misaligned. The 0.8° figure is for typical salinity and is not a constant — it varies with salinity, and the card says so. From Atmospheric Optics. Twinned rainbows — small drops are held near-spherical by surface tension while large drops are flattened by air resistance and may oscillate; ray tracing shows departures from sphericity of 1–2% noticeably distort the primary; the reconstruction is a mixture of spherical drops with oblate spheroids shortened by about 2.5%, i.e. two showers through one cone. Drop shapes are modelled as two conjoined half-spheroids of differing oblateness, with Debye-series work on polydisperse distributions showing that anomalous drop-size distributions can twin the primary, secondary, tertiary and quinary bows at certain solar elevations. This account is simulation-led and reconstruction-led rather than settled by direct measurement of drops in a photographed twinned bow, and is presented as such. Methane bows on Titan — liquid methane's refractive index of about 1.29 against water's ~1.33 gives a primary radius of at least 49°, same colour order, with an orange cast from the sky; visible bows are expected to be rare because of haze, while the atmosphere is substantially clearer in the infrared, making infrared bows more likely. No methane rainbow has been observed; this is a calculation, and the card says so. Supernumerary arcs — geometric optics is totally unable to explain them; the first explanation is Thomas Young, 1804, by interference between two geometrical rays leaving the drop at the same scattering angle, and the arcs were historically important as evidence for the wave theory of light in the early 1800s. George Airy (1838) dismissed Young's account as “the imperfect theory of interference” because it predicted the arcs at the wrong angles; Young had not accounted for the 90° phase shift a ray suffers at a focal line, and with that shift included the interference theory is surprisingly accurate. The modern reassessment is Lock, J. A. & Adams, C. L. (and related work), “Supernumerary arcs of rainbows: Young's theory of interference,” Applied Optics 56(19), G104 (2017), which is also the source for Airy's success having overshadowed Young's contribution; read at abstract and summary level. Third- and fourth-order rainbows — both stand on the sun's side of the sky, at roughly 40° and 45° from the sun, and both were first photographed in 2011: Großmann, M., Schmidt, E. & Haußmann, A., “Photographic evidence for the third-order rainbow,” Applied Optics 50(28), F134–F141 (2011), and Theusner, M., “Photographic observation of a natural fourth-order rainbow,” Applied Optics 50(28), F129 (2011). These are a small number of confirmed images obtained by specialists under heavy contrast processing, not a survey; the phrase used in the literature is “photographic evidence,” and we keep it. The correction that a photographed “triple rainbow” is nearly always primary + secondary + reflection bow is ours, and follows from the geometry. Glories — Mie theory generates full-colour simulations but offers no explanation; Debye-series analysis indicates one internal reflection; van de Hulst proposed in 1947 that backscattering arises from surface waves producing a toroidal wavefront, and that remains the consensus — but the standard statement of it is that the precise mechanism remains rather obscure, with alternative accounts including two-ray interference between surface waves still offered. Following Laven, P., “How are glories formed?”, Applied Optics 44(27), 5675 (2005) and the surrounding Atmospheric Optics material. This is the one entry here whose mechanism is openly unfinished, and it is carded rather than smoothed. Moonbows — moonlight is too faint to excite the cone receptors, so vision falls to the rods, which give greyscale; all spectral colours are present and do appear in long-exposure photographs. Given as well-established visual science and atmospheric optics rather than from a paper read for this guide. Red bows — at low solar elevation the long slant path removes the short wavelengths by Rayleigh scattering before the beam reaches the drops; the dispersion is unimpaired and operates on an already-narrowed spectrum. Given as well-established atmospheric optics. Dewbows — the light still arrives from a circular set of directions 42° from the antisolar point, and the pattern traced on flat ground is the intersection of that cone with a plane: a hyperbola at low sun, an ellipse at high sun. The card keeps both halves — the conic section is a fact about the grass, the circle is a fact about the light — because sources that give only one of them are the reason the effect is routinely described as a distorted rainbow. From Atmospheric Optics. Full-circle bows — the bow is always a full circle centred on the antisolar point, which lies below the horizon whenever the sun is above it. Reflection bows — a smooth water surface behind the observer reflects sunlight upward, acting as a second source with its own antisolar point above the horizon; the resulting arc shares the primary's feet and rises more steeply. Distinct from a reflected bow, which is a mirror image; the two names are routinely swapped. Polarisation — close to the rainbow singularity the primary is about 96% s-polarised, because the internal reflection occurs near Brewster's angle: for water at n = 1.335 (green light) Brewster is 37° while the Descartes ray reflects at 40°, where the ratio of p to s reflectivities is about 0.03. Polarisation is radial and tangential around the arc, and a polarising filter can accordingly suppress or enhance it. Following the Europhysics News rainbow review (2006), HyperPhysics, and Atmospheric Optics. The sun’s angular width — the solar disc is about 0.5° across, so simulations run with a point source at infinity give sharper, more saturated bands than any real bow; the finite disc broadens the peaks, desaturates the colours and shifts them by about half a solar diameter, with the primary running roughly 1.9° peak to peak. Taken from standard treatments of rainbow scattering at summary level rather than recomputed. Ice halos — the circumhorizontal arc forms in hexagonal plate crystals in cirrus and requires the sun above 58°, which makes it impossible north of 55°N or south of 55°S; the sun exceeds 58° for roughly 140 hours a year in London against roughly 670 in Los Angeles. Those two figures carry the guide's argument about rarity and are the numbers to check hardest; they are quoted from the halo literature via Atmospheric Optics and Wikipedia's circumhorizontal arc entry, which agree, and we did not reach a primary computation. “Fire rainbow” is a popular name and not a description. Held honestly: the sort into five changing parameters is ours, arrived at after the entries and not before them — sorting by appearance was tried first and failed twice in opposite directions, once on the fogbow and once on the sea spray bow, which is the argument the gates band makes on the page. Several cards rest on Atmospheric Optics, an expert secondary source rather than a primary, and it is named as such rather than laundered into a citation. The generation-scale claim that a rainbow is remade every few milliseconds is stated qualitatively rather than with a figure, because we did not verify a rate. The double rainbow — one internal reflection leaves the drop at about 42.7° from the antisolar point and two at about 50°, with the colour order reversed so the reds face each other; the secondary is 43% as bright as the primary; and neither bow returns light into the range between 42.7° and 50°, which is why the sky there is darker. All four figures follow Stull, R., Practical Meteorology, and are the same ones printed in No. 70 — deliberately so, because a catalogue and its zine disagreeing about a number is worse than either being wrong alone. Alexander’s dark band is named for Alexander of Aphrodisias, who recorded the darkening around 200 CE. This card was briefly filed as a withheld hand-off and was moved into section two on Ryan’s call: a field guide is a handoff, and withholding the mechanism cost the catalogue its most useful card while saving nothing — the argument the card declines to take, that secondary is a rank the physics does not contain, is still No. 70’s, and the card says so on its face. The antisolar cone — that the bow is centred on the shadow of your own head, that there are as many bows as there are observers, and that Roger Bacon established it from an astrolabe and a measured 42° in the Opus Majus around 1267 with no theory of refraction at all — is likewise not an entry, because it is the entire subject of Your Shadow Is the Centre (No. 69), including the turn that Bacon reasoned from the un-shareability to the mechanism. Two entries here depend on that geometry and cite it rather than retell it. The cultural rainbow — the flag, the song, and the 2023 Wisconsin school-district decision — is deliberately not an entry either, and lives at Skepticism and Wonder, which is also where the argument that a rainbow is not an illusion is set out in full. Companion to Your Shadow Is the Centre (No. 69) and Both Arcs, One Drop (No. 70). There is no standard rainbow.
