A Field Guide to
Resting

Fifteen ways of resting among our Terran kin, sorted by what is doing the resting — and the catalogue does not converge. There is no standard way to rest. Not a standard amount, not a standard posture, not a standard organ, and in one case not even a standard measurement. An animal with no brain rests on a four-hour cycle with no day to answer to. A bird goes ten months without landing. A squirrel holds a body temperature below freezing. A seal skips weeks of REM and never pays it back. Lying still at night is an option. It was never the standard.

Sponges and plants get none: for one, nobody knows yet, and saying actually about an open question is bluff rather than rigour; for the other, the misnaming was never a verdict passed on the organism.

Five things that do the resting

The sort is ours, and it came out of the entries rather than ahead of them. We began meaning to sort by how much, and that collapsed on contact with the literature — the numbers turn out to be the least reliable thing on the page, which is now an entry rather than an axis. We tried sorting by taxon, and it put a jellyfish and a nematode in different rooms for no reason that mattered. What varies cleanly is what carries the rest: a body with no centre to switch off, a body that cannot stop moving, a body shut down for a season, a body whose quantity we have mostly guessed at, and a body being held by something outside itself. None of these ranks above another, which is the whole reason it is a catalogue and not a ladder.

One · Before there was a brain

Nothing here has a centre to switch off

Three animals that rest without the organ the word usually assumes. Two of them have a nerve net and no brain at all; the third has 302 neurons and rests on a schedule set by its own development. All three meet the behavioural definition of sleep — quiescence, a raised arousal threshold, and a rebound afterwards if you take it away — which is the criterion that separates sleep from merely being still. The oldest resting animal we can point to has no ledger, and no clock either.

Two · Without stopping

Rest is not the opposite of motion

Three animals for whom stillness was never available, and who rest anyway — one hemisphere at a time, or in the glide between wingbeats, or hanging vertically in open water. The frigatebird and the swift both break the assumption that rest requires the motion to stop; the swift also breaks the assumption that a record is uniform, because some individuals in the same study never landed and others roosted for whole nights.

Three · For a season

Months, and nothing is lost by it

Three animals that rest for a duration no one asks them to justify — and in each case the interesting part is what the body does not pay. A bear goes half a year without weight-bearing movement and keeps its skeleton. A squirrel holds a core temperature below freezing and thaws unharmed. A fish seals itself in a cocoon of its own mucus and waits out a drought that may last years. Long rest is not intrinsically costly. It is costly in bodies that are not arranged for it.

Four · The amount nobody agrees on

The number is the shakiest part

Three animals where the quantity of rest is the thing in question — and the middle card is the one this guide would keep if it could keep only one. The famous twenty-hour bat is a single 1960s measurement at a single ambient temperature, on an unknown number of animals, and it falls to eleven hours in a cooler room. Roughly one per cent of mammal species have had their sleep measured at all. So when a table tells you what an animal's sleep is, the honest reading is often this is what somebody once recorded, once, in a box.

Five · Held by something

The resting is done by the arrangement

Three animals whose rest is not a capacity they have but a condition they enter — and in all three the work is done outside the body. A huddle reaches blood temperature while the air outside is nineteen below. A fish builds a mucus tent that costs it two and a half per cent of a day's energy and stops almost every parasite. An otter ties itself to the forest so that sleeping does not mean drifting. Give a body something to be held by and rest stops being a discipline.

Where the apparatus stops

Two cards with nothing to correct

Every card above this one carries three columns: a trait, what it sounds like, and what is actually the case. That table exists because a verdict had been reached about an animal and the verdict was wrong. These two have no verdict to overturn. One because nobody knows yet, and saying actually about an open question is not rigour but bluff. One because the misnaming was never a judgement passed on the organism — it was a convenience of ours, and the two people most associated with it both flagged it themselves. So the table is absent, and the absence is the argument.

What this guide refuses

Not a league table. There is no winner here. The elephant at two hours is not more efficient than the bat, and the bear is not more disciplined than the swift. Not a set of instructions. None of these animals is a model for a person, and the resemblances are rhymes rather than proofs. Not rest as maintenance. Nothing here is recharging in order to work better tomorrow. Hydra is not resting so it can divide on schedule; the division is what the resting is for, which is not the same thing. Not the tidy numbers. Where a figure is one old measurement in a warm box, this guide says so instead of printing it.

Held honestly

The Hydra and Cassiopea states are called sleep-like by the people who found them, and that hedge is kept — what is established is that they meet the behavioural criteria, not that a polyp experiences anything. The lungfish cocoon is living tissue (Science Advances, 2021) and whether it is antimicrobial is contested by a 2024 preprint that has not been reviewed; we name the challenge and lean on neither. The sperm whale's drift dives are a resting posture with both eyes closed and near-total unresponsiveness — whether that is bihemispheric sleep is suggested, not settled. And the widely circulated claim that sperm whales use exhaled bubbles as ballast during these dives appears only in content farms, so it is not here.

Figures follow the primary literature, and where a figure would not stand up it is described rather than printed. Hydra — Kanaya, H. J., Park, S., Kim, J.-H. and colleagues, "A sleep-like state in Hydra unravels conserved sleep mechanisms during the evolutionary development of the central nervous system," Science Advances 6(41), eabb9415 (2020): a nerve net and no brain; quiescence, an elevated arousal threshold, homeostatic rebound and shortened sleep latency after deprivation; melatonin, GABA and PRKG1 effects conserved; mechanical and pharmacological deprivation markedly reduced proliferating cells; and no free-running circadian rhythm — under constant conditions the daily pattern disappears and a roughly four-hour ultradian rhythm remains. The authors' own hedge, sleep-like state, is kept. Cassiopea — Nath, R. D., Bedbrook, C. N., Abrams, M. J. and colleagues, "The jellyfish Cassiopea exhibits a sleep-like state," Current Biology 27(19), 2984–2990 (2017): the first animal without a centralised nervous system shown to have a sleep state, on all three behavioural criteria. C. elegans — Raizen, D. M. and colleagues, "Lethargus is a Caenorhabditis elegans sleep-like state," Nature 451, 569–572 (2008): a two-to-three-hour quiescent period before each of four moults, with reversibility, reduced responsiveness and homeostasis, regulated by the cGMP-dependent protein kinase gene egl-4. Great frigatebird — Rattenborg, N. C., Voirin, B., Cruz, S. M. and colleagues, "Evidence that birds sleep in mid-flight," Nature Communications 7, 12468 (2016): flights up to ten days and 3,000 km, a mean of 42 minutes of sleep per day aloft against over twelve hours ashore, slow-wave bouts of up to several minutes, more often unihemispheric in flight than on land, with bihemispheric episodes also recorded — so half a brain awake is not a requirement for staying up. The two-month aerial figure is a different study on the same species: Weimerskirch, H. and colleagues, "Frigate birds track atmospheric conditions over months-long transoceanic flights," Science 353(6294), 74–78 (2016). That sleep occurred while soaring and never during flapping is from Rattenborg, N. C., van der Meij, J., Beckers, G. J. L. & Lesku, J. A., "Local aspects of avian non-REM and REM sleep," Frontiers in Neuroscience 13, 567 (2019) — the same lead author reviewing his own data. Common swift — Hedenström, A. and colleagues, "Annual 10-month aerial life phase in the common swift Apus apus," Current Biology 26(22), 3066–3070 (2016): thirteen birds, some followed two years running, spending more than 99 per cent of the ten-month non-breeding period airborne — and the record is not uniform, since some individuals roosted briefly or for whole nights in midwinter while others did not land at all. Sperm whale — Miller, P. J. O., Aoki, K., Rendell, L. E. & Amano, M., "Stereotypical resting behaviour of the sperm whale," Current Biology 18(1), R21–R23 (2008): 59 whales tagged; passive shallow drift dives in a stereotyped vertical posture occupying under about a tenth of the time, with both eyes closed and a group unresponsive to a closely passing vessel until it inadvertently touched them. Whether this is bihemispheric sleep is suggested by the authors, not established, and it is given that way here. The circulating claim that sperm whales use exhaled bubbles as ballast during these dives traces only to content farms and is not carried. American black bear — Tøien, Ø., Blake, J., Edgar, D. M. and colleagues, "Hibernation in black bears: independence of metabolic suppression from body temperature," Science 331(6019), 906–909 (2011): metabolism suppressed to about 25 per cent of basal while core temperature is regulated between roughly 30 and 36 °C in multi-day cycles, and heart rate falls from 55 to as few as 9 beats per minute. The skeleton: McGee-Lawrence, M. E., Wojda, S. J., Barlow, L. N. and colleagues, "Grizzly bears (Ursus arctos horribilis) and black bears (Ursus americanus) prevent trabecular bone loss during disuse (hibernation)," Bone 45(6), 1186–1191 (2009): no difference in bone volume fraction or tissue mineral density between hibernating and active bears, with cellular activity indices falling while resorption and formation stay balanced. Arctic ground squirrel — Barnes, B. M., "Freeze avoidance in a mammal: body temperatures below 0 °C in an arctic hibernator," Science 244(4912), 1593–1595 (1989): twelve animals with implanted temperature-sensitive transmitters hibernating in outdoor enclosures near Fairbanks, adopting and spontaneously arousing from core temperatures as low as −2.9 °C, with February minima averaging −1.9 °C in the body against −6 °C in the soil, and plasma showing normal solute concentrations and no antifreeze molecules — supercooling rather than chemistry. African lungfish — aestivation in a cocoon of its own hardened mucus, with feeding and excretion ceasing, oxygen uptake via the lungs alone and heart rate falling from about 25 to roughly 2 beats per minute; durations of years are reported, up to about four. That the cocoon is living tissue is Heimroth, R. D. and colleagues, "The lungfish cocoon is a living tissue with antimicrobial functions," Science Advances 7(34), eabj0829 (2021) — and the antimicrobial half of that claim is contested by a 2024 bioRxiv preprint that has not been through review, which we flag and lean on neither way. African elephant — Gravett, N., Bhagwandin, A., Sutcliffe, R. and colleagues, "Inactivity/sleep in two wild free-roaming African elephant matriarchs — does large body size make elephants the shortest mammalian sleepers?", PLOS ONE 12(3), e0171903 (2017): two matriarchs in Chobe National Park over 35 days, with an actiwatch implanted in the trunk and a collar gyroscope, sleeping about two hours a day and on several occasions going up to 46 hours without sleep while travelling around 30 km. Little brown bat — and this is the entry to read hardest, because it corrects a number everyone repeats. Harding, C. D., Yovel, Y., Peirson, S. N., Hackett, T. D. & Vyazovskiy, V. V., "Re-examining extreme sleep duration in bats: implications for sleep phylogeny, ecology, and function," Sleep 45(8), zsac064 (2022): the familiar "up to 20 hours a day" comes almost entirely from two Brebbia-era studies of the 1960s and 70s on Myotis lucifugus and Eptesicus fuscus; the specific 19.9-hour figure was recorded at an ambient temperature of 33 °C and rests on a single study with an unknown number of specimens; the same measurements give 11.0 hours at 26 °C; and two fruit bat species come in at about 14.8 and 14.7 hours, comparable to a Syrian hamster. The paper's wider point is carried on the card: roughly one per cent of mammal species have had their sleep measured at all, much of the comparative record is decades old, and it was largely gathered in artificial conditions including social isolation. The commonly quoted giraffe and domestic-cat figures come out of the same thin literature, and the giraffe is quoted in circulation at both 1.9 and 4.6 hours, which is the tell. Northern fur seal — Lyamin, O. I. and colleagues, "Fur seals suppress REM sleep for very long periods without subsequent rebound," Current Biology 28(12), 2000–2005 (2018); and on the eyes, Kendall-Bar, J. M., Lyamin, O. I., Siegel, J. M. and colleagues, "Eye state asymmetry during aquatic unihemispheric slow wave sleep in northern fur seals (Callorhinus ursinus)," PLOS ONE 14(5), e0217025 (2019): during unihemispheric slow-wave sleep the eye contralateral to the sleeping hemisphere is closed about 99.4 per cent of the time, while the eye contralateral to the waking hemisphere opens in brief bouts averaging 1.9 seconds at around 8 per minute. Emperor penguin — Gilbert, C., Blanc, S., Le Maho, Y. & Ancel, A., and Ancel, A. and colleagues, on huddling energetics, including "Energy saving processes in huddling emperor penguins: from experiments to theory," Journal of Experimental Biology 211, 1–8 (2008): a maximum ambient temperature inside a huddle of 37.5 °C recorded while it was −19 °C outside; about two thirds of the metabolic saving attributable to reduced cold-exposed body surface and one third to the microclimate; free-ranging birds able to huddle running on average 21 per cent lower metabolic rate than loosely grouped birds; and the huddle shifting in a wave, a penguin moving every 30 to 60 seconds and triggering its neighbours. Parrotfish — Grutter, A. S., Rumney, J. G., Sinclair-Taylor, T., Waldie, P. & Franklin, C. E., "Fish mucous cocoons: the 'mosquito nets' of the sea," Biology Letters 7(2), 292–294 (2011): Chlorurus sordidus exposed overnight to blood-feeding gnathiid isopods with and without cocoons; roughly 10 per cent of cocooned fish were attacked against virtually all of the uncocooned, and building one costs about 2.5 per cent of the fish's daily energy budget. Whether the cocoon works by masking scent or as a physical barrier is not settled. Sea otter — rafting and kelp-anchored rest, carried at one card's depth and handed to The Stone You Keep (No. 58), which spends the otter properly; the hand-holding romance is a story told about them rather than a finding and is not here. Sponges — no neurons at all, yet documented diurnal contraction–expansion rhythms, light-controlled, in the demosponge Tethya wilhelma; whether any sponge sleeps is genuinely open, which is why the card carries no reframe table. Plants — nyctinasty, the circadian-clock-driven nastic movement of leaves at the onset of darkness. The provenance is read from the out-of-copyright primary rather than from summaries: Darwin, C. (assisted by F. Darwin), The Power of Movement in Plants (1880) records that "the so-called sleep of leaves is so conspicuous a phenomenon that it was observed as early as the time of Pliny; and since Linnæus published his famous Essay, 'Somnus Plantarum,' it has been the subject" of study — so the word is Linnaeus's, the observation is ancient, and Darwin himself calls the daytime version "improperly" named: "The leaves of some plants move during the day in a manner, which has improperly been called diurnal sleep." Held honestly: the sort into five bands is ours, arrived at after the entries; a sort by amount was tried first and failed, which is why the amount is now a band about its own unreliability rather than an axis. Nothing on this page licenses any claim about human beings — these are kin with their own business, the resemblances are rhymes rather than proofs, and the belonging claim in No. 59 deliberately does not rest on any of them. There is no standard way to rest.
