Shared
Air

There is only one air — a single, restless, well-mixed body with no walls inside it. What you breathe out drifts and spreads, and within a year or two it has circled the whole planet. We are not sipping from separate cups.

Don't blame the canary.
Change the air.

the bird isn't fragile · its alarm is information

One air

One breath holds something like ten thousand billion billion molecules. Once an old breath has scattered through the sky, your next lungful very likely carries atoms breathed by strangers on the far side of the Earth — and by people long gone.

Shared-air space

To share a room is to trade breath. Nervous systems in the same space read one another and settle into a shared rhythm — a calm body slows your breathing, a braced one quickens it. Good air makes room to regulate.

Habitable, for whom?

The same room is not the same for everyone. Fragrance, smoke, dust, exhaust, an airborne virus — the load lands unevenly, heaviest on those with the least power to leave. One person's ordinary afternoon is another's asthma attack.

“What's unbearable for the most sensitive is quietly wearing on everyone. Build it breathable for the canaries, and it's breathable for all.”

Side B of B
Build it
Open a window.The cheapest intervention there is, and among the most effective. Air that moves is air that dilutes.
Add a plant.Not because one pot filters a room, but because the green world breathes on the opposite beat to you — and a room people want to be in is half the work.
Wear the mask.Yours protects the room as well as you. It is a thing you do for the people in it.
Run a Corsi–Rosenthal box.A box fan and a few furnace filters taped into a cube, scrubbing a room's air for the price of a nice dinner. Conceived by Richard Corsi and first built by Jim Rosenthal in 2020, and deliberately never patented — the instructions are free, which is the whole point. Look them up; thousands of people have built one.
Then say so, out loud.Air you have made breathable is only shared if people know it is safe to come in.
What to say
“Can we open a window?”Asked of the room, not apologised for.
“This is a fragrance-free space.”A property of the room — not a favour to one person in it.
“There's a filter running in the corner.”Infrastructure, stated plainly, so nobody has to ask.
“What would make this room breathable for you?”And then change the room.
What not to say
“You're too sensitive.”The old mistake, and the one this sheet exists for. Blaming the bird.
“Nobody else has a problem.”The load lands unevenly. That is the finding, not the rebuttal.
“Just sit by the door.”That is the fix dumped on whoever is being harmed.
Not:

Not “too sensitive” — the canary isn't fragile; its alarm is information.

Not a private accommodation — habitable air is a commons, and a shared responsibility.

Not extraction — we move quietly and plant things; we don't move fast and break lungs.

Not scarcity — breathable, welcoming space isn't rationed; it's built and shared.

Shared air is only shared if everyone in it can breathe.

The science is standard: the atmosphere as a single well-mixed body; the reciprocal carbon–oxygen cycle of respiration and photosynthesis; the Great Oxidation Event, when cyanobacteria first oxygenated the sky (~2.4 billion years ago); and the stellar origin of the oxygen and nitrogen we breathe. Shared-air space is a term coined by Helen Edgar and Ryan Boren. The Corsi–Rosenthal box was conceived by Richard Corsi and first built and tested by Jim Rosenthal in 2020; it is unpatented and freely shared. The ethos is solarpunk: adrienne maree brown, Emergent Strategy (2017); Adam Flynn, “Solarpunk: Notes toward a manifesto” (2014); and the movement slogan “move quietly and plant things” (Andrew Dana Hudson). The miner's canary has long been taken up by disabled, chronically ill and environmental-illness communities naming themselves the canaries of a world whose air has gone bad.
