A Field Guide to
Being a Shark

Twelve ways to be a shark — the companion catalogue to The Skeleton That Skipped Bone (No. 32), The Ick Is a Border (No. 33) and Two Hundred Years Before You're Grown (No. 34). A shark that fits in your hand and one the length of a bus. One that walks. One that grazes. One that fires its jaws away from its face. One that is 272 years old and not considered grown until 156. Same clade. No standard shark.

One clade, no standard These are all sharks. Not relatives at a distance — one group, sharing a body plan and 400 million years. And inside it: twenty centimetres to nearly nineteen metres, plankton to seals to seagrass, a walker, a grazer, a glow. Belonging to a category never made anyone alike.
On the words Two entries are here because of a phrase rather than an animal. "Living fossil" and "supercharged senses" are both verdicts wearing descriptions — one pointing down, one pointing up, both skipping what the animal is doing.
Not: Not a ranking — the biggest is a filter feeder and the smallest is complete. Not "primitive" — no lineage is standing still, and nothing alive is a fossil. Not superpowers — the hammerhead's head is wider, not sharper. Flattery misreads too. Not a shortfall — a shark that cannot chase evolved a jaw that travels instead. Not a category we get to keep — "sharks are carnivores" lasted until somebody checked.
Star stuff, swimming

Every shark here is built from the same star-forged calcium and carbon, and most of them spend it on cartilage rather than bone — see The Skeleton That Skipped Bone (No. 32). One clade, one inheritance, and twelve completely different answers to the question of how to be alive in water. The spiky profile, at the scale of a whole group.

Figures follow the primary literature. Whale shark maximum length ~18.8 m; dwarf lanternshark ~20 cm (with a 28.9 cm record reported in 2021). Greenland shark ages from Nielsen et al. (Science, 2016) and swim speed from Watanabe et al. (2012). Goblin shark jaw protrusion — up to 3.1 m/s and 8.6–9.4% of body length — from Nakaya et al. (Scientific Reports, 2016). Thresher tail-slap hunting from Oliver et al. (PLoS ONE, 2013), 25 events filmed off the Philippines in 2010. Epaulette shark hypoxia and anoxia tolerance from the elasmobranch physiology literature. Cookiecutter counterillumination and the unlit collar following Widder (Environmental Biology of Fishes, 1998). Bonnethead seagrass digestion from Leigh, Papastamatiou & German, "Seagrass digestion by a notorious 'carnivore'" (Proc. R. Soc. B, 2018) — up to 62.1% of gut content mass, digested at ~50% efficiency. Hammerhead findings from Kajiura & Holland (no increase in electrosensory sensitivity; wider search area) and McComb et al. (J. Exp. Biol., 2009) on enhanced visual fields. Zebra shark parthenogenesis from Dudgeon et al. (Scientific Reports, 2017). Held honestly: maximum sizes are records, not typical animals; the cookiecutter's dark collar is the leading explanation rather than a settled one; and the cephalofoil is agreed to serve several overlapping functions, so "wider, not sharper" is one well-tested finding and not the whole account. The reframe — that a clade this varied has no standard member, and that "living fossil" and "supercharged" are the same move in opposite directions — is ours.
