# Six Principles and a *Room*

A counsellor's code of ethics is addressed to two people. The distress is usually coming from a third thing, and the third thing cannot be struck off. On what a relational ethic owes after the door — and on a professional body that has just moved, and where its step stopped.

---

This page starts with a question somebody else asked. Helen Edgar sat an evening session on ethics as part of her [BACP](https://www.bacp.co.uk/) training, came out of it, and wondered whether there was a Star Stuff version — whether everything this site keeps saying about difference, relation and belonging adds up to an ethics, or only to a mood.

It is a fair challenge and it deserves a specific answer rather than a warm one. So this is not an essay about ethics in general. It is an essay about one document, which is real, public, free to download, and about to be replaced.

## Six principles

The *Ethical Framework for the Counselling Professions* is what BACP members in the UK are bound by. The edition in force as this is written was first published in 2018 and took effect on 1 July that year. It has three parts — a commitment to clients, a section on ethics, and a long section on good practice — and at the centre of the ethics section sit six core principles, given here in full because the whole of this essay is a reading of them.

**Being trustworthy:** honouring the trust placed in the practitioner.
 **Autonomy:** respect for the client's right to be self-governing.
 **Beneficence:** a commitment to promoting the client's wellbeing.
 **Non-maleficence:** a commitment to avoiding harm to the client.
 **Justice:** the fair and impartial treatment of all clients and the provision of adequate services.
 **Self-respect:** fostering the practitioner's self-knowledge, integrity and care for self.

BACP · Ethical Framework for the Counselling Professions, 2018 · Ethics, paragraph 5

Before anything else: **this is a good document.** Nothing that follows is a complaint about counsellors, who are for the most part the people trying, inside services that have been cut to the bone. The framework is careful in ways that documents of its kind usually are not. It knows its own principles collide, and instead of pretending otherwise it says so and tells you what to do about it.

“A decision or course of action does not necessarily become unethical merely because it is controversial or because other practitioners would have reached different conclusions in similar circumstances. A practitioner's obligation is to consider all the relevant circumstances with as much care as possible and to be appropriately accountable for decisions made.”

BACP · Ethical Framework 2018 · Ethics, paragraph 7

That is a grown-up sentence. It refuses the fantasy of a code that resolves cases for you, and it puts the weight on reasoning you can be held to. We have no improvement to offer on it.

Read the six principles again anyway, and count the nouns that are doing ethical work. *Practitioner. Client. Services.* That is the cast. Every principle is a duty running between two people, with one half-clause — the provision of adequate services, inside *Justice* — reaching past them.

## The word that appears once

That reading is an impression, and impressions are cheap. The document is public and machine-readable, so it can be counted instead.

How these counts were made

Both frameworks were downloaded as published PDFs from `bacp.co.uk` and converted with `pdftotext -layout`; words were counted with `grep -oiw` on the extracted text. The 2018 extraction runs to **6,825 words**, the 2026 to **4,124**. Anyone can reproduce this in about two minutes, and should — a count you have not run yourself is a claim somebody made, and this site has been wrong that way before.

In the 2018 framework, the word **environment** appears **once**. Not in the principles, not in the values, not in the commitments to clients, not anywhere in the long good-practice section. It appears in paragraph 8, in a sentence about the practitioner's inner life.

“Personal moral qualities are internalised values that shape how we relate to others and our environment. They represent a moral energy or drive that may operate unconsciously and unexamined.”

BACP · Ethical Framework 2018 · Ethics, paragraph 8

The environment is present as something the practitioner has an attitude toward. It is a direction their moral energy points. It is not a party to anything.

| **system / systemic / structural / institutional** | 0 | — |

The single *organisation* is the best detail in the table, and it is not a gotcha — it is the shape of the thing. The only organisation named in a professional ethics framework is the professional body's own company number.

The two uses of *setting* are worth stating precisely, because they are the closest the document comes and they do not get there. Both instruct you to apply the framework *in ways that are appropriate to* your role and your service's setting. The setting is a variable that modifies your duty. It is never a thing that can be at fault.

**None of this is an oversight, and reading it as one would be the lazy version of this essay.** A professional code regulates professionals. Its sanction is registration, and you cannot strike off a building. The framework says who it is for in its own opening: it exists so that BACP conduct panels have a reference point when a complaint is made against a member. A document built to answer *did this member act well?* is going to be composed almost entirely of members and the people they saw.

The trouble is not that the code is wrong about its job. It is what happens to everything the code has no slot for.

## Where the fault goes to live

Picture the referral. An Autistic teenager is not coping at school. By the time they reach a counselling room, a good deal has already happened to them, and almost none of it happened *inside* them.

The lighting is fluorescent and flickers at a rate most people cannot consciously see. The timetable moves them through six rooms and six social configurations in a day, with the transitions unbuffered. The corridor at changeover is louder than a nightclub. The uniform policy specifies fabric they cannot wear. The reasonable adjustment was agreed in March and has not been implemented, because the person who agreed it left. The waiting list ran eleven months, and in month seven they stopped sleeping. The diagnostic category they were given routes them toward a service commissioned to reduce behaviours rather than to change any of the above. The school is judged on attendance, so every accommodation costs somebody a number they are measured on.

Now open the code. Six principles. *Autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, self-respect, being trustworthy.* Every one of them is a question about how the practitioner conducts themselves toward this young person. There is no line to write the lighting on. There is no line for the eleven months. There is no line for the commissioning incentive, and none for the adjustment that evaporated when a member of staff resigned.

When the only parties to an ethic are two people, everything that is not one of those two people has to be recorded somewhere else — and the only somewhere else on offer is inside the person who came in.

This is the deficit model arriving by paperwork rather than by prejudice. Nobody has to believe anything cruel. The form simply has one box, the box has a name in it, and the lighting is not eligible.

There is a serviceable word for the thing that actually produced the distress, and it is *assemblage* — a temporary gathering of unlike things (bodies, buildings, timetables, categories, funding cycles, lights) that between them make some outcomes easy and others impossible. It comes from Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, and it is genuinely useful here, so we will use it and then be careful with it.

A note we owe, from four pieces ago

This site recently audited its own habit of reaching for French philosophers to say things that people with a much older claim have been saying, in print, with names and nations attached. Zoe Todd calls the pattern *aer nullius*, and her charge is not that Deleuze is wrong — it is **who else could have told you, and whether they got a line in the bibliography**. Relational selfhood is not a 1980 invention, and an ethics that locates the self in its relations has addresses far older than this one.

The honest response is narrow: use the word, credit the two men it came from, and do not present it as the discovery. It would be worse to reach past that for somebody else's jurisprudence to lend weight to an argument of ours — so we are not going to. [No. 81 makes that case at length](https://starstuff.earth/already-an-entity-zine.html), including the paragraph it decided not to write.

## The floor is already poured

There is an obvious way for this argument to go wrong, and it is worth naming before going further, because it is the reason a lot of people are wary of relational ethics in the first place.

If harm is produced by assemblages, if selves are made in relation, if nothing is fixed and everything is becoming — then what stops any of it from dissolving into *well, that's just another assemblage*? An ethics with no floor is not an ethics. It is a way of being unavailable for blame, and the people it fails first are the ones with the least power in the room.

This site put its floor in some time ago, and it is one sentence.

“There is exactly one disqualifier at this door, and it is not difference — it is harm.”

Star Stuff · [Inclusion Safety: Owed, Not Earned](https://starstuff.earth/inclusion-safety-creed.html)

The Creed is a doorway ethic: it settles who belongs, and the answer is everybody, because belonging is constitutive rather than conferred. This essay is the other half — what is owed once people are through the door — and it inherits the floor unchanged.

So: **a relational ethic does not weaken *do no harm*. It widens the list of things capable of doing it.** The practitioner's duty is untouched. What is added is that a timetable can be an agent, a waiting list can be an agent, a commissioning incentive can be an agent, and none of them stop being answerable just because they cannot attend a hearing. Somebody set that timetable. Somebody signed that contract. Difference is not the problem. Harm is, and harm has more authors than a two-party code can list.

## Then the profession moved

Here is where an essay of this shape normally ends, with the writer having proved something about a profession that was not consulted. That is not available, because while this was being written the profession moved — further and more deliberately than most.

BACP spent four years revising the framework. The replacement was published in 2026. Its own copyright page says it takes effect from 1 November 2026; BACP's guidance page says it becomes mandatory at midday on Tuesday 3 November 2026, and members are committed to the 2018 edition until then. Both dates are given here because both are published and they are not the same date.

The new edition puts a sentence on its cover, under the title, where a subtitle goes.

“Working ethically is working within relationships”

BACP · Ethical Framework for the Counselling Professions 2026 · cover

The relational turn that this site has been arguing for from the physics end is, as of this November, the front cover of Helen's professional body's code of conduct. It is worth sitting with that for a second before saying anything clever about it.

And the change is not cosmetic. The whole document is rebuilt. There are now three sections — *Working ethically within relationships*, *Ethical principles*, *Core responsibilities* — and the first of them is a method for reasoning rather than a list of duties. The Latin is gone: *autonomy*, *beneficence*, *non-maleficence* and *self-respect* appear zero times in the new edition, replaced by plain English — *Do no harm*, *Best interest*, *Respect*. Six principles have become twelve. And one of the twelve is this:

“**Anti-oppression:** Be aware of relational power dynamics, ensuring relational power imbalances do not create or lead to marginalisation or minoritisation”

BACP · Ethical Framework 2026 · Ethical principles

A mainstream professional body in the United Kingdom now lists **anti-oppression** as a core ethical principle, alongside honesty and confidentiality, binding on tens of thousands of practitioners. Elsewhere the new edition requires collaboration with the person a decision will affect, requires a written rationale when collaboration is not possible, and defines collaboration as “taking deliberate steps towards mitigating potentially harmful power dynamics and inequalities between people.” It even opens a slot for the outside: practitioners must now account for *external factors*, meaning “those factors that sit outside the relationship but may impact the relationship,” explicitly including “organisational expectations and requirements.”

That is a real step, taken deliberately, over four years. Say so plainly before saying anything else.

## Where the step stopped

Now run the same count on the new document, because the interesting thing about a step is where it stops.

*The same words, both editions*

| Word | 2018 · 6,825 words | 2026 · 4,124 words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **relationship / relationships** | 34 | 27 |
| **relational** | 2 | 2 |
| **organisational** | 0 | 3 |
| **environment** | 1 | 0 |
| **setting / settings** | 2 | 0 |
| **systemic** | 0 | 0 |
| **structural** | 0 | 0 |
| **institutional** | 0 | 0 |

Relationship language is up sharply once you account for the shorter document. Organisational language appears for the first time. And *environment*, which appeared once, now appears not at all.

The two occurrences of *relational* in the 2026 edition are the ones that matter, and they are both inside the anti-oppression principle: *relational power dynamics*, *relational power imbalances*. Read the principle again with that in view. The framework has learned to see power — and it has located power **between the people in the relationship**, twice, by name. Oppression, in the new code, is something that happens in the space between a practitioner and the person in front of them.

A great deal of it does. That is a true and hard-won thing to have written down. But it is not where most of it comes from, and there is a body of work saying so precisely.

“But these injustices are not merely interpersonal – they are structural. They arise from socio-material environments that facilitate ease for neurotypical ways of being while simultaneously marginalizing neurodivergent ways of being.”

Zamir Kadodia & Joel Krueger · Epistemic injustice, niche construction, and neurodiversity · Philosophical Psychology, 2026

Kadodia and Krueger are writing about epistemic injustice — Miranda Fricker's term for the harms done to us specifically as knowers, when our testimony is discounted because of who is giving it, or when the concepts we would need to describe our own experience have been crowded out. Their move is to say that the niches we think in are built, maintained and reproduced, and that neuronormative assumptions get built into them, so that some ways of being are afforded ease and others are made to work for it. The injustice is in the room's construction, not only in anybody's manner.

That is exactly the gap between *relational* power and structural power, stated by people whose subject is us.

And there is one more thing in the 2026 edition worth reading slowly, because it is where the room finally does appear.

“Contracts and context of work – our commitment through the application of this Ethical Framework is to collaborative working, however, there may be occasions when our contracts for work, or the context where we are working, specifically exclude collaborative working. Where this is the case, the reasons need to be documented clearly.”

BACP · Ethical Framework 2026 · When collaboration might not be possible

This is honest drafting and we would not want it removed — a code that pretended practitioners are free agents inside services that constrain them would be worse, and lying. But notice the grammatical position the room has been given.

The environment enters the new code chiefly as a reason you could not do the ethical thing, and the requirement is to write down why.

It arrives as an exemption rather than as a defendant. Which is not a failure of nerve; it is the limit of what a *professional* code can be. Registration is the only sanction it holds, and no room has ever been registered. So the step stops where the instrument stops, and pointing at that is not an accusation. It is a description of the next problem, and the next problem does not belong to counsellors alone.

## The third ecology

There is a small book that names the missing piece exactly, and it was written by somebody who spent his working life in a clinic. Félix Guattari — the second name in Deleuze and Guattari, and a practising psychotherapist at the La Borde clinic for four decades — argued in 1989 that any adequate ethics has to run across three registers at once, which he called the three ecologies: **mental**, **social**, and **environmental**. His claim was that treating them separately guarantees failure in all three, because a mind is not repairable in isolation from the relations it sits in, and neither of those is repairable in isolation from the material world they sit in.

Set the two frameworks against that and the shape is unmistakable, and unmistakably progressive.

*The 2018 edition reaches the practitioner's interior and their conduct toward a client. The 2026 edition reaches the relationship and names power inside it. The third band is where the lighting, the timetable and the eleven-month wait live — and it is the one whose word went from one to zero.*

Guattari is not being invoked here as a proof of anything. He is being used the way a name for something is used: the profession has taken the second rung, and there is a third, and it already has a name and a literature. That is a lead, not a verdict, and the mapping above is our reading of two documents rather than a finding either of them would recognise.

## What it looks like on a Tuesday

An ethics that stops at the diagnosis of a gap is not much of an ethics. The practical question is what changes on an ordinary day, and the answer to that one is not ours — it is already written, by the person who asked the question at the top of this page.

Helen Edgar's [*Ethodivergent Hearth Building*](https://autisticrealms.com/ethodivergent-hearth-building-a-relational-neuroqueering-community-practice/) came out of a planning conversation with Stimpunks for the Neuroqueer Learning Spaces project, and it asks what it takes to build spaces that *sustain* neurodivergent people rather than merely include us. The hearth is the image: a warm centre people return to, which may not be a room at all.

“In ethodivergent hearth building, these sensory and relational centres become vital anchors, places to return to without performance, where our difference is held with warmth rather than shame or stigma.”

Helen Edgar · Ethodivergent Hearth Building: A Relational Neuroqueering Community Practice · Autistic Realms, 10 July 2025

The word underneath it is **ethodiversity**, Ombre Tarragnat's term, which extends the neurodiversity paradigm past the human brain to the whole range of ways of sensing, moving, attending and relating — across species, across the living and the non-living. It is the vocabulary this site has been using for a year in its more-than-human pieces, and it turns out to be an ethical vocabulary rather than a descriptive one, because once *ways of relating* are the unit, the material conditions of relating stop being scenery.

Read as an answer to the gap above, the hearth is unglamorously concrete. It is time that bends to bodyminds instead of bodyminds bent to timetables. It is fallow rest, stim time, quiet time. It is co-regulation, sensory consideration, bodily autonomy, relational consent. It is access intimacy — Mia Mingus's term for the feeling of not having to explain your access needs — treated as an ordinary design requirement rather than a lucky accident. None of that is a therapeutic technique. All of it is a change to the third band.

Which is the practical form of the whole argument. If the distress is being produced in the room, then **the ethical act is often a change to the room**, and the person best placed to make it is frequently not the practitioner — it is whoever set the timetable, wrote the commission, chose the lighting, or built the waiting list. A relational ethic does not let those people out. It is the only kind that has ever had a place to write their names.

## What this is not

Not:

- **Not an attack on therapists, or on BACP.** The 2018 framework is a careful document that knows its own principles collide. The 2026 revision moved further than most professional bodies have. Both are quoted here at length precisely because they are worth reading.
- **Not an argument that rules are oppression.** Codes with teeth are how people with less power get recourse. The complaint is about the size of the frame, never about having one.
- **Not a dissolution of responsibility.** An ethic in which everything is an assemblage and nobody is answerable is worse than the one it replaced. The floor is the Creed's, unchanged: harm is the disqualifier, and rooms have authors.
- **Not physics proving philosophy.** Nothing about relational quantum mechanics establishes anything about ethics, and this site has a whole page about why we do not make that move. A rhyme, not a proof.
- **Not somebody else's law recruited to settle ours.** There is an older and more complete answer to “what is missing from a code that cannot name the land,” and it belongs to people who are not us. See [No. 81](https://starstuff.earth/already-an-entity-zine.html), which declines the same shortcut at greater length.
- **Not a finished ethics.** This is one document read closely, and a gap named. The gap is real; the filling of it is a much larger job than an essay.

## The line

Helen's question was whether this site's commitments add up to an ethics. They add up to at least this much, and it can be put in one sentence that a practitioner could actually use on a Tuesday:

An ethic you can only point at a person is an ethic that will keep finding the fault in the person.

Everything else follows from taking that seriously. Belonging is owed rather than earned, which the Creed already settled. Harm is the one disqualifier, which it also settled. What this page adds is the third thing: **the list of possible authors of harm is longer than the list of people in the room**, and an ethics that cannot write the longer list will keep producing the same verdict about the same people, in good faith, forever.

The universe doesn't pathologize its own variation. Codes do, occasionally, by having nowhere else to put the blame.
