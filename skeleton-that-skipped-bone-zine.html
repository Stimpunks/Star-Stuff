Where we started

The skeleton
you were promised

This whole collection opens with a bone.

Bone Song starts where you'd expect: your calcium was forged in stars, and the skeleton it built is piezoelectric. Press on it and it makes voltage. Iwao Yasuda found that in the 1950s. Your bones are listening to how you move.

That's the founding image here. Star-forged mineral, laid down in a living lattice, humming quietly under load.

And then the ocean says: sure. Or don't.

There is a whole class of vertebrates — sharks, rays, skates, the strange deep chimaeras — that never turns up to the bone. Same phylum. Same backbone-having club. No true bone anywhere in the body.

Not a lesser version of yours. A different answer to the same question, and it has been working for a very long time.

same club, different build
yasuda 1954–1957 · dean & summers 2006

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 2
What's in there instead

A mosaic
where the bone would be

"Cartilaginous" makes it sound floppy. It isn't.

A shark's skeleton is cartilage with a mineral crust laid over it — thousands of small blocks, packed edge to edge like paving, sitting on top of an uncalcified core. The blocks are called tesserae, from the word for mosaic tiles, and that is exactly what they look like.

Mason Dean and Adam Summers, reviewing the tissue in 2006, describe a skeleton whose mineralisation comes in distinct types — areolar, globular, prismatic — arranged differently depending on the animal's age, its species, where in the body you're looking, and how it feeds. The skeleton is tuned to the life.

And the design does something bone can't. A tiled surface over a soft core bends further before it breaks, then springs back. It's lighter than bone. It doesn't need marrow cavities or a blood supply threaded all the way through.

None of that is a workaround. It's a different set of trade-offs, chosen — over deep time — by an enormous number of successful animals.

You were taught one skeleton and told it was the skeleton. There are others, and they are not rough drafts.

dean & summers 2006 · zoology
a mosaic, tuned to the life it carries

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 3
The part that changes the story

They didn't miss it.
They let it go

For most of a century the shark was taught as the before picture.

Cartilage first, bone later — sharks as a living snapshot of the stage vertebrates passed through on the way to becoming us. Primitive. Ancestral. Not there yet.

The fossils stopped cooperating. In 2020 Martin Brazeau and colleagues described Minjinia turgenensis, an Early Devonian fish from Mongolia on the placoderm grade — close to the common ancestry of all jawed vertebrates — and it is full of endochondral bone. The kind sharks don't have.

Which flips the sequence. If the shared ancestor already had bone, then the shark's bonelessness isn't something it never got round to. It is something its lineage gave up. A derived condition. A loss.

We have run into this exact shape before, one zine ago. The crab body plan was gained five times and lost seven — leaving turned out to be the commoner event. Here it is again in the skeleton: subtraction is not the same as never having arrived.

And whatever they traded away, they have been fine. The chondrichthyan record runs back to the early Silurian — call it 440 million years, on scales and teeth at first. Four mass extinctions. Still here.

The animal you were told hadn't got there yet had been there, and chose otherwise.

nature ecology & evolution, 2020
the before picture was never before

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 4
The word doing the damage

"Primitive"
is a verdict

Watch how it works. Primitive has a technical meaning — a trait resembling an ancestral state — and it carries a second meaning it never declares: unfinished. lesser. earlier on the way to better.

Both meanings ride in the same word. So a real observation ("this trait resembles the ancestral condition") arrives already sentenced.

And here the technical meaning was also wrong. The shark skeleton isn't ancestral. It's a later change. The word was carrying a verdict it hadn't earned, about a fact that wasn't a fact.

Our field guide to nervous systems flagged the same habit in neuroscience — jellyfish nets called primitive, worm brains called simple. Same move, different tissue.

It's the move that gets made about us, too. Low-functioning. Developmentally delayed. Behind. Every one of them a measurement with a destination smuggled inside it, and the destination is always somebody else.

the verdict inside the description
low-functioning · delayed · behind

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 5
The turn

Then someone decided
it was a cure

Here is what happened next to the skeleton we've just spent five pages admiring.

Somewhere along the way a claim got loose: sharks don't get cancer. Cartilage has no blood vessels of its own; tumours need a blood supply to grow; therefore — the reasoning went — a shark must be carrying something that stops them. Grind it up. Take it as a powder.

It became an industry. Capsules, powders, whole shelves of them, sold to people who had just been given the worst news of their lives and were looking anywhere at all.

Notice the shape of the claim, because it is the important part. The animal is no longer an animal. Once you've decided a body contains the answer to your problem, the body stops being a life with its own reasons and becomes a supply.

And then there's the small matter of whether it was true.

the premise, the leap, the shelf
an animal becomes a supply

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 6
The record

Sharks
get cancer

They always did. Somebody just had to go and count.

In 2004 Gary Ostrander and colleagues went through the literature and the Registry of Tumors in Lower Animals and found 42 documented tumours in cartilaginous fishes — across at least 21 species, in nine families and seven orders. Twenty-four in sharks, sixteen in skates and rays, two in chimaeras.

Tumours of the nervous system, the gut, the skin, the blood-forming tissue, the reproductive organs, the endocrine system — and of the skeleton itself. At least fifteen were malignant, invading normal tissue. Their paper's title says the quiet part: Shark Cartilage, Cancer and the Growing Threat of Pseudoscience.

Then it was tested properly. Charles Loprinzi and colleagues ran a randomised trial in patients with advanced cancer and reported in 2005: no difference in survival, no difference in quality of life, and side effects bad enough that people struggled to keep taking it.

So: the premise was false, the product did nothing, and the animals were killed anyway.

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 7
The pattern underneath

A body read as a cure
gets harvested

This is the sentence the zine exists for, so here it is plainly.

The shark was not harmed because anyone hated it. It was harmed because somebody decided its body contained something useful, and once that decision is made, everything else follows without anyone needing to be cruel on purpose.

You stop asking what the animal is doing with its own skeleton. You start asking what the skeleton can do for you. And then you go and get it.

It is the same operation when it's aimed at people. We know this frame very well:

the search for the cause, so there can be a cure. The interest in Autistic people that arrives only as what are they good at, could we use that. The inspiration story where a disabled life is raw material for somebody else's lesson. The research funding that goes to prevention rather than to the things we've actually asked for.

All of it starts the same way — not with hatred, with usefulness. A body is read for what can be extracted, and the reading feels like appreciation right up until the moment it costs you something.

Being valued for what can be taken out of you is not the same as being valued.

extraction begins as appreciation
and then it comes to collect

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 8
Back to the animal

What the skeleton
is actually for

It's for the shark.

That's the whole answer, and it's worth sitting with, because it is the answer that got skipped.

The tiled cartilage is doing a job every second of that animal's life. It holds a jaw that has to bite without shattering. It carries a body through water with the springy recoil that makes the swimming cheap. It grows with the animal, tuned — species by species, region by region, feeding style by feeding style — to the exact loads that particular life puts on it.

It is not a message to us. It is not a resource with an animal wrapped round it. It is a body, solving the problem of being that body, in a way that has held up across four mass extinctions.

Your skeleton is doing the same thing. Yasuda's finding wasn't that your bones are useful to somebody — it's that they're listening, minute by minute, to the life you are actually living.

Two different mineral answers. Both of them the same star-forged calcium. Neither of them owed to anyone.

the same element, twice
neither of them owed to anyone

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 9
Held with care

What we are
not saying

There are several wrong turns available here, and a few of them are flattering. Naming them.

Notsharks are better. We're not swapping which skeleton wins. Bone is extraordinary. Tesserae are extraordinary. There is no podium.
Notlosing a trait is secretly superior. "They gave up bone" is a fact about history, not a moral. Loss isn't noble. It's just not the failure it was read as.
Notmedicine is the enemy. The people who counted the tumours and ran the trial are the medicine. Pseudoscience is what failed the patients here, and it failed the sharks too.
Notanyone who took the pills was a fool. They were frightened people who had been lied to by an industry. The blame sits with whoever sold it.
Notdisabled people are like sharks. We are not making anyone an animal metaphor. The shared thing is the frame — a body read for what can be taken out of it — not the bodies.
Notbiology proving a politics. A rhyme, not a proof. That vertebrates built two skeletons is not why difference deserves welcome — it would deserve welcome in a universe that only ever made one.

no podium, no ranks
two answers, both standing

Skipped Bone · Stimpunks 10
Closer to home

You don't owe anyone
a reason

Everything in this zine turns on one question, and it's a question that gets asked about people constantly.

What is it for?

Asked of a shark's skeleton, it produced a shelf of pills that didn't work and a lot of dead animals. Asked of a person, it produces the same shape: what are you good for, what does your difference get us, where's the upside.

You've probably been handed the flattering version. Autistic people are so detail-oriented — brilliant for quality assurance. It sounds like praise. It's a receipt. It says the welcome is conditional on the yield, and quietly hands you the bill if the yield stops.

So here is the plain form of the refusal, which is the same one we'd make on the shark's behalf if it could be bothered to care what we think:

The skeleton is for the animal. Your mind is for you. Not a resource. Not a lesson. Not a supply. A body doing the work of being itself, which is the only justification anything in this universe has ever had, and it has always been enough.

Four hundred million years of no bone at all, and nobody down there is apologising.

The tiles aren't a rough draft of a spine. You aren't a rough draft of anyone.

change the question
and the whole shelf empties

The Skeleton That Skipped Bone is Zine No. 32 in the Stimpunks Star Stuff series, and a deliberate companion and counter-song to Bone Song (1) — which opens this collection on piezoelectric bone, and which this zine answers with a vertebrate that has none. It is also a second visit to the argument in Five Times a Crab, Seven Times Not (31): that a lineage leaving a trait is a different fact from a lineage never having arrived at it. It extends the naming of buried verdicts begun in A Field Guide to Nervous Systems (FG 5) and The Lines We Drew (9). It was written for a young person in our community whose special interest is sharks. It is theirs.

Sources & notes. On the tissue: Dean, M. N. & Summers, A. P. (2006). Mineralized cartilage in the skeleton of chondrichthyan fishes. Zoology, 109(2), 164–168 — source of the tessellated-cartilage description, the tesserae overlying an uncalcified core, and the three calcification types (areolar, globular, prismatic) varying with age, species, body region and feeding behaviour. On the loss of bone: Brazeau, M. D., Giles, S., Dearden, R. P., Jerve, A., Ariunchimeg, Y., Zorig, E., Sansom, R., Guillerme, T. & Castiello, M. (2020). Endochondral bone in an Early Devonian 'placoderm' from Mongolia. Nature Ecology & Evolution, 4, 1477–1484 — describing Minjinia turgenensis and supporting a phylogenetically deeper origin of endochondral bone, with its absence in chondrichthyans as a secondary condition. On the age of the group: the chondrichthyan fossil record reaches the early Silurian (~440 Ma), though the earliest material is scales and teeth rather than skeletons; Doliodus problematicus (Early Devonian, Canada) is the oldest known largely intact shark. On the cure claim: Ostrander, G. K., Cheng, K. C., Wolf, J. C. & Wolfe, M. J. (2004). Shark cartilage, cancer and the growing threat of pseudoscience. Cancer Research, 64(23), 8485–8491 — source of the 42 documented chondrichthyan tumours across at least 21 species in nine families and seven orders (24 sharks, 16 skates or rays, 2 chimaeroids), of the affected organ systems including the skeleton, and of the at-least-15 malignant count. Loprinzi, C. L., Levitt, R., Barton, D. L., Sloan, J. A., Atherton, P. J., Smith, D. J. et al. (2005). Evaluation of shark cartilage in patients with advanced cancer: A North Central Cancer Treatment Group trial. Cancer, 104(1), 176–182 — no difference in overall survival or quality of life, with adverse effects driving poor compliance. Piezoelectric bone is Iwao Yasuda's (1954–1957), as set out in Bone Song.

Held honestly. The secondary-loss reading of the chondrichthyan skeleton is the direction the current evidence points, and it is a live area rather than a closed one — Brazeau and colleagues present Minjinia as reviving and supporting that view, not as settling it. "No true bone" means no endochondral bone: chondrichthyans do mineralise, extensively, and the tesserae are real mineral; some rays carry bone-like tissue in dermal denticles and thorns, which is an active question in the literature. The reasons a lineage would shed bone are not established here and we have not claimed any. We have not put a number on how many sharks the cartilage trade killed, because we could not verify one in a primary source; what is documented is that the premise was false and the product did not work. And the ethics do not come from the biology. That the universe built more than one skeleton is not why a person deserves welcome — as about.html puts it, we are noticing a rhyme, not collecting a proof.

The star stuff is Carl Sagan's, and the SN 1006 remnant on every Stimpunks page. Calcium from a dying star, spent two ways. The universe doesn't pathologize its own variation.
