The Channel You Cannot Mask

on four dogs, thirty-six strangers, 675 correct trials out of 720, and why the quiet room with the dogs is restful for a reason nobody flatters anybody by naming

The room we left

We went and sat with the dogs

Many of us have done this. There is a gathering somewhere in the house, and there is a quieter place in it, and the dogs are usually in the quieter place. So that is where we are.

It gets described from the outside as avoidance, and from the inside it does not feel like avoidance at all. It feels like going where the work stops.

Account — Ryan Boren

“Like many Autistic people, I always felt like I got along better with dogs than humans. Whenever I was at someone's home for a gathering, I'd hang out with the dogs some place quiet and leave the humans to themselves.”

This is an account and it is not data. One person, remembering. It is the reason this zine exists and it is not evidence for a single claim in it. Where a claim needs a source, there is a source.

The explanation usually offered for this is that dogs are more empathetic than people. We went looking for that and could not get it to hold up. What we found instead was better, and it is not a compliment to anybody — it is a fact about which channel is being read.

The channel

A room grades one narrow band

Whatever else a gathering is, it is a place where a few specific outputs are continuously assessed. Not all of you. A band.

Face. Voice. Timing. Gaze. Posture. The rate at which you return a serve. Whether your expression is legible from across a room, and whether it is legible as the thing being felt. That band is what masking works on, and the work is real: it is authored output, produced to specification, in real time, while also having a conversation.

The measurement

Four dogs, thirty-six strangers

In 2022, four dogs at Queen’s University Belfast were put to a question with the easy answers designed out of it: can you tell, by smell alone, whether a person was stressed when this sample was taken?

The stressor was deliberately dull. Participants counted backwards aloud from 9000 in units of 17, for three minutes, with no aids. Breath and sweat were collected before the task and again after it. Stress was confirmed independently — self-report and physiological measures — and of 53 people recruited, eleven were excluded for not having a sufficient stress response at all. The dogs were never shown a face.

Individual accuracy ran from 90.00% to 96.88%. The last of those numbers is the one to sit with: on the very first trial with a stranger's odour — nobody the dog had met, no history, no relationship, no owner, no love — the dogs were right 34 times out of 36.

Figure 1

Three ports, and one of them is you

The design matters more than the percentage, because it is what rules out the easy explanations. It was double-blind: nobody present knew which port held which sample, so nobody in the room could leak the answer.

Phase one trained the dog on the stress sample against two blanks. Phase two is the real question — the stress sample, the same person's baseline sample, and a blank. Not can you find a human smell. Can you tell these two states of one person apart.

Wilson, Campbell, Petzel & Reeve

The Channel You Cannot Mask · Stimpunks 5
What actually changed

Stress has an outgassing

Here is the sentence the authors actually wrote, and notice what it is about.

“Results indicate that the physiological processes associated with an acute psychological stress response produce changes in the volatile organic compounds emanating from breath and/or sweat that are detectable to dogs.”Wilson, Campbell, Petzel & Reeve, PLOS ONE 17(9):e0274143, 2022

Not dogs understand our feelings. Not dogs love us enough to notice. Chemistry. A body under acute stress breathes faster, the pulse rises, the blood pressure lifts — and the mixture of compounds coming off it changes. The authors cite that mechanism as a hypothesis from earlier work rather than something they measured, and we are reporting it the same way: the profile change is inferred from the dogs' behaviour, not chemically identified in this study.

What is not in doubt is the discrimination itself. Something in the sample was different, reliably, across 36 strangers.

And a volatile organic compound is not a performance. It is metabolic exhaust. You do not choose it, rehearse it, time it, or get better at it. There is no version of you that is doing it well.

The Channel You Cannot Mask · Stimpunks 6
The turn

It is not asking you to be legible

Put the two halves together and the quiet room stops being a mystery.

A room reads the authored channel and grades it. The dog reads a channel you were never able to author in the first place — and therefore cannot be marked down on.

The reason it is restful is not that the dog understands us better. It is that the dog is reading the one channel we are not required to manage.the argument of this zine, in one sentence

This reframes something worth reframing. The experience of being hard to read is usually filed under deficit: something missing in the signal, something faulty in the sender. But the Belfast dogs read 36 strangers at 93.75% across 720 trials with the authored channel entirely absent — no face, no voice, no timing, no gaze.

The signal was never missing. It was being looked for in the wrong band.

That is a claim about rooms, not about noses. A room could in principle stop grading the narrow band; it mostly does not. The dog's advantage is that it never had access to the band we spend ourselves on.

The honesty

Four dogs. Their word: proof of principle

If we stopped at spread seven this would be a nice zine and a slightly dishonest one. Three things have to go on the page.

One. The sample is four dogs. The authors say so themselves, in their own limitations: “It must be noted that our sample consists of only four dogs.” They add that this is in line with other bio-detection studies, because training a specialist dog takes a long time. They call the work proof of principle rather than a generalisable finding, and we are calling it that too.

Two. Discrimination is not interpretation. The study shows the dogs could tell two samples apart. It does not show they knew what the difference meant, that they connected it to the person, or that they cared. Three separate claims, and only the first one was measured.

Three. The four dogs were trained to alert. They were rewarded for indicating the stress port. That is a detection task, not a spontaneous act of concern — and reading it as concern is exactly the move this collection exists to refuse.

What we did not use, and why. The famous study in this area is the oxytocin gaze loop (Nagasawa et al., Science, 2015), and it is not on any spread here. Published commentaries flag confounds between its dog and wolf conditions and note that not all dogs showed the effect. It is contested, not refuted — and a contested result is not a foundation.

And it may not be comfort

Two years later a different group asked the question we ought to be most afraid of: what does smelling our stress do to the dog?

Eighteen dogs. Odour samples from three unfamiliar volunteers, collected during stressful and during relaxing activities. Then a cognitive bias task — a food bowl at a trained rewarded location, a trained unrewarded location, and three ambiguous spots between them.

Under stress odour, the dogs became significantly less likely to approach the near-negative bowl than with no odour at all. The authors read this as possible risk-reduction behaviour. They also found the odour appeared to affect learning.

So the dog in the quiet room with us may not be being soothing. It may be being careful. That is the honest shape of it, and it is the part that stops this being a comfortable story about how much dogs love us.

Parr-Cortes, Müller, Talas, Mendl,
Guest & Rooney · Sci Rep 14, 2024

The trainer

Before you train a dog

In 2019 an Autistic professional dog trainer answered a question that gets asked as an insult and turns out to be a diagnosis.

The question is whether applied behaviour analysis is dog training for children. Carol Millman — B.Sc. in psychology, animal health technology diploma, a decade in practice, apprenticed at an assistance dog society — took it seriously and answered as a professional, in NeuroClastic.

Her answer is not the one the question expects. It is that the comparison is unfair to the dog trainers.

“Before you train a dog you need to accept that it is a dog.”Carol Millman, B.Sc., RAHT, CPDT-KA, CTDI — NeuroClastic, 27 March 2019

Her first task, she writes, is to make sure every one of the dog's fundamental needs is met — before any conditioning happens at all. Relieve the fear rather than train the animal not to display it. Let the retriever retrieve. Buy the terrier a sandbox instead of asking it to stop being a terrier.

The inversion

The time bomb dog

There is one line in Millman's piece that does more work than the rest of it together, and it is a piece of ordinary trade knowledge.

“A dog who has been trained not to growl is considered by trainers to be a ‘time bomb dog.’”Carol Millman, NeuroClastic, 27 March 2019

A growl is information. Suppress the display and you have not removed the fear; you have removed the warning, and the bite arrives with no notice. Trainers have a name for this outcome because they have seen it.

Set that beside the channel argument and it clicks into place. Extinguishing an authored signal does not touch the state underneath it. The state keeps outgassing. The dogs in Belfast could smell it through the sample jar.

So here is the inversion, and it is Millman's, not ours: the professional standard for a dog is that you accept what it is, meet its needs first, and never train away a signal you need. That standard is higher than the one applied behaviour analysis has held for us.

Care with this comparison. The dog-training analogy is an indictment of ABA, never a description of Autistic people as animals to be handled. It has force only because it is made by a dog trainer, about her own trade's ethics, and because she is Autistic and writing from inside our community. Both halves matter; separate them and it becomes the insult it is meant to answer.

Refusals

Not:

NotA superpower. The dog's nose is not a gift and not a moral quality. It is a working sensory system that evolved for other purposes entirely, and nothing about how a nose works is why anyone deserves care. The belonging claim in this zine is not licensed by the biology; it is only illustrated by it.
Not“Get a dog.” This is not a therapy prescription, and we have deliberately cited no outcome literature on assistance dogs, because we have not read it. A dog is an animal with needs, a lifespan and a cost, not an intervention. Nobody's access needs are met by being handed a living creature to be responsible for.
NotDogs are better than people. The finding is about a channel, not a ranking. A person who stops grading the narrow band is doing something the dog cannot do at all — it cannot decide to read you differently. The room is the thing that could change.
NotEmpathy, measured. Four dogs told two samples apart. That is discrimination. It is not interpretation, it is not concern, and it is not love — and the 2024 follow-up suggests what the dog does with our stress may be caution rather than comfort. We are not cashing a detection result as a feeling.
NotA reason your dog owes you a job. The whole force of Millman's argument is that you accept the animal first and ask second. A zine that ended in and this is what dogs are for would have reproduced exactly the extraction it spent eleven spreads objecting to.

The account, and what it is not

The account on spread two is Ryan Boren's, in his own words, and it is the reason the zine exists. It is printed as an account and not as evidence — one person, remembering, with no method and no repeat. Every measured number here comes from somebody who was measuring. The same device, for the same reason, appears in No. 92.

What we cut, and what is open

The famous study is not here. Nagasawa et al.'s oxytocin gaze loop (Science 348, 2015) is the piece of work this zine would most obviously have been built on, and it is on no spread. Published commentaries flag confounds between the dog and wolf conditions and note that the effect was not uniform across dogs. It is contested rather than refuted, and this zine needed a floor. Also cut: Albuquerque et al. (Biology Letters 12, 2016) on cross-modal recognition of human emotional expressions, which we could find neither replicated nor overturned — an absence of evidence about the evidence, and not a foundation either.

A whole spread we removed. The first outline carried the sweep — dogs have served humanity for millennia in varied roles at every scale of civilisation. The paper offering that survey (Fallahi, Masoudi, Maghsoudi & Vaez Torshizi, Veterinary Medicine and Science 10, e70012) is titled “A 40,000 years history” while its own body gives the range as “between 10,000 and 40,000 years ago” — a factor of four — and cites secondary sources for it. The confidently-assigned archaeological floor is far more recent: about 15,000 years in Europe and over 12,500 in Far East Asia (Frantz et al., Science 352, 2016). Rather than repeat a title we could not stand behind, the sweep is gone and the roles are not claimed.

What the dogs were doing is open. Wilson's four dogs were trained to alert on the stress sample and rewarded for it. Whether an untrained dog spontaneously attends to this odour, what it makes of it, and whether the 2024 caution result generalises are all unsettled. The only thing established here is that the difference is there in the sample and a nose can find it.

What this deliberately does not re-argue

No. 15 owns the dog's umwelt — scent-time, where a breeze says who passed and how long ago — and this zine borrows none of it back. No. 39 owns what masking cost, including the uncomfortable half; spread three needs only the narrow point that masking operates on the authored channel. No. 24 owns shared-air space, Helen Edgar's coinage, which is the human version of the room where the managing stops. Co-regulation is argued across twenty pages of this collection already and is not re-argued here.

A rhyme, not a proof. We are not claiming that a dog's nose settles anything about how people should be treated. We are pointing at a case where a signal everybody agreed was missing turned out to be present, legible and reliable — as soon as somebody stopped looking in the band we spend our lives performing.
