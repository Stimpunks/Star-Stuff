The Name Was
Already the Verdict

on a record with no words that was banned for its title, a title the musician did not choose, and thirty years of engineering that had already decided a deviation from the specification was damage

Thirteen links · nine documented, three contested, one leap. The chain runs on two rails — what was built, and what was heard. It crosses twice — from what was heard to what was built, and back.

The Name Was Already the Verdict · Stimpunks 1
The event · spring 1958

A record with no words in it, kept off the radio

Two and a half minutes. No lyrics. No voice at any point. Radio stations across the United States declined to play it, on the grounds that it would incite violence among teenagers — and it reached number 16 on the pop chart and number 11 on the rhythm and blues chart anyway, on jukebox and counter sales.

The Library of Congress, adding the record to the National Recording Registry in 2008, puts the mechanism in one sentence: “though Rumble contained no lyrics, many radio stations banned Rumble for its incendiary title.”

Hold that still for a second, because everything else here follows from it. A signal was transmitted that contained no propositions at all. There was nothing in it to agree or disagree with, nothing to quote in a complaint, no line anybody could point at. Whatever the objectors heard, they brought with them and left in it.

So the chain has a shape before it has any links. Something arrives that the apparatus cannot read, the apparatus reads it anyway, and the reading gets attributed to the thing.

What follows is thirteen steps back and forward from that record: to the word that was applied to it, to the accident that produced its sound, to thirty years of engineering that had already classified that sound as a fault, and to the reason a country was ready in 1958 to believe that a piece of music could make a child dangerous.

The Name Was Already the Verdict · Stimpunks 2
Link 1 · 1957–58

It was not called that when it was made

documented

The record that was banned for its title did not have that title when it was recorded. It was called Oddball.

That is not a footnote. The word that got it banned was not in the music, was not in the session, and was not the musician's. It arrived later, from outside, and the ban followed the word rather than the record.

Two labels — Capitol and Decca — turned it down. Archie Bleyer's Cadence label, best known at the time for Andy Williams and the Everly Brothers, took it on, which is not where anybody would have looked for the loudest record of 1958.

A small thing worth keeping: the Library of Congress essay writes the original title as “Originally (accurately?) the piece was called Oddball.” The parenthesis is the Library's, not ours. Somebody at the national archive of recorded sound thought the first name fit better, and put a question mark in a document that does not usually contain them.

So the chain's first link is a substitution. A descriptive name, chosen by the person who made the thing, is replaced by an evocative one chosen by somebody who heard it — and the second name is the one that gets acted on.

Link 2 · the renaming

Nobody agrees who supplied the word

contested

Two accounts, both from serious places, and they name different people.

The Library of Congress essay credits Archie Bleyer's daughter: she heard the record, liked it, and — after she and her friends decided the sound called to mind street gangs, fights and West Side Story — renamed it. The Library marks its own claim: “Legend has it that she also renamed the tune.”

Rolling Stone, in its 2005 obituary, credits Phil Everly, on the grounds that the rough sound conjured images of a street fight.

We are not going to pick. This joint is marked contested and stays that way, and the disagreement is more useful than a resolution would be — because the two accounts agree on everything that matters.

Both say the name came from the listening side rather than the making side. Both say it was chosen for an association the sound produced in somebody's head. And both describe the same operation: a texture arrives, a listener supplies a scene, and the scene gets written on the label.

Then the record is banned for the label. Whoever said the word, the word was a response to the music and was then treated as its content.

Link 3 · Fredericksburg, Virginia, 1957

The sound was an accident before it was a technique

documented

At a record hop, a local disc jockey asked the band for something the kids could do The Stroll to. The guitarist did not know the chords to the record of that name, so he improvised.

Then somebody grabbed one of the microphones and stuck it up against an amplifier.

That is the origin of the sound. Not a design, not a pedal, not an idea about rebellion — a person in a hall pointing a live microphone at a speaker and the room getting a noise nobody had planned. In Wray's account, the kids went ape, and the band played the new thing four times in a row because the crowd kept chanting for the weird song.

“Play that weird song! Play that weird song!”

— the crowd at Fredericksburg, as Wray remembered it, quoted in the Library of Congress Registry essay

Note what the room did with it, because it is the opposite of what the radio would do eight months later. The same unclassifiable noise was received as a reason to play it again. Nobody in that hall needed to know what it was.

Link 4 · the studio

He had to damage equipment to get the accident back

documented

In the studio it came out clean. Too clean, and — Wray's word — too country. The accident did not survive being recorded properly, which is a sentence worth reading twice.

So he made a hole in the equipment. In his own account, given to Guitar magazine and quoted in the Registry essay: he got a pen and started punching holes in the tweeters. “I didn't mess with the big speaker.” Plus tremolo, and a combination of the two at the end.

The version everybody prints is tidier than his. The New York Times obituary and Rolling Stone both have him poking a pencil through the speaker cone, and that is the sentence that has travelled — into Wikipedia, into a hundred articles, into our own first draft of this page.

A pen through the tweeters is a more precise act than a pencil through the cone: it is the high end he was after, and he deliberately left the woofer alone. The smoothed version is easier to say and slightly wrong, which is this collection's whole subject arriving early.

What he was doing has a plain description. He introduced a defect on purpose, in a component whose entire specification is to introduce none, because the defect was the part that meant something.

Link 5 · Hudson River, 2 August 1927

Thirty years of work had gone into removing exactly this

documented

Harold Black, six years out of college, is riding the Lackawanna Ferry across the Hudson to Bell Labs on West Street. He wants to write something down and has nothing to write on, so he uses his morning paper — one page of which happens to be blank.

The problem he is solving is stated plainly in the engineering history: the distortion and instability of vacuum-tube amplifiers, compounded when they were connected in tandem over a long-distance telephone system. Every repeater along the line added its own nonlinearity to the one before. Speech came out the far end unrecognisable.

His answer is negative feedback: take some of the output, invert it, feed it back to the input. You lose gain — a great deal of it — and in exchange the amplifier stops depending on the temperamental part of itself. Linearity is bought with amplification.

It works, and it is one of the century's genuinely great inventions. It carried long-distance telephony, fire control in the Second World War, the first operational amplifiers, and it survived the move from vacuum tubes to microelectronics intact, because it was never really about tubes.

Gain traded for linearity. The amplifier stops being the thing that decides what comes out; the feedback network does, and a network of passive parts is far less temperamental than a valve. Everything downstream of 1927 in audio engineering treats the difference between input and output as an error to be driven toward zero.

Link 6 · 1580s, and 1887

The word had held the verdict since before anyone could measure it

documented

The property Black spent his career reducing has a name, and the name is not neutral. It never was.

Distortion, in English from the 1580s: the action of distorting; the state of being twisted out of shape. From Latin distorquere — dis-, completely, plus torquere, to twist. The same torquere that gives us torque, and torture.

The technical sense — a change in the waveform of a signal by a device — arrives in 1887. So by the time Black got on the ferry, the electrical property had been carrying that word for forty years, and the word had been carrying its judgement for three hundred.

Out of shape. Not a different shape. Out of it.

— what the word has claimed since the 1580s

There is a version of this history where the property gets a flat name — nonlinearity, which is what the mathematics calls it and which asserts nothing at all. That is not the name it got. It got a name that says the signal has been twisted away from the shape it should have had, and the shape it should have had was decided by what the telephone network needed.

Which was the right decision, for the telephone network. The trouble starts when the specification stops being a description of a job and becomes a description of how sound ought to be.

Link 7 · the hinge

One measurement, two names, and only one of them is a fact

contested

Put an oscilloscope on Wray's amplifier in 1958 and on a Bell repeater in 1938 and you can measure the same thing: the output is not a scaled copy of the input. Harmonics are present that were not sent in.

That is the measurement, and it is not in dispute. Everything else on this spread is a naming decision.

Call it distortion and you have said: the device is failing to do what it is for. Call it timbre, or tone, or the sound of that amp, and you have said: the device has a character, and the character is why you chose it.

Same reading. Same physics. The difference is a claim about whose specification counts, and it is smuggled in as though it were part of the measurement.

We mark this joint contested rather than documented, because the two previous links are history and this one is an argument about history. It is the step a reader is entitled to refuse.

Two prepositions doing all the work. Not to specification is a fault report. Not in the specification is a description of something the specification never undertook to cover. The oscilloscope cannot tell them apart, because the difference is not in the signal.

Link 8 · New York, April 1954

Why a wordless record could be dangerous at all

documented

Four years before Rumble, the United States built a working apparatus for deciding that a medium was making children violent — and did it fast, in public, with a psychiatrist at the front.

Fredric Wertham's Seduction of the Innocent is published in 1954. The Senate Subcommittee on Juvenile Delinquency, chaired by Estes Kefauver, holds hearings in New York on 21–22 April and 4 June 1954, taking twenty-two witnesses — psychiatrists, publishers, retailers, cartoonists. Wertham is among the most important of them, and tells the subcommittee that Hitler was a beginner compared to the comic book industry.

The same year, the industry forestalls legislation by censoring itself: the Comics Magazine Association of America creates the Comics Code Authority, and a seal on the cover becomes the condition of distribution.

What that whole sequence establishes is a habit of mind, and the habit is the link. A medium can be judged dangerous on the strength of what an adult believes a child will do with it, without any need to demonstrate the mechanism, and the judgement can be enforced by people who are not a court.

By 1958 the country is, in the Library of Congress's phrase, “anxious over sudden spikes in juvenile delinquency” and “eager to drum out anything that could be cited as an instigator.” A record called Rumble is a thing that can be cited.

Link 9 · 2010, and 2012

The evidence under the apparatus was falsified, and it took fifty-eight years to check

documented

Wertham's personal archive was opened to researchers in 2010. In 2012 the librarian and scholar Carol L. Tilley published what she found in it.

“Seducing the Innocent: Fredric Wertham and the Falsifications That Helped Condemn Comics”, Information & Culture: A Journal of History 47(4), 2012, pages 383–413. Comparing his own case notes against the book built from them, Tilley documents that he revised children's ages, distorted their quotes, omitted other causal factors, and generally played fast and loose with the data he had gathered.

The censorship regime was real. The clinic behind it was partly invented.

— and the gap between those two sentences is fifty-eight years wide

This is the link this collection exists for. Not pseudo-science, which announces itself. This is the other thing: the authority of the clinic kept, the practice of the clinic dropped — an argument that looked like evidence, was treated as evidence, produced a code, changed an industry, and was not audited until the man had been dead for thirty years and the archive was opened.

Nothing about the method was exotic. Tilley read the notes and compared them to the book. That was available the entire time. What was missing was anybody with standing who wanted to look, and a climate in which looking would have been welcome.

Link 10 · the joint that does not hold

Did any of that actually reach this record?

contested

Here is where a chain like this wants to close itself, and where we are going to stop it.

No documented line runs from the Kefauver subcommittee to the banning of Rumble. No federal body banned the record. No code covered it. What happened is that individual stations, in Boston and New York and elsewhere, declined to play a record — which is not censorship in the legal sense at all, and is a decision each of them made privately and mostly without leaving a paper trail.

What we can say is that the Library of Congress, describing the climate, connects the two: a nation anxious about delinquency, eager to identify instigators. That is a national archive's characterisation, and it is evidence about the atmosphere rather than about the causal step.

This is the form doing its job rather than failing. A Burke chain is built to feel inevitable — that is the pleasure of the genre and it is exactly what makes it dangerous. So every joint here carries a tag, and the reader can see which ones are load-bearing history and which are ours.

If you delete this link, the chain still stands: a record was banned for a name it acquired from a listener. What you lose is the explanation of why 1958 was ready to do that, and the explanation is a good one. It is just not proved.

Link 11 · what was actually available to object to

A slang word, a texture, and a chart position

documented

Strip the record down to what a programme director in 1958 could point at, and the list is very short.

One word, on the label, supplied by a listener after the fact. One texture, which is the thing this chain has spent five links describing and which no one had vocabulary for. And the numbers: number 16 pop, number 11 rhythm and blues.

That second chart position is not decoration. The Library of Congress raises it directly: industry charts of the time show the record scoring “just as strongly, if not more so, with young black audiences, as it did with white teenagers, no doubt adding further to the furor.”

The “no doubt” is theirs and we are not adopting it. An inference about motive, however plausible, is not the same kind of object as a chart position — and this page has spent eleven links insisting on that distinction, so it does not get to relax here.

What is not an inference: number 11 R&B. A record that crossed over in both directions in 1958, at a moment when a great deal of American institutional energy was devoted to preventing exactly that.

So the objection had a word and a feeling to work with. The feeling was not admissible, so the word did the work — which is what names are for when you need to act on something you cannot articulate.

Link 12 · 1958 onward

The thing they kept off the radio became the floor

documented

Pete Townshend has said that Rumble was the reason he first picked up a guitar. Bob Dylan called it the greatest instrumental ever made.

The Library of Congress essay lists the rest in one breath: the Kinks, the Cramps, Jeff Beck, Jimmy Page, Jimi Hendrix, Eric Clapton, Marc Bolan, PJ Harvey, Neil Young, Bruce Springsteen, Paul McCartney. The deliberate defect became the default, and within fifteen years an amplifier that could not produce it was a limitation rather than a virtue.

The Registry added the record in 2008. The Rock and Roll Hall of Fame inducted Link Wray in 2023, in the Musical Influence category — eighteen years after he died, sixty-five after the ban.

One more thing, and we are going to leave it where the record leaves it. Wray's mother was Shawnee, and the Library of Congress states it plainly; he described his childhood as hiding from Klan raids, and said he grew up “Shawnee poor” where Elvis grew up white-man poor. Census records for the family say White. Both of those are true statements about the documentary record and we are not resolving them here.

Our rack We Were Punk First handles that question at length. This chain notes only the part that belongs to it: for most of the sixty-five years, almost nobody outside the family knew.

Link 13 · the leap

A deviation from the specification, read from outside

leap

Here is the step this chain has been walking toward, and it is a leap. We are marking it, and the marking is not a formality.

Everything up to here is about amplifiers, archives and radio programmers. What follows is about people, and an analogy is not an inference. Nothing in the history of audio engineering establishes anything whatsoever about human beings.

With that said. The pattern this chain has traced has four parts, and all four are documented:

One. A property is measured accurately. Two. A specification, written for a particular job, describes what the measurement should have been. Three. The gap between them is given a name that contains a verdict — twisted out of shape — and the verdict is carried inside the name rather than argued for. Four. Enormous, genuinely brilliant institutional effort goes into eliminating the gap, and the effort is largely right, because the specification was right about the job.

And then a person finds that the thing being eliminated is the part that carries who is playing.

The measurement was never the argument. The specification was the argument, and it arrived already named.

— the leap, stated so it can be refused

We are not going to tell you what to do with that. This site has a view, and the view is on every other page. What this page is for is showing you a case where the whole sequence is on the record — the memo, the ferry, the etymology, the hearings, the falsified case notes, the ban, the chart position, the induction sixty-five years late — and where nobody had to be a villain for it to happen.

What the specification was actually for

The telephone specification was correct. Long-distance speech needs the far end to sound like the near end, repeaters in tandem compound their errors, and Harold Black's answer to that was excellent engineering that we all still use. Nothing in this chain is an argument against it.

The chain is about what happened to the word afterwards. A specification is a description of a job. It is not a description of how the world should be, and the moment its vocabulary escapes the job it was written for, it starts issuing verdicts it was never entitled to issue. Distortion escaped in 1887 and has been at large since.

Not an argument against measurement, engineering or specifications. Black's amplifier is one of the great inventions and this page says so twice. The collection this zine belongs to exists to reclaim science from the things that wear its clothes, not to be suspicious of it.
Not a claim that amplifiers tell you anything about people. Spread 15 is tagged leap for that reason, and it is the only leap here.
Not a story with a villain. The radio programmers were frightened, the Senate was responding to real public alarm, and Wertham believed he was protecting children. The falsification is documented; the malice is not, and we are not supplying it.
Not a resolution of who Link Wray was. The documentary record disagrees with itself and we have left it disagreeing.
