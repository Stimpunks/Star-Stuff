The teaching

A father told
his daughter

The sentence this zine is about did not arrive as a slogan. It arrived as something a man said to his child, more than once, the way you say a thing you want someone to still have after you are gone.

Jessica Hernandez is Maya Ch'orti' and Binnizá — Zapotec — an environmental scientist who writes about Indigenous science and the landscapes it heals. In Fresh Banana Leaves she sets the teaching down in her father's voice, not her own:

“He always told me that as long as we protect nature, nature will protect us.”Jessica Hernandez, Fresh Banana Leaves, 2022 — reporting her father

It sits inside a larger thing he taught her: that our animal and plant relatives are watching over us too, and that the more we take care of them, the more they take care of us. Later she restates it in her own voice — “nature protects us as long as we protect nature” — and adds that this is something Western science has failed to understand or explain.

Keep the chain. A teaching has a direction and a household, and both are part of what it means. Strip them and you get a bumper sticker; the words survive and the relation they came out of does not.

Why this matters before anything else. A great deal of what follows depends on this being a specific teaching from a specific tradition, transmitted person to person — not a general truth about Nature that anyone may pick up. We are quoting a daughter quoting her father, and that is how it will be cited on every page here.

The conditional

Three words we
have heard before

Read the sentence again and notice what it opens with. As long as.

For a lot of us that is not a neutral phrase. It is the exact grammar in which belonging has been offered to us our whole lives, and always with the same shape: you may stay, on terms.

As long as you can make eye contact. As long as you keep up. As long as the accommodation doesn't cost anything. As long as you're the good kind. As long as you don't stim in the meeting, don't go non-speaking at the wrong moment, don't need the thing you need twice in one week. As long as you're not too much.

Every one of those is a conditional, and every one of them means the same thing underneath: your place here is a payment plan, and you are always one missed instalment from losing it.

So when a sentence arrives saying as long as we protect nature, nature will protect us, a lot of us hear a contract — and hearing it is not a failure of reading. It is a well-trained ear doing exactly what it learned to do. The flinch is correct. It is just aimed at the wrong sentence.

If you have been offered belonging on terms, you learn to hear the terms first. That reflex has kept people safe. It is worth keeping — and worth checking.

Figure 2 · Conditional belonging. The reflex it trains is a good one.

Able, not willing

One word settles
the whole thing

A few pages after the teaching, Hernandez extends it — and the extension is where the contract reading dies. She writes that as long as we continue to remove ourselves from nature, nature will not be able to protect us from environmental impacts.

Able. Not willing. Not prepared to. Not obliged to, having received nothing.

That is a claim about capacity, and a contract has nothing to do with capacity. A party to a contract who is not paid withholds. It still could; it declines to. That is what makes it leverage, and leverage is what makes the conditional frightening.

What Hernandez describes is the other thing entirely. Remove yourself far enough from a system and it does not start refusing you — it simply can no longer reach you. The protection was never a service being rendered. It was a property of being inside the relation, and you have stepped outside it.

Nobody is withholding. Nobody is keeping the account. There is no gate, and there is no gatekeeper, and the thing you were braced for was never there.

Not a payment withheld. A relation severed.
Nature is not refusing to pay us. We walked out of the room.

A sibling to this argument. The Rest You Keep (No. 59) makes the same move against a ledger — rest is not a reward released after enough work, because nothing was ever keeping score. This one makes it against a contract. Different machinery, and both of them missing.

Figure 3 · Withheld, or unreachable. Only one of them is a threat.

Attention

Three qualities,
one of them hedged

If the relation is not a contract, what is it made of? Barry Lopez answered that in 1997, in an essay called A Literature of Place, and he was careful in a way that matters.

He names three qualities that make a lasting connection to a place: paying intimate attention; a storied relationship to a place rather than a solely sensory awareness of it; and living in some sort of ethical unity with a place.

Read that third one slowly. Some sort of. Lopez is describing something he is sure is there and not sure how to name, and he leaves the uncertainty in the sentence where a reader can see it. That hedge is not weakness. It is a writer refusing to sound more certain than he is about somebody else's relationship to the ground.

Start with the first. Paying intimate attention is the whole entry point, and it is also — read from where we are standing — a description of monotropic attention: a deep single channel that goes far into one thing rather than thinly across many. Lopez calls it the beginning of connection. We get told it is a symptom.

The same attention. Two completely different names, depending on who is doing it and whether anyone thought to ask what it was for.

The child who can tell you every stone in one creek is not failing to be interested in the world. They are doing the first of Lopez's three things, and doing it well.

The Place Knows You're There · Stimpunks 5
Relational

Where Lopez
got it

Here is the part that usually gets left out, and it changes the shape of everything.

In the paragraph immediately before the three qualities, Lopez explains where he found them. He travels to remote places and depends on interpreters, and, he writes, “eminent among such helpers are indigenous people” — for whom he then gives three reasons: they attend more closely to nuance in the physical world and see more; their history in a place runs deep, which gives a spatial landscape a dimension in time; and they tend to occupy the same moral universe as the land they sense.

Then, and only then, the three qualities. The framework is a description of a relationship Lopez was shown. He says so himself, in order, on the page.

Which means Hernandez is not a supporting citation propping up a Lopez idea. It is the other way round. She is the tradition, speaking for itself, in the first person, without needing an interpreter.

And notice what happens to the hedge. Lopez, honestly and from outside, writes some sort of ethical unity. Hernandez's father, from inside, needs no qualifier at all. He just tells his daughter how it is.

“Indigenous people tend to occupy the same moral universe as the land they sense. Their bonds with the earth are as much moral and biological.”Barry Lopez, A Literature of Place, 1997 — the paragraph before the three qualities
“As long as we protect nature, nature will protect us.”Hernandez's father — the same claim, from inside, unhedged
“Many of us, I think, long to become the companion of a place, not its authority, not its owner.”Barry Lopez, A Literature of Place, 1997

The order of citation is the argument. It is easy — and common — to quote Lopez's three qualities as a tidy framework and then reach for an Indigenous writer as illustration. That inverts the direction the knowledge actually travelled, and Lopez's own paragraph is the evidence against doing it. We put him second here on purpose.

What it is for

The place knows
you're there

Lopez does not leave the three qualities hanging as an aesthetic. He says what they are for, and the answer is not stewardship, or virtue, or a healthier planet, though he cares about all three.

He says they are “a fundamental human defense against loneliness.”

And then he says how it works — which is the sentence this zine is named after:

“If you're intimate with a place, a place with whose history you're familiar, and you establish an ethical conversation with it, the implication that follows is this: the place knows you're there. It feels you. You will not be forgotten, cut off, abandoned.”Barry Lopez, A Literature of Place, 1997

Sit with what is being claimed. Not you will feel better. Not you will have communed with something. Something much more specific: you will be known. Somewhere will hold the fact of you.

That is a claim about recognition — and recognition is the thing a great many of us have been going without in rooms full of people.

Held honestly. Lopez writes the implication that follows, and the sentences after it are the shape of a felt conviction, not a measurement. We are not claiming a creek has a mind, or that it registers you the way a friend does. The claim on this page is about what the relation does to the person in it — and that part is not mystical at all.

Lived experience

Lonely in a
room full of people

Lopez said loneliness. So let us be precise about which loneliness, because there is a version of it that the usual advice cannot touch.

Hari Srinivasan's Presence Without Belonging (2026) is about exactly this. Autistic loneliness, he argues, is routinely misread as social isolation or low social motivation — when in fact it very often happens in the middle of ongoing social participation. We are at the job, in the class, at the table. And still not there.

His framework separates presence from belonging, and names three conditions belonging actually requires:

Recognition — being understood and taken seriously as a social subject. Access — being able to participate without extraordinary communicative, sensory or temporal effort. Sustainability — participation you can keep up over time without disproportionate cumulative cost.

Take away any one and you get presence without belonging: contact without connection, which is lonelier than being alone and much harder to explain to anyone.

Now hold that beside Lopez. He is describing a relationship that delivers recognition — the place knows you're there — and it asks no mask for it, charges no extraordinary entry cost, and runs on the kind of attention we already have rather than the kind we have to perform.

That is not nature being therapeutic. That is a relation whose access requirements we happen to meet.

Environment

No word for
conservation

Hernandez makes an observation about her own language that does more work than a whole shelf of argument.

In the Zapotec variant her family speaks, there is no word that translates directly to conservation. The nearest they could arrive at are gapanú — to take care of something — and rianda — to heal. Neither one carries what conservation means. And she draws the conclusion herself: conservation was not needed before colonization, so Indigenous languages can serve as indicators of what has been introduced into our lands.

She therefore chooses to say healing rather than conservation, because healing is what her culture actually holds.

Think about what a missing word is evidence of. Not a gap in the language. A gap in the problem. You do not build a word for setting a bone in a world where nobody falls.

And this is the same move the whole zine has been making, arriving from a different direction. A vocabulary of protection-as-transaction — conserve, offset, manage, steward the resource — is not the older layer that got covered over. It is the newer one. The relation came first, and it did not need contract language, because it was never a contract.

Not a claim about all Indigenous languages. This is one variant of Zapotec, described by one speaker about her own family's speech, and she says plainly that languages adapt and vocabularies grow. It is evidence about a specific language, offered by someone entitled to offer it — not a general law about Indigenous thought.

Systems

The straw ban,
and who paid for it

Hernandez has one more thing to say, and it is the reason this zine belongs on a site about disability rather than a site about nature.

She argues that Indigenous holistic thinking is not systems thinking, because many Indigenous communities do not divide the world into systems in the first place. Western knowledge separates everything into systems and boxes — which is why the environmental sciences fan out into an array of disciplines that do not speak to each other.

And then she gives the example, and it is ours: Seattle's 2018 ban on plastic straws. An environmental solution, designed inside the environment box. Outside that box, disabled people lost access to an essential tool. She quotes Alice Wong on why plastic straws are essential — the harm was documented by the people it landed on, as it always is.

Her point is not that the ban was well-meant but clumsy. It is structural: when everything is separated into systems, more harm can be done with the solutions that are thought of. The box is what produces the harm. Draw the boundary and everything outside it becomes somebody else's department.

Which is the same severance the whole zine has been circling. Remove ourselves from the relation and nature cannot reach us. Remove disabled people from the environmental box and the solution cannot see us. Same cut, two casualties.

An environmental policy that harms disabled people is not a trade-off between two goods. It is one failure — the failure to have been in relation with everyone the decision touches.

Held honestly

What this zine
refuses

This is an argument about belonging that runs through land, and that is a road with a great many potholes in it. Here are the ones we are steering around, named so you can hold us to them.

The largest is the easiest to fall into and the hardest to see from inside: the slide from a place can offer recognition to nature is good for you. The second sentence sounds like the first being friendly. It is not. It turns a relation back into a service, which is precisely the thing spread 4 spent its whole length taking apart.

And one that is ours specifically. An argument that we find recognition outdoors must never become another room with a door on it. Plenty of us cannot get to a creek — because of pain, money, transport, safety, a body that does not do stairs or heat or distance, a neighbourhood with no green in it. A windowbox counts. A pigeon on a ledge counts. One tree you know the state of, all year, counts. Lopez's requirement is intimacy, not acreage.

Nota contract. Protection is not a payment we render and nature returns. As long as here describes a relation's reach, not its terms. Nobody is keeping the account.
Notnature as therapy. We are not prescribing the outdoors, and a place is not a treatment that works on us. Extracting wellbeing from land is the same logic as extracting anything else from it.
Not“neurodivergent people are closer to nature.” That is the noble-savage move pointed at us, and it has been used to explain why we belong outside rather than in the building. We are not more natural. The access requirements simply differ.
Notspeaking for other beings. The collection's standing refusal, and it applies double here: saying a place knows you are there is not a claim to know what it would say.
Notthe naturalistic fallacy. Nothing here argues that because the relation is old or natural, it is therefore right, or that your belonging is licensed by an ecosystem. The universe does not owe us a precedent.
Nota general Indigenous wisdom. Every claim on these pages belongs to a named person from a named tradition — a Zapotec and Maya Ch'orti' scientist, quoting her father. Not a mood. Not a category.
Notone more thing to be failing at. If this reads as a standard of connection you are not meeting, put it down. It is a description of something available, not an assignment.

The protection was never a payment, and the belonging was never on terms. Remove yourself far enough and nothing refuses you — it simply cannot reach. So stay in reach of something. One creek, one tree, one ledge. Love you down to your star stuff — and down to the ground you are standing on.

Reflection

Which place already knows you're there? Not the one you think ought to — the one that does.

Where in your life has belonging been offered with an as long as attached? What were the terms, and who set them?

Lopez's first quality is intimate attention. What do you already pay that kind of attention to — and who taught you to call it a problem?

Recognition, access, sustainability. Pick a room you are in every week. Which of the three is missing?

A correction, and it is ours. Lopez's three qualities circulate widely — including in our own Stimpunks glossary, and in a Stimpunks post — under the name “reciprocal ethical unity,” presented as his framework. He never wrote that phrase. What he wrote is “paying intimate attention; a storied relationship to a place rather than a solely sensory awareness of it; and living in some sort of ethical unity with a place.” The word reciprocal appears in the next paragraph, in a question he asks on the reader's behalf — “How can you find such a reciprocity?” — and again later, in “an ethical and reciprocal relationship with everything around us.” So the familiar phrase is a portmanteau of two separate moves, and in fusing them it does something worse than compress: it hardens Lopez's deliberate hedge, some sort of, into a term of art, and converts a question into a listed quality. We carried the compressed version ourselves before checking the essay, which is the only reason we can describe how it happens. The glossary entries are flagged for the same fix. Related, and found in the same pass: a Stimpunks post attributes to Lopez the line about living “as if your children's future mattered” — that is Robin Wall Kimmerer, in Braiding Sweetgrass, and it is not quoted here because we have not yet confirmed it against the book rather than against quotation sites.

Honesty notes. Three things held loosely. First: Lopez writes “the place knows you're there. It feels you” as an implication that follows — the shape of a conviction, not a measurement, and no claim is made here that a landscape registers a person the way a person does. What is claimed is what the relation does to the human in it. Second: the pairing of Lopez's loneliness claim with Srinivasan's framework is ours, not theirs. Lopez died in 2020 and never read Srinivasan; Srinivasan's paper is about human social participation and says nothing about land. Two authors, twenty-nine years apart, are being put in a room together by us — the resemblance is a rhyme, not a result, and the columns in Figure 6 are our reading. Third: reading Lopez's “intimate attention” as monotropic attention is likewise our move and not his; monotropism is a theory of Autistic attention, not a theory of place, and the overlap is a description we find useful rather than a finding either literature reports.

You do not have to earn the ground. Go and be known by something. Love you down to your star stuff.
