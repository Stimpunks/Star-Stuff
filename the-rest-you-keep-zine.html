The finding

Nobody files
a report

Hydra is a real animal, not a thought experiment. A freshwater creature a centimetre or two long, related to jellyfish and coral, usually found clinging by its foot to a pond plant with a ring of tentacles at the other end.

It has no brain. No central nervous system at all — just a loose mesh of nerve cells spread through the body, with nothing anywhere gathering that mesh into a decision-maker.

And Hydra sleeps. It goes still. It is harder to rouse. Keep it awake and it sleeps longer afterwards to make up the debt, and falls asleep quicker when you let it — which is the homeostatic signature, the thing that separates sleep from merely sitting there. Keep it awake long enough and the number of dividing cells drops off.

So rest is not a bonus feature bolted onto a body's real work. In an animal with no brain to argue the point, rest is part of how the body gets built and stays working at all.

Which puts the usual story in a strange position. The assumption that rest must be earned — tracked, justified, released once enough has been put in — requires something keeping score. A ledger. An account with a balance.

Hydra has no ledger. It rests anyway.

Held honestly, and early. This is not the argument that Hydra rests, therefore your rest is natural, therefore it is allowed. Using natural to mean permitted is its own trap, and this collection refuses it everywhere else — the universe does not owe us a precedent, and a resemblance is a rhyme, not a proof. Hydra is not evidence that you are allowed to rest. Hydra is evidence that the thing you were asking permission from was never there.

Kanaya et al. 2020 · Science Advances
a sleep-like state, in an animal with no brain

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 2
Attention

The tunnel,
not the line

One more thing about Hydra, because it is the hinge. Put it in constant light or constant dark and its daily rhythm vanishes — it has no circadian clock to free-run. What is left is a cycle of roughly four hours, returning and returning.

So the oldest resting animal we can point to is not keeping time against a day. It rests on a rhythm of its own that comes back around.

Which is the shape a lot of us already know. Autistic attention often does not spread thinly across many things at once; it pools, and goes deep into a few. That is monotropism — Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser and Wenn Lawson, 2005 — and time inside that kind of focus has its own shape. Not slower or faster. Helen Edgar calls it spiral time: not a line you are falling behind on, but a spiral you are moving through, returning to the same place on purpose.

Now the consequence, which is the whole reason this spread exists. If rest is offered by pulling that attention away — the meditation app, the walk, the gentle activity, when what the mind actually wants is stillness with the interest still in view — then it is not rest. It is another demand, wearing a rest costume.

Rest that fights your attention is not rest.Helen Edgar · Reclaiming Rest (2025)

For attention that works this way, rest often means staying in the tunnel. Going further into the one thing. Not being pulled out of it and told to relax.

What this is not. Hydra's four-hour cycle is not monotropic time, and no shared mechanism is being claimed — one is a cnidarian ultradian rhythm and the other is how human attention is organised. The rhyme is worth noticing and it is not evidence. The monotropic account stands on its own literature, and on us.

Murray, Lesser & Lawson 2005 · Edgar on monotropic time
deeper in is a direction, not a retreat

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 3
On the wing

Rest is not the
opposite of motion

A great frigatebird can stay in the air for up to two months without landing. Weimerskirch and colleagues tracked them across the Indian Ocean, riding thermals up and gliding down in a roller-coaster that costs them almost nothing. That is one study, and it is about where the birds go.

A different team asked a different question of the same species: what is the brain doing up there? They fitted EEG recorders and followed non-stop foraging flights of up to ten days and three thousand kilometres — different birds on different trips, not the two-month records. Over those flights the birds slept an average of 42 minutes a day. Back on land: over twelve hours.

The detail that matters is how. Slow-wave sleep arrived in bouts that could run several minutes; the REM episodes lasted only seconds. It was more often one hemisphere at a time in the air than it ever is on land, with the open eye generally facing the direction of the turn — though both hemispheres did sometimes go under together, so half a brain awake is not a requirement for staying up. And it happened while the birds soared on rising air, never while they flapped.

So rest here did not require stillness. It required a system that could rest part of itself while another part kept working — and a glide, rather than a wingbeat, to do it in.

It slept in the glide. Nothing stopped.

Two studies, two claims, and not a target. The two-month figure and the 42-minute figure come from different work and are not one finding; the sleep study's own flights ran up to ten days. And 42 minutes is sleep restriction, not an achievement — the birds catch up on land, and nothing here suggests a body thrives on less. The point is narrower: rest was possible mid-flight, in pieces, without the motion stopping.

Rattenborg et al. 2016 · Weimerskirch et al. 2016
part of a system rests while the rest keeps flying

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 4
Relational

Partial presence is
real presence

Lurking in a group chat without posting. Camera off. Texting instead of calling. Reading every message and answering none of them today. Being in the room with the sound down.

None of that is absence.

What gets counted in each of those cases is output — messages sent, words spoken, a face on screen. But absence is a claim about somebody not being there, and not one of those is that. They are all being there, with the output turned down.

Every Saturday the Stimpunks Solidarity Session leaves an otter cam running while the conversation drifts in and out. Some cameras on, most off. Some people speaking, some in text, some just there. Nobody has to watch it. That is the part that makes it work — and the reason it works is that the room does not price presence by output.

Because a relationship that only holds when everyone shows up at full intensity was never built to hold a nervous system that varies. And they all vary. Ours vary visibly, which is a difference in legibility, not in kind.

Presence does not need full output to count as presence.

No animal on this spread, on purpose. Every other spread here has one, and the obvious move was to set the frigatebird's half-lit brain beside a camera-off Saturday and let the resemblance carry the claim. That is the metaphor this zine refuses five spreads later — and it would misdescribe the bird, which sleeps about 42 minutes a day in short bouts inside its glides, never while flapping, and is awake for the rest of the flight rather than cruising along permanently half-lit. The distinction is Helen Edgar's, from No. 58: the otters are evidence; we are testimony. This spread is testimony. It does not need a bird standing behind it.

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 5
Lived experience

No ramp,
just the edge

Interoception is the sense that reports the inside of your own body. Hunger. Heartbeat. Thirst. Tiredness arriving.

For a lot of us that signal comes through quiet, or late, or in a shape that is hard to read. Which means the depletion does not announce itself while it builds. You feel fine. Then you do not. There is often no slope in between.

Meltdown and shutdown live at that edge. They are not chosen and they are not a rest strategy — they are what a nervous system does when the cost has already been accumulating and no warning arrived in time to spend it differently. An emergency stop, where a signal should have come first.

It would be easy to read that as proof that rest is optional if you are only careful enough — that with better monitoring you could time it precisely. It is proof of the opposite. Missing the signal costs too much.

Rest cannot wait for a warning that may never come.

Said plainly, because the framing gets this wrong. This zine does not call meltdown or shutdown a form of rest. Describing them as enforced rest makes an injury sound like a strategy, and then makes the person responsible for having needed it. They are the cost of rest having been unavailable. Everything else in here is about the rest that gets chosen, before the edge.

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 6
Six months, no load

The bear's
bone

Bone answers to load. That is why this whole collection opens with Iwao Yasuda's finding that a bone under pressure generates a charge — a skeleton is not scaffolding, it is a tissue that reads what is being asked of it and rebuilds accordingly.

Which sets up a problem. Take the load away and bone is supposed to go. Put a person on months of bed rest, or in orbit, and it does: resorption outruns formation and the skeleton thins measurably.

A hibernating bear goes roughly six months with no walking, no weight-bearing, none of the loading that is supposed to keep a skeleton honest.

It loses no bone. Researchers compared hibernating and active grizzlies and black bears and found no difference in bone volume fraction or mineral density. Cellular activity dropped — but resorption and formation dropped together, staying balanced. The bear does not out-work the problem. It turns the remodelling down at both ends and comes out in spring with its skeleton intact.

Long rest cost this animal nothing structural. So the loss is not a law.

A bear is not a person, and this is not advice. Humans do lose bone in prolonged disuse; nothing here suggests skipping movement, and the researchers study bears precisely because we cannot do what they do. The claim is narrow and it is about the rule, not the routine: there exists a physiology in which half a year of stillness is not paid for in skeleton. What looked like an inevitable trade is a fact about particular bodies in particular arrangements.

McGee-Lawrence et al. 2009 · Bone
not pushing through — turning it down evenly

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 7
Environment

Rest is a property
of an arrangement

The bear's skeleton is doing its own work from the inside. The den is doing a different job, and that one is measurable in the energy budget.

A hibernating black bear drops its metabolic rate to about a quarter of basal while its core temperature falls only five or six degrees, and its heart runs as low as nine beats a minute. That is the striking part of Tøien's work: the metabolic suppression is largely uncoupled from the cooling. Deep rest here is not the body going cold and switching off.

Holding that state still costs something, and the den is what lowers the bill. Bears select dens for insulation — buried, snow-covered, sheltered from wind and disturbance — and a well-insulated den measurably reduces what has to be spent on staying warm. The animal's contribution is to be somewhere that will hold the conditions steady, and then to stop.

So rest is not only a capacity a body either has or lacks. It is what happens when a body and its surroundings are arranged so that resting is possible without effort being spent on making it possible. Temperature. Darkness. Safety. Nobody needing anything right now.

Change the surroundings and the same body can no longer do what it just did.

Two findings, deliberately not merged. The den lowers the energy cost of hibernating; the preserved bone is an internal remodelling result. Nobody has shown that a worse den costs a bear its skeleton, and this spread does not imply it — the bone would likely hold in a poor den and the animal would simply pay more to be there. Keeping them apart is the honest version, and it is the stronger one: the body and the room are each carrying a documented share.

Tøien et al. 2011 · Science · den microclimate literature
the arrangement carries part of the cost

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 8
Systems

The debt that was
never owed

Underneath most of how rest gets talked about sits an earning frame: rest as something released after enough output. A balance paid down. Never quite yet.

Tricia Hersey, who founded The Nap Ministry, names what that frame is doing:

Rest is a form of resistance because it disrupts and pushes back against capitalism and white supremacy.Tricia Hersey · Rest Is Resistance: A Manifesto (2022)

Her ground is specific and it is not ours to blur: rest stolen from Black people under slavery and its long afterlives, and a grind culture that reads bodies as machinery. She names what the machinery story is:

Rest pushes back and disrupts a system that views human bodies as a tool for production and labor. It is a counter narrative. We know that we are not machines.Tricia Hersey · The Nap Ministry, 21 February 2022

A counter narrative. That is the precise word for it, because the earning story is a narrative and not a measurement — and the argument is a Black liberationist one before it is anything else.

What it hands us is the shape of the accounting. If rest must be earned, someone is holding the ledger, and whoever holds it decides who is behind. That is a mechanism for withholding, and it works best on people whose output is already read as suspect — which is to say, on us, routinely, from the first time a report card described us as not applying ourselves.

And this is where Hydra comes back. There is no ledger in a nerve net. The debt was not discovered in the body; it was issued — and a debt that was issued can be refused.

Not flattened into wellness. Hersey's rest is a demand made from inside Black liberation struggle, not a self-care tip and not a general theory we get to lift clean. We cite her because the accounting she describes is the same accounting we are handed, not because our situation is hers.

Hersey 2022 · The Nap Ministry
rest as refusal, not as recovery time

The Rest You Keep · Stimpunks 9
Held honestly

What this zine
refuses

Every piece in this series ends by naming what it will not claim. The refusals are load-bearing: a zine about rest is unusually easy to turn into its opposite, and most of the ways that happens are kind-sounding.

Rest gets recommended to us constantly. Almost none of it survives contact with how our attention actually works, and a surprising amount of it arrives as one more thing to do properly.

If it needs to be justified afterwards, it was not rest. It was unpaid work with the lights off.
Not:Rest so you can produce. Hydra is not recharging in order to divide more efficiently on Monday. Rest is not a maintenance interval on a working machine, and framing it that way just moves the ledger.
Not:An animal metaphor for people. The bear is not a model of who to become and the frigatebird is not an instruction. They are kin, doing their own thing, in bodies that are not ours.
Not:“Just relax.” Offered as advice it is usually a demand — for stillness that fits somebody else's nervous system, delivered to someone with no spare capacity to perform it.
Not:Rest as a project. Optimised, tracked, done correctly, reviewed. That is the grind with a softer noun on it.
Not:Meltdown as rest. Calling a shutdown enforced rest turns an injury into a strategy, and then hands the person the bill for having needed it.
Not:Natural, therefore allowed. The animals are not a permission slip. Nature grants nobody anything, and a belonging claim resting on biology can be revoked by the next paper.
Not:The smoothed version. The hedges in here are not padding — no circadian rhythm in Hydra, two months and 42 minutes from two different studies, a bear that is not a person. The true versions are stranger and they are enough.

Reflection

Where does your attention already want to go? What would it mean to let rest happen there, instead of somewhere more respectable?

What is the den you would need — the arrangement, not the willpower — for your body to hold steady the way the bear's does?

What debt have you been paying down that was never actually owed? Who issued it, and when did you start believing the balance?

When did you last rest without preparing an account of why you were entitled to?

Honesty notes. Four things this zine holds loosely, and one correction. First: the authors title Hydra's state a sleep-like state before calling it sleep, and that hedge is kept here — what is established is that it meets the behavioural criteria, not that a polyp experiences anything. Second: Hydra's four-hour rhythm is not monotropic time and no shared mechanism is claimed; the resemblance is a rhyme, not a proof. Third: the two frigatebird figures come from two separate studies and are not one result — the two-month flights are Weimerskirch, the 42 minutes is Rattenborg over flights of up to ten days — and 42 minutes a day is sleep restriction the birds recover from on land, not a standard to admire. Corrected after shipping: spread 4 first said about seven in ten in-flight bouts were asymmetric. That number came from a search summary rather than a paper and appears in neither Rattenborg source — the 2019 review says nearly half of the asymmetric sleep passed the unihemispheric threshold, a different claim over a different denominator. The page now states only what the authors state, and the flapping detail is added because that review is Rattenborg reviewing his own data. Fourth: a bear is not a person. Humans do lose bone during prolonged disuse, which is exactly why bears are studied; the claim made here is that the loss is not a universal law, not that anyone should stop moving. Relatedly, the den's documented job is reducing the energy cost of hibernating — no one has shown that den quality affects the bone result, and the two findings are kept apart on spreads 7 and 8 rather than merged into a tidier story. The corrections, and there are two, both about where a real Hersey sentence actually lives. Rest disrupting capitalism and white supremacy is widely cited — and was cited to us — as We Will Rest! (2024). It is in Rest Is Resistance: A Manifesto (2022), in the passage naming Rest Is Resistance the Nap Ministry’s tagline and mantra. The counter narrative passage on spread 9 was attributed to the 2024 book too; it is in neither book, but in a Nap Ministry post of 21 February 2022 — two years before We Will Rest! existed. Both were settled by opening the sources rather than by finding a third site that agreed. The circulated form of that second passage also drops two sentences without marking the omission — It is a counter narrative and We are divine — and the first of those turns out to be the load-bearing one, so it is quoted here whole. And nothing here claims the animals' arrangements justify ours — the universe does not owe us a precedent. The belonging claim does not rest on Hydra. It rests on us, and it reaches all the way down.

You are not behind. There was no schedule. Rest, and keep resting. Love you down to your star stuff.
