Saturdays, 10am Central

Nobody has
to watch

Every Saturday the Stimpunks Solidarity Session leaves an otter cam running while the conversation drifts in and out. Some cameras on, most off. Some people speaking, some in text, some just there. Nobody has to watch it. That is the part that makes it work.

Our co-creative director Helen Edgar wrote down what the cam was doing for her: on a day when listening and speaking were both off the table, it gave her somewhere to put her attention and a reason to stay in the room — a way to exist in parallel with people she wanted to be near.

Then she noticed the resemblance. The otters float, juggle a stone, somersault off alone for a while, drift back for a minute of shared play, and peel away again into their own absorption. Which is, more or less, the shape of the session. And the shape of a lot of our lives.

Maybe it isn't only that otters are soothing to watch. Maybe they offer an embodied version of something our community already knows and lives.Helen Edgar, Stimpunks Solidarity and Otters (2026)

The Stone You Keep · Stimpunks 2
The finding

There is no
standard otter

Here is what happens when you actually follow one sea otter population closely enough to tell the individuals apart. Where food was plentiful, everybody ate more or less the same things. Where food was scarce, the population did not converge on one best way to be an otter. It fanned out.

Distinct individuals settled into distinct diets — one on snails, one on clams, one on urchins, one on crabs — and each carried a whole matched suite of behaviours to go with it: where to dive, how deep, how long, what to do on the surface. And the crucial part: each specialist was measurably better at its own prey than a generalist would have been.

That is not variation as noise around an average. It is variation as the answer. The averaged otter — snail-and-clam-and-urchin-and-crab, all at once, moderately — is a number, not an animal. It forages nowhere.

A population of specialists is not a population that failed to standardise.

Held honestly. The fanning-out showed up under food limitation — the crowded, hungry site, not the comfortable one. Scarcity is not a gift and we are not going to call it one. What the finding says is narrower and better: when the pressure came, difference was what let the population keep eating.

Tinker, Bentall & Estes 2008
specialisation, not a spread around a mean

The Stone You Keep · Stimpunks 3
Point Lobos, 1964

The stone is not
a metaphor

Two observers sat on the California coast for six days and wrote down thirty separate instances of a sea otter using a tool. The otters floated on their backs, laid a stone on the chest, and pounded shellfish against it. The same stone was frequently kept and reused across one food item after another.

They also counted the blows. A mussel took an average of 35.5 strikes to open — and the range ran from 6 to 88.

Sit with that range. It is the same animal, the same tool, the same job. One otter is done in six. Another is still going at eighty-eight. Nobody is doing it wrong. There simply is no correct number of blows, and an average of 35.5 describes no single mussel that was ever opened.

Helen read the kept stone as a stim tool: something reliable and joyful to return to, the way a special interest or a sensory object works — regulation through flow. The reading holds. And the stone is not standing in for anything. It is a rock, and it is load-bearing.

There is no correct number of blows. There is your number of blows.

Hall & Schaller 1964 · 30 instances, 6 days
the range is the finding

The Stone You Keep · Stimpunks 4
The part that matters most

Some otters never
use a stone

Follow 196 tagged sea otters over years and tool use turns out to vary enormously between individuals. Some reach for a rock constantly. Some essentially never do.

The non-users are not the otters who haven't figured it out yet. They specialise on prey a stone wouldn't help with — urchins, crabs, smaller bivalves that open under a bite. The frequent tool users are on the snails and clams that don't. Two routes to a fed animal, and the route is chosen by what you're built and practised for.

Then the finding that should be printed on a wall somewhere: the tool users showed less tooth damage. Cracking hard shells with your teeth costs you your teeth. Using the rock spares them.

So the accommodation is not a shortcut, and it is not a crutch, and it is not less than doing it unaided. It is the difference between arriving at old age with your teeth and arriving without them. The support is legible in the skeleton.

An accommodation is not a discount on the task. It is what keeps the body intact while the task gets done.

Law et al. 2024, Science
the tool shows up in the skeleton

The Stone You Keep · Stimpunks 5
Under the calm

What it costs
to float

A sea otter on its back in cold water is one of the most restful images available to a human being. It is also, thermodynamically, a furnace.

Sea otters have no blubber — the one marine mammal that went back to the sea and never grew the fat layer. They insulate with fur instead, the densest coat measured on any animal. And even that is not uniform: sample different places on one otter's body and the density runs from around 26,000 to 165,000 hairs per square centimetre. Even the record-holding coat is not the same coat all over.

Fur alone still isn't enough. Their resting metabolic rate runs about three times what an animal of that size should need — and the heat comes from leak in the skeletal muscle itself, energy deliberately not captured, spent as warmth. It is running in newborn pups before their muscles can do anything else. They are warm from the inside, from birth, on purpose.

The otter is not performing serenity, and it is not secretly grinding. It is simply that what it takes to be that body in that water is invisible from the shore.

The calm surface is not the whole cost. It never was, for anyone.

Not the moral. We are not saying rest is earned by hidden effort, or that you must be secretly working hard to deserve it. That is the trap this whole zine is walking away from. The point is narrower: you cannot read the cost off the surface — not the otter's, not each other's, not your own.

Wright et al. 2021, Science
warm from the inside, from birth

The Stone You Keep · Stimpunks 6
How the resting is done

Anchored,
and together

Sea otters rest in rafts — loose groups on the surface, often gathered in kelp canopy. And they wrap themselves in the kelp, so that sleeping doesn't mean drifting.

Notice what is doing the holding. Not vigilance. Not grip strength. Not a sleeping animal working at staying put. The kelp holds it. The otter's whole contribution is to be somewhere that will hold it, and then stop.

This is co-regulation stated as physics, and it is the reason our Saturday works. Nobody is regulating themselves harder. There is a room with an otter cam in it, and a rule that you don't have to perform being present, and the room does the holding. Rest is not an individual skill any more than staying in one place is.

Then, when they wake, they peel off again — solo play, a stone, a somersault, an occasional minute alongside another otter before drifting back into their own absorption. Cave, then watering hole, then cave. Nobody makes them justify the ratio.

Give a body something to be held by and rest stops being a discipline.

One thing we're leaving out. You have seen the picture of otters asleep holding hands, and you have read the caption about love. Rafting is real and kelp-anchoring is real; the hand-holding romance is a story the internet tells about them. We don't need it. Being held by the forest you live in is the better fact anyway.

The Stone You Keep · Stimpunks 7
Aleutian Islands, 1974

The forest that holds you
is the forest you hold

Two researchers compared Aleutian islands that had sea otters with islands that didn't, and found the difference was not subtle. Where otters were present: kelp forest. Where otters were absent: sea urchins, in enormous numbers, and no kelp at all. A barren.

The otters eat urchins. The urchins eat kelp. Take the otters out and the second link runs unchecked until the forest is gone and the seafloor is a monoculture of spines. This is the study that made keystone species a phrase people know, and the otter is still its textbook case.

Now put it beside the last spread. The otter anchors itself in kelp to rest. The kelp is there because the otter is there. It is held by the thing its presence sustains.

Which is a claim about presence, not productivity. The otter is not managing the forest. It has no plan. It is eating what it eats, in its own specialised way, and the forest exists as a consequence. Being there is the contribution. Nobody had to earn their place in the kelp by being useful on purpose.

Remove the ones who don't look essential and the whole thing flattens to one species with spines.

Estes & Palmisano 1974, Science
presence is load-bearing

The Stone You Keep · Stimpunks 8
1741–1911

What they came
for was the coat

The fur that lets a sea otter live in cold water without blubber is the finest coat on any animal alive. So the fur trade came for it, for a hundred and seventy years, across the whole North Pacific.

By 1911 there were roughly two thousand sea otters left in the world — around ninety-nine per cent of the original abundance gone, the survivors scattered in a handful of remnant colonies. A treaty signed that year is most of the reason there are any at all.

Sit with the shape of that. The trait that made the animal possible — the specific, extraordinary adaptation that let it live where it lives — is the exact trait that made it a commodity. It was not hunted for being deficient. It was hunted for being remarkable.

We know that shape. It is why this zine will not tell you your difference is a superpower, or an asset, or a competitive advantage. Being valued for a trait is not the same as being valued. The otters were valued enormously. It nearly finished them.

They did not come for the otters who were failing. They came for the coat that worked.
What this zine refuses

Not:

NotCute. They are charming and we will not pretend otherwise. But charm is the easiest place to stop looking, and everything here is on the other side of it — in Science, in PNAS, in the Journal of Mammalogy. They were interesting long before anyone found them adorable.
NotInspiring. We are interesting, and we will not pretend otherwise. But a person you find inspiring is a person you are watching instead of listening to, and the word tends to arrive just as the listening stops. The otters are evidence; we are testimony; neither is decoration. Nothing here is here to make you feel good about watching us cope.
NotA superpower. Specialisation is measurably more efficient on its own prey. It is not a gift, a compensation, or a hidden talent that repays the world for tolerating you. It is just what being specifically built is like.
NotRest so you can produce. The otter is not recharging in order to forage better tomorrow. Rest is not a maintenance interval on a working machine.
NotBe a keystone. The keystone finding is about presence, not heroism. Nobody is asking you to hold up an ecosystem. The otter isn't trying to.
NotThe smoothed version. Not the lifelong favourite rock, not the hand-holding sweethearts. The true versions are stranger and they are enough.
NotAn animal metaphor for people. Otters are not a lesson wearing fur. They are kin doing their own thing, and the resemblance runs both ways or not at all.

Reflection

What is your stone — the reliable thing you return to that makes a hard task possible? Who has ever suggested you should manage without it?

Where in your life has an accommodation been treated as a discount on the work, rather than as the thing keeping you intact while you do it?

What holds you while you rest — and is it a person's effort, or is it a room built so that no effort is needed?

Whose cost have you been reading off the surface lately, including your own?

Honesty note. The stone is described here only as the primary observations support it: a stone kept and reused across successive food items within a bout of feeding. The widely repeated claim that each otter keeps one favourite rock for years or for life, in a pouch under the left forearm, traces to popular retellings rather than to the primary literature, so it is not made here — the pouches and the reuse are real, the lifelong romance is not established. Likewise, rafting and kelp-anchored rest are well documented, while the image of otters sleeping hand-in-hand as an expression of affection is a story told about them rather than a finding. The Tinker result is a comparison between sites differing in food availability, not an experiment on a single population over time, and the diversification it reports is a response to scarcity — we draw no moral from scarcity itself. Nothing here claims the otters' arrangements justify ours; the universe does not owe us a precedent, and a resemblance is a rhyme, not a proof. The belonging claim does not rest on the otters. It rests on us, and it reaches all the way down.

The stone is a rock. It is also, for the otter that keeps it, the difference between eating and not, and between old age with teeth and without. Keep your stone. Love you down to your star stuff.
