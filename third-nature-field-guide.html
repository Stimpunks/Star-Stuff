A Field Guide to Third Nature

Thirteen habitats nobody meant to make, sorted by what made them — an old way of working, a building, a poisoning, an evacuation. Anna Tsing’s third nature is what manages to live despite capitalism. This is a catalogue of what it is actually living on, and of the improvement now removing each one. None of these is ranked, and none of them is a comeback story.

L★S
How to use this guide Each entry gives an organism or a community, the human by-product it lives on, how the popular telling misreads it (“sounds like / actually”), and full field notes with sources. The last line of every card names the improvement — the thing being done, usually for good reasons, that removes the habitat. Click any entry to open its notes.
Where this came from The companion to Oblivious to Encounter (No. 74), which follows scalability from the sugarcane plantation to the mushroom nobody can farm. Its tenth link is the one this guide is built out of: matsutake became rare across Japan by abandonment rather than destruction, because a support nobody had named went with a change in how people heated their houses. That turned out not to be a special case.
Not the extremophile guide Life in Impossible Places (No. 3) catalogues life in conditions that were always harsh — vents, brine, boiling springs, solid rock — and argues it is about fit, not grit. Every place in this guide is one we made. The radiation-eating fungi of Chernobyl are carded there and are deliberately not repeated here.

Four things that made a habitat

The sort came out of the entries, and the first sort we tried failed. We began meaning to sort by how damaged the place is — bombsite, mine spoil, exclusion zone at one end; hay meadow and coppice at the other. That collapsed almost at once, because damage turned out not to predict anything. A lead-poisoned spoil heap and a hay meadow are at opposite ends of any damage scale and are the same kind of object here: both are by-products of human work that something came to depend on, and both are being tidied. What actually varies is what we were doing at the time — working a landscape, putting up a structure, contaminating a place, or leaving. So the sections run by that instead. They do not rank. And one thing recurs in every section, which is the reason the guide exists: the support was a by-product, so nobody wrote it down as a requirement, so nothing defends it when the practice that produced it improves.

One · The work

When the habitat was a by-product of the job

Four species that lived on the margins of an older way of using land — the firewood cut, the late hay, the dirty seed sack, the coppice rotation. None of the four was harmed by the land being destroyed. In every case the land is still there and the practice changed, which is a much harder thing to notice and a much harder thing to argue against, because each change was an improvement by any ordinary measure.

Two · The structure

When the habitat is a gap in something we built

We put up rubble, eaves, flues and ledges for reasons of our own, and things moved in. This section runs deliberately as a gradient of dependence: a bird that lost one accidental habitat and found the next; a bird whose gaps are being sealed right now; a bird we assumed had nothing else and which turns out to have kept a wild option; and a bird that has none at all. The last card goes the other way, and it is here to stop the section reading as a lament.

Three · The damage

When the wreckage itself is the habitat

The uncomfortable section, and it is uncomfortable in a specific way that is worth naming before you read it. A poisoned spoil heap and an oil platform are not arguments for poisoning or drilling. They are places where the damage is already done and something now depends on the state it left — which makes the cleanup a decision with two sides rather than one. The third card is here for a different reason: it is the one where we had the story wrong, and the correction arrived in 2025.

And · Not carded

One place we are not going to reframe

Every other entry gets a “sounds like / actually” table, because in every other case there is a settled answer and a popular telling that misses it. The Chernobyl Exclusion Zone is the one where we do not have the settled answer — two bodies of published work disagree, and running our format over that would mean picking a winner in a live scientific dispute and presenting it as a correction. It gets a dashed card, no table, and an account of the disagreement instead.

Not: Not an argument that damage is good. Tsing is explicit that matsutake’s tolerance for wrecked ground “is not an excuse for further damage,” and neither is any card here. A spoil heap is not a reason to poison ground; it is a fact about ground already poisoned. Every entry in section three is downstream of harm we would prevent if we could go back. Not ruin as an aesthetic. There is a way of writing about bombsites and exclusion zones that enjoys them, and it is a way of not looking at who was bombed and who was evacuated. The habitat is real. So is what made it. Not “nature is resilient.” Every species here is holding on inside a specific and narrow requirement, and most of them are declining. Resilience is never a demand, and a species persisting in a bad place is not evidence the place is survivable — only that this one is still there so far. Not against improvement. Insulating buildings is good. Cleaning seed corn is good. Capping a dangerous flue is good. Not one of the improvements named on these cards should be reversed, and saying so is not the argument. The argument is that a by-product nobody recorded as a requirement gets removed without anyone deciding to remove it — and that a thing you have named is a thing you can at least choose about. Not a metaphor for us. No organism here stands in for a person, and none of them is Autistic. The rhyme between a support nobody named and the accommodations that quietly disappear when a workplace modernises is a rhyme, noticed after the ecology rather than imposed on it. A rhyme is not a proof. Not settled. The Chernobyl card carries no table because the science is contested. The chimney swift card exists partly to correct an assumption we would have made. And the Underground mosquito was a textbook case for twenty-six years before the genomes arrived.
Star stuff, in the leftovers

The lead in a spoil heap and the zinc a spring sandwort hyper-accumulates were made in dying stars, went into a rock, were dug out by people, and are now holding open a grassland that European law protects. Nothing in that sequence was planned by anyone, including the part that is now conservation policy.

What the thirteen have in common is not damage and it is not toughness. It is that each one depends on something we do for a reason of our own — and that because the dependence was never anybody’s intention, it was never written down. There is no line in any specification that says keep cutting the firewood, or leave the gap under the soffit, or do not sieve the seed quite so well. So when the practice improves, the requirement goes with it, and the loss shows up in the record as nothing at all — because the record only ever held the practice.

A support nobody named is a support nobody defends. That is what this catalogue is for, and it is the sentence Oblivious to Encounter (No. 74) hands over at its tenth link and does not have room to spend. The zine argues how sameness got built. This one lists what was living in the gaps.

Sources follow the primary and authoritative literature, and where we did not reach a primary this note says so. Third nature is Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing’s term, from The Mushroom at the End of the World: On the Possibility of Life in Capitalist Ruins (Princeton University Press, 2015), read in the primary: “third nature,” that is, what manages to live despite capitalism, extending William Cronon’s first and second nature from Nature’s Metropolis, which Tsing credits. The matsutake card follows the same primary — peasant woodlands cut for timber plantations, paved for suburbs or abandoned as people moved to the city, fossil fuel replacing firewood and charcoal, unworked woodlands growing into dense broadleaf thickets, shade-stressed pines killed by an invasive nematode, and matsutake “rare across Japan” by the mid-1970s. That cultivation remains commercially impossible is Yamanaka, T., Yamada, A. & Furukawa, H., “Advances in the cultivation of the highly-prized ectomycorrhizal mushroom Tricholoma matsutake,” Mycoscience 61(2), 49–57 (2020), used at abstract and review level rather than read in full. Black redstart figures are from Gilbert, G., Gibbons, D. W. & Evans, J., Bird Monitoring Methods (pp. 329–331), read directly: Schedule 1 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981; the population has exceeded 100 occupied territories; “currently estimated to be 27–74 pairs”; breeding sites “typically in industrialised, built-up or derelict surroundings”; the underlying survey is Morgan, R. A. & Glue, D. E., “Breeding survey of black redstarts in Britain, 1977,” Bird Study 28, 163–168 (1981). The bombsite history and the city-by-city correlation with regeneration are from the Black Redstart Action Plan material at blackredstarts.org.uk, which is a specialist secondary source carrying no survey attributions, and is treated as one. Common swift — UK Red List in the 2021 conservation status assessment, with nest-site loss through renovation and insulation, aerial insect decline and climate given together as causes; the roughly 70% Breeding Bird Survey decline is quoted from BTO/RSPB summaries rather than from the BBS report itself. Compensatory nest boxes are studied in Bird Conservation International (“Nest-boxes for Common Swifts Apus apus as compensatory measures in the context of building renovation”), cited at title and abstract level. Chimney swift — approximately 67% decline since 1970, and near 90% in Canada, from Partners in Flight and national assessments; the correction that a substantial portion still nests in large hollow trees in forest-dominated landscapes follows Ornithological Applications, “It’s not all about chimneys: Informing conservation of the natural nesting habitat of Chaetura pelagica,” and the tree-cavity work in Avian Conservation and Ecology 9(2); both at abstract level. Purple martin — that east of the Rocky Mountains there is no documented nesting in a natural cavity, and that gourd housing was recorded in Choctaw and Chickasaw settlements in the 1700s, are from the Purple Martin Conservation Association and state wildlife agency accounts; we did not reach a primary ornithological survey for the “not a single documented” claim, and it is stated here as those sources state it. Corncrake — the increase from 480 to 589 calling males between 1993 and 1998 under delayed mowing and unmown corridors, and 1,289 calling males in 2014, are from RSPB and Conservation Evidence summaries; the post-2015 slippage and its attribution to changed scheme prescriptions is RSPB’s own framing. Corncockle — the collapse between roughly 1930 and 1960, and that improved seed cleaning mattered alongside herbicides because its large seeds screen out easily, follow Plantlife’s arable plants material and BSBI species accounts. High brown fritillary — Britain’s most threatened butterfly, about 85% decline since the 1970s and a 94% range contraction, with cessation of coppicing and of traditional bracken management given as causes; from Butterfly Conservation’s Species Action Plan and UKBMS. These figures are from a Species Action Plan that is not recent; they are reported with that caveat rather than as current-year numbers. Calaminarian grassland — Annex I habitat 6130, “Calaminarian grasslands of the Violetalia calaminariae,” per JNCC, whose account states that heavy metal toxicity “is believed to maintain the open vegetation, retarding succession.” JNCC’s habitat account does not list reclamation or remediation among threats — we checked, and say so. The threats named on the card come instead from Simkin, J., Calaminarian Grassland: Report for the North Pennines AONB Partnership, Version 3, revised May 2017, read directly: “Gravel extraction, landscaping, and the reworking or stabilisation of spoil heaps can all lead to total loss of habitat from a site, and it is generally the best sites, from an ecological viewpoint, that are vulnerable to these”; that mine water remediation reducing metal deposition “can be expected to increase rates of succession and species loss downstream”; that spring sandwort “is a poor competitor and is soon lost as the grass sward closes” and is known as leadwort from its value to miners as an indicator of galena; and that the lichen Stereocaulon nanodes “was abundant around the remains of Nenthead smeltmill until the restoration of Nenthead Mines.” Oil platforms — Claisse, J. T. et al., “Oil platforms off California are among the most productive marine fish habitats globally,” PNAS 111(43), 15462–15467 (2014), and the accompanying PNAS commentary “Should we ‘reef’ obsolete oil platforms?”; both read at abstract and commentary level. The figure that partial removal retains roughly 80% of fish biomass relative to complete removal is from the rigs-to-reefs literature and is reported as such. London Underground mosquito — the original account is Byrne, K. & Nichols, R. A., “Culex pipiens in London Underground tunnels: differentiation between surface and subterranean populations,” Heredity 82, 7–15 (1999). The revision is Haba, Y. et al. (McBride lab, Princeton), “Ancient origin of an urban underground mosquito,” Science, 23 October 2025, DOI 10.1126/science.ady4515 — population genomics of about 350 contemporary and historical samples placing the origin of molestus aboveground in the Mediterranean basin or Middle East over a thousand years ago, plausibly with early agriculture. The Science paper is behind a paywall and was read through the Princeton Environmental Institute and Ecology & Evolutionary Biology announcements and press coverage, not in the original. Richard Nichols’ response calling the new work convincing is reported in press coverage and is quoted here as reported, not from him directly. Peregrine — the organochlorine crash and recovery, 1,769 breeding pairs in the BTO’s 2014 survey, over 200 urban sites, and the finding that urban and coastal birds fared better than upland ones. A figure of “only three breeding pairs” at the 1963 low appears in several popular accounts; we could not reconcile it with the national survey history and have deliberately printed no low-point figure. The urban habitat-selection work is Kettel, E. F. et al. in the Journal of Urban Ecology 7(1), cited at title level. Chernobyl Exclusion Zone — Deryabina, T. G. et al., “Long-term census data reveal abundant wildlife populations at Chernobyl,” Current Biology 25(19), R824–R826 (2015), against the body of work by A. P. Møller and T. A. Mousseau reporting reduced abundance and diversity; the methodological disputes — sampling concentrated in the Red Forest, treatment of ingested and inhaled dose, and reanalyses finding effects only at higher doses — are summarised from Knowable Magazine’s review of the disagreement. This card carries no reframe table for that reason, and the guide takes no side. Held honestly: the sort into four things that made a habitat is ours, arrived at after the entries; sorting by degree of damage was tried first and failed, which the gates band says on the page. This guide is Britain- and North-America-heavy, because that is where the long-run monitoring data is, and that is a fact about surveillance effort rather than about where accidental habitat occurs. Several cards rest on conservation-organisation summaries rather than primary papers; each is named as such above rather than laundered into a citation. Companion to Oblivious to Encounter (No. 74). The extremophile catalogue, including the radiation-tolerant fungi of Chernobyl, is Life in Impossible Places (No. 3). A support nobody named is a support nobody defends.
