A Field Guide to
Being a Tortoise

Twelve land tortoises, and an arc that runs from the Moon to a graveyard to a beginning. The first animals to go around the Moon and come back were two tortoises, and they beat every human there by two months. Then: a burrow that houses 350 other species, a shell full of holes, a vegetarian that hunted, an animal that hatched around 1832 and is still eating grass. And then four whose endings are not yet facts.

Tortoise is not the opposite of turtle Testudinidae — the land tortoises — is a real family, and everything here belongs to it. But the everyday words don't carve it cleanly: American English calls the whole order turtles and tortoises a kind of turtle; British English splits tortoise (land), turtle (sea) and terrapin (fresh water). Both are fine. Neither is the taxonomy. The companion is Field Guide No. 10, and the Mojave desert tortoise lives over there.

The guide

Twelve tortoises

Ordered on purpose. It opens in space, moves through the ordinary astonishments of a slow land animal, and ends with the four entries where what happens next is a choice. The last one is a tortoise from one ocean doing a dead tortoise's job in another, and it is working.

Not: Not slow as a moral. Slowness is a strategy with a bill attached — see No. 34. Not armour. The pancake tortoise's shell is full of holes and it is the best climber of them all. Not settled. "Tortoises are herbivores" held until somebody filmed one hunting, and "it inflates in the crevice" is now thought to be wrong. Not a mascot. Testudinidae is among the most trafficked families on Earth; loving the shape is not the same as leaving the animal a hillside. Not inevitable. Pinta closed. Fernandina reopened. Île aux Aigrettes is 529 hatchlings that did not have to happen.
Star stuff, on land

Every tortoise here is built from star-forged calcium and carbon, and each spends it on ribs and vertebrae grown wide and fused — bone that turns pressure into charge, which is where this collection began, in Bone Song (No. 1). Two of them were carried around the far side of the Moon in 1968 and came home to be weighed. The star stuff went and had a look, and it was a tortoise.

Figures follow the primary and authoritative sources. Zond 5 launched 15 September 1968 from Baykonur with a biological payload including two steppe tortoises, rounded the Moon's far side at about 1,950 km on 18 September, and splashed down in the Indian Ocean on 21 September; the tortoises survived, having lost roughly 10% of body weight, and were reported in good health (NASA NSSDCA record 1968-076A, and the Soviet mission reporting). Apollo 8 carried the first humans around the Moon that December. Gopher tortoise burrow dimensions and commensal counts from Florida FWC and the gopher-tortoise literature — burrows over 14 m long and 3 m deep, and more than 350 species recorded using them; published totals vary by study and by how invertebrates are counted, so the figure is given as "more than 350" rather than exactly. Pancake tortoise — flattened, fenestrated, flexible shell; wedges into rock crevices with the forelimbs; noted as the fastest and best-climbing tortoise. The long-repeated claim that it inflates itself to lock into a crevice "chuckwalla style" is now believed to be untrue, and this guide says so rather than repeating it. Giant tortoise predation — Gerlach, J. & Zora, A., "Giant tortoises hunt and consume birds," Current Biology 31(17), 23 August 2021: an adult female Aldabrachelys gigantea filmed on Frégate Island, Seychelles, on 30 July 2020, pursuing a tern chick along a log and swallowing it, over about seven minutes — the first documented deliberate hunting by a wild tortoise. Jonathan — Seychelles giant tortoise on St Helena, hatched circa 1832 (no birth record; he was estimated at about 50 when brought to the island in 1882), recognised by Guinness World Records as the oldest living land animal; reports of his death in early 2026 were false. Galápagos shells — saddleback carapaces occur on drier, lower islands and allow the neck to reach higher browse; domed carapaces occur in wetter highlands with abundant ground vegetation. Speckled padloper (Chersobius signatus) — males 6–8 cm and females to nearly 10 cm straight carapace length, 95–165 g, restricted to Little Namaqualand, South Africa. Indian star tortoise — more than 34,000 live animals reported confiscated 2000–2015, over 21,300 of them within India; 2,098 seized from a single dinghy by the Sri Lankan Navy in June 2017; around 11% of global chelonian seizures. Ploughshare tortoise (Astrochelys yniphora, angonoka) — Baly Bay, Madagascar; shells engraved by conservation staff to reduce their value to the trade, a measure that conservationists report has not clearly deterred poachers. Lonesome George, the last Pinta Island tortoise, died 24 June 2012. Fernanda — found on Fernandina in 2019 and confirmed as Chelonoidis phantasticus by genome sequencing published in Communications Biology (2022) by Princeton, Yale and Ecuadorian researchers, matching the male specimen collected in 1906. Île aux Aigrettes — 26 Aldabra giant tortoises introduced 2000–2011 as ecological replacements for the extinct Mauritian Cylindraspis inepta, with a minimum of 529 hatchlings produced in 11 years, low mortality, and measured enhancement of germination for large-seeded native plants. Held honestly: ages of very old tortoises are estimates, not records — Jonathan's 1832 is a back-calculation, and the frequently quoted ~255 years for Adwaita of Kolkata is not established and is not used here. Commensal-species counts for gopher tortoise burrows vary between studies. The star tortoise's pattern is usually explained as disruptive camouflage in dry grass; that is the standard account rather than a finding this guide can point to a test for, and it is worded as such. Ecological replacement is contested conservation practice, not settled policy, and the Mauritius programme is one well-documented case rather than a general licence. The reframe — that a family this varied has no standard member, and that four of these endings are decisions rather than facts — is ours. Companion to A Field Guide to Being a Turtle (No. 10) and Everywhere That Turtles Go (No. 35). There is no standard tortoise.
