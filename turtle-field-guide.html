A Field Guide to
Being a Turtle

Twelve ways to be a turtle — the companion catalogue to Everywhere That Turtles Go (No. 35). One with no hard shell that swims warm through cold water. One that breathes through the wrong end. One that pees through its mouth. One that survives winter by not being touched. One we called silent for as long as we didn't listen. Same group. No standard turtle. And then, kept separate on purpose, five turtles that people made.

One group, no standard All of these are turtles — one body plan, more than 200 million years. Inside it: no scutes, no hard shell, no tongue, lungs that gave up breathing. Belonging to a category never made anyone alike, and almost every entry here is a thing a turtle is supposed to have and doesn't.
Why the last five look different Because they are different. The turtles people made are not specimens, and running "sounds like / actually" over somebody's cosmology would be obscene. They get a dashed border and a different card. The format difference is the argument.
Section one

Twelve turtles

Ranged deliberately: every ocean, the Amazon, North American ponds, three Australian rivers, an Asian river that is nearly empty of them now, and two deserts. Both suborders — the hidden-necks that fold vertically and the side-necks that fold flat. The order runs from the largest to the last of its kind.

Section two

Five turtles people made

Because turtles reach almost everywhere, almost every people had to think with one — and they did not think the same thing. These five are not variants of a single world-turtle myth, and the commonest error in Western retellings is to merge them. So they are catalogued as what they are: whose they are, what the turtle does in that world, and what gets got wrong. No misreading table, because the card is not here to correct anybody's cosmology. The fifth entry is a card that declines to be one; the reason is on it. Read alongside spread 11 of Everywhere That Turtles Go.

Not: Not a ranking — the one with no shell and the one that fits in a nest are both whole turtles. Not "missing" — no scutes, no tongue, no rib-breathing. Every absence here is somebody's working answer. Not silent — we recorded 2,122 sounds the moment we brought a hydrophone. Assume the instrument before you assume the absence. Not one world turtle — five traditions, five turtles, and merging them is how a knowledge system becomes a motif. Not a mascot — Rafetus swinhoei is down to two or three animals and no known female. Borrowing the shape is not saving the animal.
Star stuff, in a shell

Every turtle here is built from star-forged calcium and carbon, and each one spends it on the same trick: ribs and vertebrae grown wide and fused into a carapace. Bone that turns pressure into charge — see Bone Song (No. 1). One body plan, one inheritance, and twelve completely different answers to the question of how to be alive with a skeleton on the outside. The spiky profile, at the scale of a whole order.

Figures follow the primary literature. Leatherback dive depths to ~1,280 m; body temperature maintained up to ~18 °C above the surrounding sea via gigantothermy (large body, peripheral insulation, circulatory control) — following Paladino, O'Connor & Spotila (Nature 344, 1990) and the leatherback thermal-strategy literature; the carapace is hundreds of interlocking ossicles in oil-saturated skin, not a bony box with scutes. Mata mata feeding from Lemell et al., "Feeding patterns of Chelus fimbriatus" (J. Exp. Biol. 205, 2002) and Lemell et al. (Amphibia-Reptilia 31, 2010) — a hyoid apparatus two-thirds the length of the skull, poorly developed jaw adductors, and a nearly reduced tongue. Alligator snapping turtle lingual lure and the four phases of predation from Drummond & Gordon's luring study; Macrochelys is the only living reptile known to carry a predatory lure inside its mouth. Rheodytes leukops cloacal respiration — up to ~70% of total oxygen uptake, bursae lined with branching papillae increasing surface area ~16-fold — from Priest (1997) and Priest & Franklin (2002). Pelodiscus sinensis buccopharyngeal urea excretion from Ip, Loong, Lee, Ong, Wong & Chew, "The Chinese soft-shelled turtle, Pelodiscus sinensis, excretes urea mainly through the mouth instead of the kidney" (J. Exp. Biol. 215, 3723–3733, 2012). Painted turtle overwintering from Costanzo, Lee and colleagues — hatchlings in shallow nests exposed below −10 °C, freeze tolerance limited to about −4 °C, survival therefore resting on supercooling, which soil ice nuclei active at −3.5 to −5 °C can defeat on contact. Arrau vocalisation from Ferrara, Vogt & Sousa-Lima, "Turtle vocalizations as the first evidence of posthatching parental care in chelonians" (J. Comp. Psychol., 2013) and "Sound communication and social behavior in an Amazonian river turtle" (Herpetologica, 2014) — 2,122 sounds in 11 types, from hatchlings in eggs and in open nests and from adult females, with females approaching and responding in the river. Chelonia mydas natal homing follows Brothers & Lohmann (Current Biology, 2015). Rafetus swinhoei: the last known female died at Suzhou in April 2019 during recovery from anaesthesia after artificial insemination; a second female confirmed at Đồng Mô, Vietnam in 2020 was found dead in April 2023. Held honestly: maxima and depth records are records, not typical animals. The Rafetus count is stated as "two or three" because published counts disagree and keep moving — it is a figure to re-check, not to quote. "Cloacal respiration" is bimodal, not a replacement for lungs; the ~70% figure is aquatic oxygen uptake under study conditions, and popular claims of 100% or of weeks submerged are not supported by those papers. Painted turtle hatchlings are moderately freeze tolerant — supercooling is the primary strategy, not the only one. And the Arrau result establishes that these turtles vocalise and that females respond to hatchlings; it does not establish what any given call means. The reframe — that an order this varied has no standard member, and that an assumed silence usually indexes the listener — is ours. Companion to Everywhere That Turtles Go (No. 35), Bone Song (No. 1), and A Field Guide to Being a Shark (No. 9), which makes this argument about a different clade. There is no standard turtle.
