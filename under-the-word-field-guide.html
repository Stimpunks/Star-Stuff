Under the Word

Eighteen borrowed and invented words for Autistic joy, each with its honest provenance, the monotropic reading the words were collected for, and whatever is actually holding it up. The guide’s own finding is that what is underneath is not one kind of thing — thirteen of these words have a mechanism under them and five have a person. None of them is ranked, and none is the best one.

L★S
How to use this guide Each entry gives the word, where it actually comes from, the monotropic reading from Words, and what is underneath — with a “how it circulates / as it checks out” table for the claims that travel further than they should. Click any entry to open its field notes and sources.
Where the words came from Words: Inspired by the experience of being monotropic — a free open-source community e-book by Helen Edgar (Autistic Realms), Marion McLaughlin (Aurora Consulting) and Felicity Goodhall (Flappy Inky Feathers), collecting about forty-eight words and reading each one through monotropism. The words are theirs and the monotropic readings quoted on every card are theirs. What this guide adds is the provenance and the mechanism. They credit their own sources plainly — words found across social media and books, with a mention to @aesthetic_logophile on Instagram and to Tom Read Wilson, On The Tip Of My Tongue, who inspired the project. That openness is what made the provenance work possible to do honestly. The edition read for this guide is the 104-page January 2025 PDF; the booklet is living and still being added to, so check the source for the current one.
Provenance is graded, not flattened These words are not all the same kind of object, and running them together as “untranslatable words” would be the flattening this house exists to refuse. Some are attested and traceable to a named person. Some are real words carrying a gloss that came off an aesthetic word-list rather than a dictionary. Some were invented online within the last ten years — which is not a flaw and is said plainly on the card, because a community that reaches for a word, does not find one, and makes one is the most Star Stuff thing in the booklet. What would be dishonest is selling recent invention as ancient wisdom.

Two things that hold a word up

The sort came out of the entries and was not the sort we set out to make. The plan was to file these by feeling — joy, longing, solitude, kinship — and that failed immediately, because the feelings overlap almost completely and the sort carried no information. What actually divides them is what you find when you look under the word. For thirteen there is a measurable mechanism, and it is startlingly often variation: a brook sounds like a brook because its bubbles differ in size, and a glitter path exists because wave facets differ in slope. For five there is no mechanism at all — there is a person, with a date, who wrote the thing down. Neither section is the better one, and a word with a person under it is not a weaker word. It is a different kind of true.

When there is something measurable underneath

Thirteen words where the pleasure the word names has a physical cause you can go and check. The cause is not always what the word implies, and in several cases it is better: the shining path on the sea belongs to whoever is looking at it, the dapple under a tree is a thousand portraits of one enormous thing, and the smell of an old book is the book coming apart. Nothing here is offered as proof that the feeling is real. The feeling was already real.

Two · A person

When what is underneath is somebody who wrote it down

Five words with no mechanism to find, because the thing under them is a person and a date. That is not a lesser foundation — it is a checkable one, and in every case here the primary source says something more interesting than the circulating gloss. Two of the five are documented coinages by living or recently living writers, which means these are not ancient words at all, and their cards say so.

Not: Not that the science proves the feeling is real. The feeling was already real, and physics is not a permit. Every mechanism here was found after the fact, by people who already loved the thing. If a card ever reads as though a brook needed Minnaert’s approval before it was allowed to be beautiful, the card has failed. Not Autistic joy justified by its usefulness. Nothing here argues that these pleasures are worth having because they regulate, restore, or make anybody easier to be around. They are worth having. That is the whole of the claim. Not the superpower framing. Monotropic attention is not compensation for anything, and noticing the shine on water in detail is not a trade you made for something else. Not a wall of loanwords implying that other languages have finer feelings than the reader’s own. That genre is what produced most of the bad glosses corrected on these cards. English has “the smell of an old book” and it is doing fine; what a borrowed word buys is compression, not depth. Not ancient wisdom. Three of these words were invented on the internet in the last decade or so and one is a Latin word wearing a meaning it never had. The cards say which, on their faces.
The line: Thirteen of the guides in this collection arrive at there is no standard star, nest, egg, shark, rainbow, nervous system, way to rest, way of being family. (Derived by reading every hit, not by counting them — one guide matches the phrase in order to decline it.) This one arrives somewhere adjacent and slightly different, because what varies here is not the words — it is what happens when a bodymind meets a brook. There is no standard way to be moved. The mechanisms are shared and public and the same for everyone; what any given person does when they meet one is not, and the words in this booklet exist because forty-eight of those experiences went looking for language and found the dictionary short.
