A Field Guide to
Unfinished Animals

Sixteen animals we reconstructed wrong, sorted by the kind of mistake — and every card says whether the argument is over. There is no final draft. One was drawn upside down for fourteen years and then back-to-front for twenty-four more. One was three animals in three different groups. One had its skull mounted on the end of its tail. One was two arms and nothing else for forty-four years. Three of these are still being argued about in print. The animals were never wrong. The drawings were, and the drawings are signed and dated.

How to use this guide Each entry gives an animal, what we said it was, what the evidence turned out to say, who published the correction and when — and a status: whether that correction is finished. Click any entry to open its notes.
Where this came from The companion to Thirteen Changes (No. 61), which follows one category boundary — Brontosaurus — through a hundred and forty years of being drawn, erased and drawn again. That zine asks what an honest boundary owes you. This one asks the prior question: how often does the record actually move? The answer is constantly, and in public.
Why this one breaks the house formula Twelve of these guides arrive at there is no standard star, nest, egg, shark, way to rest. This one cannot, because what varies here is not the animals — it is us. A sauropod is not unfinished. Our drawing of it is. So the line is there is no final draft, and it is a claim about the record rather than about kin.
Why two cards are dashed Because the apparatus stops there. Sixteen entries get a reframe table because a verdict was reached about an animal and the verdict was wrong. Mary Anning is not a specimen, and the last card has nothing to correct yet — that is the point of it.

Five kinds of mistake, and one place the apparatus stops

The sort is ours and it came out of the entries rather than ahead of them. We tried sorting by date of correction, which put a 1991 inversion next to a 1991 synonymy that had nothing in common with it. We tried sorting by taxon, and it separated two animals that were misread in exactly the same way. What varies cleanly is the shape of the error: the pieces assembled in the wrong order, the animal known from almost nothing, the boundary that moved, the name that outlived its own correction, and the story that got tidier than the evidence. None of these ranks above another, and none of them is a failure of science — every single correction on this page was published, dated and signed, which is the only reason we can list it at all.

And the status on every card

This is the one thing this guide adds to the house form, and it is borrowed from How We Got Here, where every joint in a chain is marked documented, contested or leap. A catalogue of corrections is dishonest if it will not say which corrections are finished. Without the notation this page would read as a list of settled triumphs, which is exactly the smoothing it is complaining about.

One · Assembled wrong

Every piece was real. The arrangement was ours.

Four animals where nobody misread a bone — they misread how the bones went together. Hallucigenia was published upside down and then, once righted, still back-to-front. Anomalocaris spent ninety-three years as three animals filed in three different groups. Cope put a skull on the end of a tail. Mantell put a thumb on a nose. Three of the four errors were made from real, well-described fossils by people doing careful work — which is the point: an assembly error is not a failure of observation, it is a failure of the arrangement you brought with you.

Two · Known from almost nothing

When the evidence is a fragment, the gap gets filled anyway

Three animals reconstructed from far too little, and each shows a different way that goes. Deinocheirus was two enormous arms for forty-four years and the honest answer stayed we do not know. Helicoprion's spiral of teeth was placed on the snout, on the back, on the tail and in the jaw, because a whorl with no skull around it will support almost any guess. And the Spinosaurus holotype no longer exists — it burned in 1944 after its describer's requests to move the collection were refused, so every reconstruction since argues from photographs and drawings.

Three · The line moved

Merged, split, and in two cases put back

Four boundaries between kinds, drawn and redrawn on the evidence. Brontosaurus was folded into Apatosaurus in 1903 and separated again in 2015. Torosaurus was proposed as merely a grown-up Triceratops in 2010, rejected in 2012, and the question is still open. Nanotyrannus was dismissed for forty years as a juvenile Tyrannosaurus and reinstated in 2025 — with a second species nobody had noticed. Tanystropheus was one species until a CT scan made it two. Two of these four went the way the sceptics did not expect, and all four were argued in public under names.

Four · The name outlived it

Corrected on the science, stuck with the label

Two animals carrying names that the evidence disproved and the rules will not let anyone change. Oviraptor means egg seizer, and it was sitting on its own clutch. Basilosaurus means king lizard, and Richard Owen established within five years that it was a mammal. In both cases the correction is published, accepted and old — and in both cases the animal will carry the accusation in every textbook forever, because nomenclatural priority protects stability rather than accuracy. This is the band the companion zine cares about most: a label can outlive its own refutation by rule, and it can outlive it by repetition, and only one of those two is written down anywhere.

Five · Tidier than the evidence

The version that travelled better than the paper

Three cases where the popular account is smoother than anything anybody published. The fish-crawls-out-of-water story has a date problem: footprints eighteen million years older than the earliest tetrapod body fossils — though those footprints are themselves disputed, which the tidy retelling of that also leaves out. The film raptor is a different animal from the fossil one. And the sluggish tail-dragging dinosaur of the old picture books was not corrected by one discovery but by a whole field changing its mind, starting with a single 1969 monograph.

Where the apparatus stops

Two cards with nothing to reframe

Every card above carries three columns: a trait, what it sounded like, and what turned out to be the case. That table exists because a verdict was passed on an animal and the verdict was wrong. These two have no verdict to overturn. One because the correction owed is to the human record rather than to a fossil, and running sounds like / actually over a woman whose finds were published under other men's names would file her with the misreconstructed animals — which is precisely the injury. One because its correction has not been published yet: on this page's own argument, some of the entries above are currently wrong, and saying actually before anybody knows which is bluff rather than rigour.

What this guide refuses

Not a list of scientists being stupid. Almost every error here was made carefully, from real fossils, by someone working at the edge of what the evidence could support. Cope had jaw fragments at one end and reasoned from them. Conway Morris had a flattened smear in shale. Being wrong from good evidence is what the method looks like from inside. Not an argument that science is unreliable. The opposite, and it is the whole point of the page: every correction listed here is published, dated and attributable, which is the only reason a catalogue like this can exist at all. Try assembling the equivalent list for the categories people get sorted into. Not a claim that the animals were unfinished. They were complete. Our record of them is the unfinished thing, which is why the arrival line here is about the record and not about kin. Not a set of gotchas about names. Oviraptor and Basilosaurus are not scandals; nomenclatural stability is a real good bought at a real price, and the band about them says so. Not settled where it is not settled. Four cards are marked contested and stay that way, including the one the companion zine is named for.

Held honestly

The status notation is ours, and it is a judgement about the state of a literature rather than a fact in it — where the reading is close, the card says so in its notes rather than in the chip. Two cards deliberately carry the awkward version of a famous story. The Elasmosaurus anecdote is usually told with Marsh catching Cope; it was Joseph Leidy, on 8 March 1870, with Marsh in the room, and Marsh's claim survives only in a newspaper piece almost twenty years later. And the Devonian trackways that displace Tiktaalik in the popular retelling are themselves disputed — Lucas (2015) reads some of the Zachełmie structures as fish traces, and a 2020 study agrees for part of the surface — so the card carries the dispute instead of the punchline. The Velociraptor quill-knob counts that circulate in press coverage are not printed here, because No. 61 could not reach that paper either. Where a claim rests on a secondary account, the notes say which.

Companion & sources

Every figure on this page is sourced in the field notes of the card that carries it, and where a figure would not stand up it is described rather than printed. There is no final draft.

Figures follow the primary literature where it was reachable, and every card that rests on a secondary account says so in its own notes rather than here. Hallucigenia — Conway Morris, S., "A new metazoan from the Cambrian Burgess Shale of British Columbia," Palaeontology 20 (1977), the description that stood the animal on its spines; Ramsköld, L. & Hou X., "New early Cambrian animal and onychophoran affinities of enigmatic metazoans," Nature 351 (1991), which inverted it; and Smith, M. R. & Caron, J.-B., "Hallucigenia's head and the pharyngeal armature of early ecdysozoans," Nature 523 (2015), which found the eyes, the mouth ring and the tooth-lined throat and so established which end was the front. Wrong on one axis for fourteen years and on the other for twenty-four more. Anomalocaris — Whittington, H. B. & Briggs, D. E. G., "The largest Cambrian animal, Anomalocaris, Burgess Shale, British Columbia," Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 309(1141), 569–609 (1985), which united a supposed shrimp-like appendage, the “jellyfish” Peytoia nathorsti and the “sponge” Laggania cambria into one predator; read at its abstract and bibliographic record, not the full paper, which is behind a paywall. Then the partial undoing: Daley, A. C. & Bergström, J., "The oral cone of Anomalocaris is not a classic ‘peytoia’," Naturwissenschaften 99(6), 501–504 (2012) — three large plates rather than four, Peytoia valid again with Laggania its junior synonym, and suction rather than biting. Elasmosaurus — Cope, E. D. (1868) placed the skull at the caudal end, having found jaw material there while the other end terminated in atlas and axis. The correction is Joseph Leidy's, in Remarks on Elasmosaurus platyurus at the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia on 8 March 1870. The popular attribution to Marsh is wrong: he was present at that meeting, and his claim to have caught it appears in a newspaper article almost two decades later. This account follows the historical reconstruction in Storrs, G. W., “Elasmosaurus platyurus and a page from the Cope–Marsh War,” and the survey of the illustrative and literature background published in Proceedings of the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia 152 (2002); both are secondary historical scholarship rather than the 1868–1870 primaries, and are cited as such. The same animal was revised again: Sachs, S., Kear, B. P. & Everhart, M. J., “Revised vertebral count in the ‘longest-necked vertebrate’ Elasmosaurus platyurus,” PLOS ONE 8(8), e70877 (2013). Iguanodon — named by Gideon Mantell in 1825 from teeth and fragmentary material, with the conical bone read as a nasal horn on the rhinoceros analogy; corrected by Louis Dollo from the near-complete Bernissart skeletons found in 1878 and mounted from 1882, which put the spike on the first digit of the hand. Given from standard museum and reference accounts, not from Mantell or Dollo at the primary. Deinocheirus — the arms were found on 9 July 1965 at Altan Ula III by the Polish–Mongolian expedition under Zofia Kielan-Jaworowska, and named by Halszka Osmólska & Ewa Roniewicz in Palaeontologia Polonica 21 (1970). Resolved by Lee, Y.-N., Barsbold, R., Currie, P. J. and colleagues, “Resolving the long-standing enigmas of a giant ornithomimosaur Deinocheirus mirificus,” Nature 515, 257–260 (2014): two near-complete skeletons, a humped back, a duck-like skull, the largest known ornithomimosaur. Forty-four years between the name and the animal. Spinosaurus — described by Ernst Stromer in 1915 from Bahariya material collected in 1912; the holotype was destroyed in the Allied bombing of Munich on the night of 24–25 April 1944, after Stromer's repeated requests to move the collection to safety were not acted on. Reinterpreted as aquatic or semi-aquatic by Ibrahim, N. and colleagues in Science (2014) and, on the tail, in Nature (2020) — and that interpretation is contested in the literature, which is why the card is marked so. The 1944 destruction and Stromer's warnings are given from museum and institutional histories rather than from an archival primary. Helicoprion — Tapanila, L., Pruitt, J., Pradel, A. and colleagues, “Jaws for a spiral-tooth whorl: CT images reveal novel adaptation and phylogeny in fossil Helicoprion,” Biology Letters 9(2), 20130057 (2013): CT of specimen IMNH 37899 (“Idaho 4”, collected 1950) showed a nearly complete jaw apparatus articulated closed, with a single whorl supported at the symphysis of the lower jaw and occupying the whole mandibular arch. Earlier published reconstructions had placed the whorl on the upper jaw, as paired upper and lower elements, or as pectoral or caudal denticles. Brontosaurus — the short form of No. 61, where the sourcing is given in full: Marsh named Apatosaurus ajax in 1877 and Brontosaurus excelsus in 1879; Riggs folded them together in 1903; Tschopp, E., Mateus, O. & Benson, R. B. J., PeerJ 3:e857 (2015) separated them again at a published threshold of thirteen character changes, hedged in their own abstract as valid “by our quantitative approach,” with a named public dissent from Donald Prothero four months later. Torosaurus — Scannella, J. B. & Horner, J. R., “Torosaurus Marsh, 1891, is Triceratops Marsh, 1889: synonymy through ontogeny,” Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology 30(4) (2010); answered by Longrich, N. R. & Field, D. J., “Torosaurus is not Triceratops: ontogeny in chasmosaurine ceratopsids as a case study in dinosaur taxonomy,” PLOS ONE 7(2), e32623 (2012), on epoccipital development and fusion, cranial suture closure and bone surface texture. No consensus has been reached, and this card takes no side. Nanotyrannus — Zanno, L. E. & Napoli, J. G., “Nanotyrannus and Tyrannosaurus coexisted at the close of the Cretaceous,” Nature (2025): the near somatically mature specimen NCSM 40000 from the Hell Creek Formation, part of the “Duelling Dinosaurs” discovery, sharing autapomorphies with the N. lancensis holotype; comparative anatomy, longitudinal growth models, ontogenetic character invariance and a new phylogenetic dataset place the taxon outside Tyrannosauridae and find two species in it, the second named N. lethaeus. Independently corroborated the same year by work finding an external fundamental system in the hyoid of the N. lancensis holotype, indicating a skeletally mature animal. Given from the paper's abstract, the issuing institution's release and its PubMed record; the full paper was not read. Tanystropheus — Spiekman, S. N. F., Neenan, J. M., Fraser, N. C. and colleagues, “Aquatic habits and niche partitioning in the extraordinarily long-necked Triassic reptile Tanystropheus,” Current Biology (2020): CT of crushed slabs resolved two species rather than one growth series — the large T. hydroides with conical teeth and the small T. longobardicus with crown-shaped teeth, both with growth marks indicating maturity, and dietary separation rather than competition. Oviraptor — named Oviraptor philoceratops by Henry Fairfield Osborn in 1924, egg seizer, lover of ceratopsians, from a specimen found atop a clutch assumed to belong to Protoceratops. Corrected through Norell, M. A. and colleagues, “A theropod dinosaur embryo and the affinities of the Flaming Cliffs dinosaur eggs,” Science 266, 779–782 (1994), and Norell, M. A., Clark, J. M., Chiappe, L. M. & Dashzeveg, D., “A nesting dinosaur,” Nature 378, 774–776 (1995). Both cited from bibliographic records rather than read at the primary, and the card is careful about what they establish: the 1995 brooding specimen is an oviraptorid later named Citipati osmolskae, not Osborn's animal. The tidy claim that the 1923 specimen itself was proved innocent is not made here. Basilosaurus — named by Richard Harlan in 1834, king lizard, on a comparison of vertebral size with Megalosaurus and Iguanodon; Richard Owen examined the material in London in 1839, found two-rooted molars unknown in fishes or reptiles, and concluded the animal was a mammal, proposing Zeuglodon. Priority kept Basilosaurus; Zeuglodon became a synonym. Given from museum and reference accounts, not from Harlan or Owen at the primary. Tetrapod trackways — Niedźwiedzki, G., Szrek, P., Narkiewicz, K., Narkiewicz, M. & Ahlberg, P. E., “Tetrapod trackways from the early Middle Devonian period of Poland,” Nature 463, 43–48 (2010): digit-bearing trackways from Zachełmie dated to the lowermost Eifelian, about 397 million years ago, roughly eighteen million years before the earliest tetrapod body fossils and around ten before Tiktaalik roseae (Daeschler, Shubin & Jenkins, Nature 440, 2006). And the tracks are contested: Lucas (2015) reinterprets some of the circular and oval depressions as fish feeding or nesting traces (ichnogenus Piscichnus), and a 2020 study of non-tetrapod trace fossils from the same quarry agrees for part of the surface, while the digit impressions and the absence of body drags on the quadrupedal trackways are held by others to rule the alternatives out. The card carries the dispute rather than the punchline. Velociraptor — Turner, A. H., Makovicky, P. J. & Norell, M. A., “Feather quill knobs in the dinosaur Velociraptor,” Science 317 (2007): quill knobs on the forearm, the anchor points for large feathers in living birds. Paywalled and not read at the primary, so only the finding its title states is used, and the knob and feather counts that circulate in press coverage are deliberately not printed. The tail-draggers — Ostrom, J. H., Osteology of Deinonychus antirrhopus, an unusual theropod from the Lower Cretaceous of Montana, Bulletin 30, Peabody Museum of Natural History, Yale (1969), whose functional reading implied an upright, actively predatory animal at odds with the cold and sluggish picture; Bakker, R. T., “Dinosaur renaissance,” Scientific American (1975), which named and argued the shift. Given from standard historical accounts of the renaissance rather than read at either primary. Mary Anning — found the first complete plesiosaur skeleton in 1823, prepared it, and drew it; William Conybeare presented it to the Geological Society without naming her. Women could not be admitted to the Geological Society of London until 1904. On her only trip to London, in 1829, she saw specimens she had collected on display with no label carrying her name. Georges Cuvier did cite her; William Buckland acknowledged her openly. Given from museum, learned-society and biographical accounts rather than archival primaries, and this card carries no reframe table on purpose. Held honestly: the sort into five kinds of error is ours, arrived at after the entries; a sort by date of correction was tried first and failed. The status notation is also ours — a judgement about the state of a literature, not a fact in it. Nothing on this page licenses any claim about human beings; these are kin and colleagues with their own business, and the belonging claim in No. 61 deliberately does not rest on any of them. There is no final draft.
