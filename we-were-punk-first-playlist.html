We Were Punk First

Thirty-eight recordings of Indigenous punk, metal and hard rock — and the argument underneath them, which begins with a record that had no words in it and got banned anyway.

Playlist & essay · Sound
One

A band that started as a piece about listening

Dead Pioneers did not begin as a band. It began as an artwork — The Punk Pan-Indian Romantic Comedy, a piece by the Colorado painter, muralist and performance artist Gregg Deal, of the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe, about his own relationship with music across a lifetime and what it did to him at each turn. The band was assembled to make the sound for that piece. Then the sound outgrew the piece.

Which means the origin of this band is itself a rack: one person, working out which records had carried him and why. Deal was in his late forties when the self-titled debut arrived in 2023. He is fifty-one now, with three albums out — the debut, PO$T AMERICAN, and Wagon Burner, released 26 June 2026 — and one very good sentence about why any of it exists.

“I wrote the record I wish I could have heard when I was young.”

Gregg Deal, interviewed in Kerrang!, 23 June 2026

That is the whole of this page's motive, said better than we could say it. It is also, precisely, why the pieces on this site keep insisting that a thing you were told was a deficit is a variation: somebody had to be the first person in the room to say it out loud, and the reason they had to is that nobody had said it to them.

Most of what Deal does on these records is spoken word over a band. That is worth knowing before you press play, because it changes what you are listening for. The songs are arguments with jokes in them, and they will not wait for you.

The band's claim, and the title of this page, is a priority claim. We Were Punk First holds that punk is not a form that Native people joined in the 1970s. Punk is rebellion; rebellion here is five hundred years old; the word arrived very late to a thing already fully under way. It is a claim about stance rather than about discography — and the discography turns out to have its own answer, which is the next section.

The rack next door argues the other half

Our punk rack It Take a Joyful Sound is built on the opposite move: in July 1977 Bob Marley recorded a guest list for an alliance that did not exist until it was read out, and naming a thing helped it happen. That is true, and this page is the other half of it. Naming is not founding. A name can call something into being, and a name can also arrive centuries late and take the credit.

Two racks on one shelf, disagreeing on purpose. We would rather print the disagreement than smooth it.

Two

A record with no words, banned for what it said

In 1958 a guitarist from Dunn, North Carolina, released a two-and-a-half-minute instrumental called Rumble. It has no lyrics. It has no title words you could object to beyond the one. Radio stations banned it anyway, on the grounds that it would incite juvenile gang violence.

Sit with that for a second, because it is the cleanest thing on this page. A signal was transmitted that contained no propositions at all. Every listener who was frightened by it supplied the fright themselves, out of their own head, and then attributed it to the record. The meaning was not in the message. The meaning was in the receiver, and the receiver blamed the sender.

Fred Lincoln Wray — Link Wray — was the son of a white father and a Shawnee mother, and he described a childhood town where the police and the shopkeepers were also the Klan. He punched holes in his amplifier's speaker cone to get the sound he wanted, and the power chord he played through it became so ubiquitous that people stopped hearing it as a choice. Pete Townshend said that without Wray and Rumble he would never have picked up a guitar. Jimmy Page and Iggy Pop said versions of the same thing. Everything loud that came afterwards came partly through him.

A correction, and it is our source that needed it

Al Carroll's Indigenous Punk — the book this rack was largely built from, and a good one — states that Wray was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2007. He was not. He was inducted in 2023, in the Musical Influence category, per the Hall's own inductee record. He died in 2005 and waited eighteen years, not two.

We are printing this because the sixteen-year gap is the argument rather than a footnote to it. The same book's entry on Dead Pioneers names the guitarist “Gregg Rivera”; he is Josh Rivera. Neither error changes a claim we are making. Both are the reason we check.

One more thing about Wray, held open rather than resolved. Carroll notes that census records show the family recorded as “White,” and also that Wray identified as Shawnee and was accepted as Shawnee by other Native people in his lifetime. Both of those are in the book, on the same page, and we are not going to pick one. They are what the record says.

Three

The drumbeat you think is Indian

You already have a sound in your head for this. A tom-tom, four beats to the bar, a heavy stamp on the first and three light ones after it. BOOM-boom-boom-boom. A minor pentatonic melody over an open fifth. You have heard it in a hundred Westerns and a thousand cartoons, and you have probably drummed it on a table.

No Native musical tradition produced it. It is a European concert-hall convention that hardened into a film convention, and by the 1920s it was a purchasable commodity: silent-film accompanists worked from cue anthologies indexed by mood and situation, of which Erno Rapée's Motion Picture Moods for Pianists and Organists (1924) is the canonical example — a rapid-reference collection of pieces sorted into fifty-two moods and situations, so an organist could find the right stock cue while the reel was running. The “Indian” sound was one of the entries. You looked it up.

So the thing millions of people believe is the sound of Native America is, in its actual provenance, sheet music sold to a man at an organ who had ninety seconds to find the page.

Your traditional powwow beat is like a heartbeat. It's you.

Al Carroll, on A People's Guide to Publishing, May 2026 — transcribed from the recording

Carroll's own account is that the Western caricature was built to sound threatening, and that Native music does something close to the reverse for him — that it is calming, and that punk and metal, by way of volume and anger, do the same job. That is a claim about regulation, made in plain words by somebody describing his own nervous system, and it is the reason a page about loud music sits on this particular site at all.

Then listen to what happens when a real drum meets a label. XIT — “Crossing of Indian Tribes,” out of Albuquerque — signed to Motown, put Taos Pueblo drums in place of a standard kit, sang partly in Navajo, Lakota and Puebloan, and were kept off American radio while charting across Europe. And then listen to 1876, from Portland, covering the Black Lodge Singers' children's song Mighty Mouse as a punk song, with powwow drums and powwow vocables, entirely unbothered.

What we could not settle

Carroll says on the podcast that the Western “Indian” music was written by Russian immigrant composers in the 1920s. We have not traced that to a primary and it is not asserted above. The transcript we worked from is machine-generated and demonstrably imperfect — in the same conversation it places the Argentine group Tacuara in Chile, where Carroll's own book says Argentina — so we treat it as a lead about the book, not as the book.

What we did confirm: that Motion Picture Moods exists, is 1924, and is organised as a mood-and-situation index for accompanists. What would settle the rest: Michael V. Pisani, Imagining Native America in Music (Yale University Press, 2005), and Tara Browner, Heartbeat of the People (University of Illinois Press, 2002) for what a Northern-style powwow song actually does with its accented beats. We have read neither. The negative claim above — that the Hollywood pattern is a studio convention rather than a transcription — is the part we are standing on.

Four

The landscape is a filter, not a silence

Ask why the far-north bands sound the way they do and you will get the word isolation. Ask why Navajo rez metal sits in that particular seam between thrash and black metal and you will get the desert. Both answers are gestures at something real, and the something real is measurable.

Air is a low-pass filter, and it is not a subtle one. A sound wave loses energy to the air it travels through, partly to viscosity and heat conduction and mostly, in ordinary conditions, to molecular relaxation in oxygen and nitrogen. The loss climbs steeply with frequency — roughly with the square of it for the classical part. Over a kilometre, a low note arrives having lost a decibel or two. A high one can lose tens. This is why distant thunder rumbles and near thunder cracks: same event, different amount of air.

Fresh snow is one of the most absorptive surfaces in the natural world. It is a porous solid — a foam of ice and trapped air — and sound entering it is scattered and damped rather than reflected. Measured absorption coefficients for new snow run very high across the mid and upper frequencies. A snowfield does not echo. It swallows.

And then the opposite effect, in the same place. Sound travels faster in warmer air, so a temperature gradient bends it. Over snow and ice, especially on a still night, the air near the ground is colder than the air above it — an inversion — so the sound speed increases with height, and wavefronts curve back down toward the surface instead of escaping upward. Range extends dramatically. Over hot open ground in daylight the gradient runs the other way and sound bends up and away, leaving an acoustic shadow a surprisingly short distance from the source.

So a landscape does not simply have more or less sound in it. A landscape decides which parts of a sound survive, and how far. The far north takes the treble out and hands the low end a long way. The daylight desert takes the whole thing and throws it at the sky. Neither place is quiet. Both are selective.

And what comes out of those places, made by people who grew up listening in them, is exactly that shape: long low tones and slow decay from the north, and something dry, fast and close-range from the desert. We are not claiming the acoustics caused the music. We are pointing at a rhyme, and marking it as one.

The landscape gives you a message, and it will affect you down to the molecular level.

Al Carroll, on the same podcast — his claim, and a sociological one, not ours and not acoustics

That line arrived on its own and we are not going to pretend we planned it. Down to the molecular level is the shape of the sentence this whole site is built on, said by somebody who had never heard of us, about something else entirely. In the north the molecules in question are ice and trapped air. In the sentence we usually write, they are carbon and calcium and iron. It is the same grammar: down to names how far a thing reaches, not why it is deserved.

A third rack, and the same fact wearing new clothes

Two Sound essays already converged without meaning to. The punk rack found that distortion is a nonlinearity adding harmonics that were never sent in; the AURORA rack found that the solar wind delivers energy and the colour is the atom's own. Both said: what comes out is characteristic of the material.

This page arrives at the same fact from the other side. Here the medium is not adding anything — it is taking things away, and what it takes is characteristic of the medium. Addition and subtraction, same principle. We noticed it afterwards, reading the three side by side, which is the only honest way to notice a pattern. It remains a rhyme rather than a proof. Atmospheric acoustics has no opinion about anybody's belonging.

Five

Singing to two hundred people

There is a decision that recurs across this music and has no equivalent in most of the rest of popular culture: bands recording in languages with a few thousand speakers, releasing the songs untranslated, and declining to explain.

Northern Haze, from Igloolik, made a metal album in Inuktitut in 1985 for Inuit listeners in Inuit towns, played it in school auditoriums and legion halls because there were no clubs, and put out nothing to help an outsider follow it. Hamac Caziim, who are Comcaac from Sonora, record traditional songs as metal at the urging of their own elders, because the young people would take the language that way. Warumpi Band's Jailanguru Pakarnu was the first rock song released in an Aboriginal language. Alien Weaponry write in te reo Māori about specific battles their own ancestors died in.

None of that is a marketing position. The audience is the audience, and if it is small then it is small. Carroll's own summary of the whole phenomenon is that this music is Indigenous people adapting the dominant culture on their own terms — taking a form that arrived with the colonisers, keeping the volume, and putting the language back in.

Which produces one of the more useful reversals on this site. We spend a lot of pages arguing that a thing should be made legible — that a person should not have to translate themselves to be counted. Here is a body of work that simply declines to be translated, and is not diminished by it, and does not owe anyone the subtitles.

Six

How a scene checks itself

There is a category of act in this world that has to be named because the music itself cannot filter it out: white supremacists using Indigenous names, imagery and languages while making records that hate Indigenous people.

Carroll's book carries an appendix of them — dozens of project names belonging, by his count, to fewer than twenty individuals, most of them one person alone in a room, most of them banned from playing anywhere, several running an openly Nazi project alongside the “Native” one. The longer history is the same shape: the founder of the modern Klan claimed Cherokee ancestry, and a Klan organiser named Asa Carter reinvented himself as the Cherokee author “Forrest Carter,” sold The Education of Little Tree and The Outlaw Josey Wales, and was believed, at scale, by Hollywood.

How the scene actually checks: you ask. Communities are small, families are known, and somebody knows somebody. It is not forensic and it does not need to be. The failure mode Carroll describes is not that verification is hard — it is that people who know very little about Native communities are reluctant to admit how little they know, so they accept whatever fits the picture they already have.

That failure mode is the whole reason this site keeps a public changelog.

Where we stop

We are reporting the findings of a book. We are not extending them. This page runs no identity verdicts of its own, on anybody — not on the acts we have carded, not on the ones we have left out. We are not equipped to, the question is not ours to adjudicate, and a website doing it from a search engine is a great deal closer to the problem than to the solution.

Where Carroll makes a judgement about a particular musician, that judgement is his, in his book, under his name, and you should go and read it there. Where his own criteria for who counts as Native are contested — and they are, including inside Native communities — we have not adopted them as ours.

Seven

Still here, and tired

Carroll's conclusion, after three hundred and four Native bands, fifteen Aboriginal bands and thirty-two Pacific Islander bands, is four words long: this music says we are still here.

The exhaustion in it is not incidental and it is not a personal failing. Dead Pioneers have a spoken-word piece whose whole architecture is a list of structural facts, ending on being tired — not defeated, not despairing, tired, which is what a rigged system produces in the people it is rigged against, and which is a description of the system rather than of them. Anyone who has spent years explaining their own existence to institutions recognises the register immediately.

And this is where we have to be careful, because it would be very easy and completely wrong to walk away from this page having quietly converted somebody else's history into a metaphor for ours.

“For Indigenous people, gathering knowledge is not just a thing that you do, it's a tool of survival.”

Gregg Deal, Kerrang!, 23 June 2026

Deal himself has described what happens with Bad Indian — a song about being asked to live up to somebody else's picture of you. He has said that people connect to it through their own experience of not measuring up and not belonging, that he accepts this as a real byproduct of the work, and that he does not accept any duty to educate anybody. Both halves of that matter, and the second half is the one that usually gets dropped.

So: the structural rhyme is real, it is narrow, and it belongs to the reader rather than to us. Being handed a portrait made by other people and then graded on how well you match it is the mechanism in “but you don't look autistic,” and it is the mechanism in the tom-tom and the headdress and the mascot. Recognising the shape is allowed. Claiming the experience is not. Colonisation is not a figure of speech.

What this site says, it says here too and no louder: the universe does not pathologise its own variation, a filter selects rather than ranks, and down to measures how far the loving reaches, not what earns it.

  • Not a metaphor. Colonisation, boarding schools and land theft are not figures of speech for anything else, including neurodivergence. Where a structure rhymes we say rhyme, in that word, and we do not claim the experience.
  • Not the noble angry Indian. If a page about this music has no He-Man joke, no Mighty Mouse cover and no time-travelling Custerbots in it, it has rebuilt the caricature from the other side. The jokes are load-bearing.
  • Not an identity audit. We run no verdicts on who is or is not Native. See section six.
  • Not a lyric sheet. Every card describes its recording rather than reprinting it. The words are the artists' work and the place to meet them is the record.
  • Not a land acknowledgement standing in for anything. Deal opens his TEDx talk with one and immediately gives it teeth — this is still Indian land — then closes by telling the room that they are now responsible for what they have just heard. A sentence at the top of a page that changes nothing below it is worse than no sentence.
  • Not artists diagnosed from a distance. Standing rule across this collection: where a musician has spoken about being neurodivergent we use their word; where we recognise something they have not named, the recognition is ours and we say so.
One — We were punk first

Dead Pioneers, who began as an artwork about what music does to a person, and then became the thing they were describing.

The priority claim
We Were Punk First
Dead Pioneers · live on KEXP

Eighty seconds, and the shortest argument on the shelf: if punk means refusal, then the word turned up five centuries after the practice. Ends on a demand rather than a thesis — language, ceremony, land. Live, because a demand read to a room is a different object from a demand on a record.

Being graded on the portrait
Bad Indian
Dead Pioneers · 2023

The first single, and a spoken-word set piece about strangers auditing your Indianness in real time — including a very long joke that a woman does not get, delivered so patiently that the patience is the point.

Appropriation, itemised
My Spirit Animal Ate Your Spirit Animal
Dead Pioneers · 2023

Released the day after Thanksgiving, which is a piece of scheduling doing as much work as the song. Sacred things sold as merchandise, answered with a title that refuses to be solemn about it.

Exhaustion as a finding
Tired
Dead Pioneers · live at PBS12

A stacked list of structural facts about who the arrangement is built for, ending not in rage but in fatigue. The live take is here rather than the studio one because you can watch a room receive it.

The second record
PO$T AMERICAN
Dead Pioneers · 2025

Title track of the album Deal calls act two to the debut. The dollar sign is not decoration: the argument is that the country and the market are the same object described twice.

The third record
Nazi Teeth
Dead Pioneers · 2026

Lead single from Wagon Burner, whose title reclaims a slur out of westward expansion. Deal says he takes seriously the responsibility to say the thing out loud; this is what that sounds like when it is not being careful.

Two — Before Link Wray there was no hard rock

A Shawnee guitarist, a punctured speaker cone, and an instrumental that got banned for things it never said.

The banned instrumental
Rumble
Link Wray & His Ray Men · 1958

No words. Banned anyway, for incitement. Listen for how little is actually happening — a slow chord sequence, enormous reverb, and space where a melody would be. The menace is entirely on loan from the listener.

The follow-up
Raw-Hide
Link Wray & The Wraymen · 1959

Same year's answer, and much faster. Where Rumble withholds, this one runs. Useful as a control: it proves the earlier record's threat was a matter of restraint rather than aggression.

Naming, from inside
Comanche
Link Wray & The Wraymen · 1959

A Native guitarist putting a tribal name on a surf-rock instrumental in the same decade Hollywood was scoring the caricature. There is no tom-tom cue in it. There is a man with a guitar and a broken speaker.

The voice, late
Fire and Brimstone
Link Wray · 1971

From the album recorded in a converted chicken shack, with Wray singing for the first time — swamp rock about being rural and poor in the South. Thirteen years after the instrumental that made him, he uses words.

Three — The drumbeat you think is Indian

What happens when actual drums arrive on a major label, and what happens when a punk band covers a powwow song for children.

Real drums, major label
War Cry
XIT · 1972

From Plight of the Redman, on Motown. Pueblo drums where the kit should be, and a song calling for self-defence that names who started the confrontation. Banned from most American radio; a European hit.

Boarding school, set to music
Reservation of Education
XIT · 1974

Six minutes on forced assimilation schooling, with room in it for children being taught to play at killing Native people as a game. For that generation this was the protest record; it charted in France and nowhere at home.

Prime-time television
We Were All Wounded at Wounded Knee
Redbone · 1973

A song about an 1890 massacre and a 1973 occupation, performed on Dutch pop TV in front of an audience who mostly did not know what it was about. It went to number one in the Netherlands.

The one everybody knows
Come and Get Your Love
Redbone · live on The Midnight Special

The biggest Native hit in American chart history, back in the charts forty years later off a Marvel soundtrack, and still routinely heard by people with no idea the band was Native. Watch the live take and the fringe answers the question.

An actual powwow beat
Mighty Mouse
1876 · powwow punk rock

A punk cover of the Black Lodge Singers' song for children, from a Portland band who call what they do powwow punk rock and use powwow drums to do it. The best possible answer to the Hollywood tom-tom is a joke aimed at kids.

Four — The landscape is a filter

The far north takes the treble out and carries the rest a long way. The daylight desert throws everything at the sky. Listen to the two next to each other.

Igloolik, untranslated
Qainna
Northern Haze

Metal in Inuktitut from a band who started in the 1970s, played school auditoriums because there were no clubs, and resorted to toy drums for a kit. The vocal is unhurried in a way nothing else in the genre is.

Thirty-three years later
Nutaraq (Poor Child)
Northern Haze

From Siqinnaarut — “return of the sun” — recorded after a gap of decades and the deaths of two members, with a son and a nephew filling the places. Same calm, more weight behind it.

The instrument is the throat
Retribution
Tanya Tagaq · 2016

Inuit throat singing driven into industrial metal, mostly without words, by a singer who can do more with breath than most bands manage with amplifiers. Eight minutes, and the earth is the one making the threat.

Black metal, Adirondack
Barefoot Ghost Dance on Blood-Soaked Soil
Blackbraid · 2022

The single that introduced a one-man Mohawk black metal project and immediately made the genre argue with itself. Long decays, flute, and a title that will not let the atmosphere stay decorative.

Central Australia
Desert Metal
Southeast Desert Metal

From Santa Teresa in the Northern Territory, and the closest thing here to a manifesto with no argument in it. Dry, fast, close-range — the sound of a place that does not carry your sound for you.

Six brothers and cousins
Young Men (Younga Barla Mapa)
Mulga Bore Hard Rock

From a community 170km out of Alice Springs, modelled on KISS and playing closer to AC/DC, and good enough that they ended up opening for KISS. Sung partly in language, and entirely without solemnity.

Five — Singing to two hundred people

Records made in languages with very few speakers, released untranslated, for an audience that was never going to be large.

At the elders' urging
Live in the Zócalo
Hamac Caziim · Comcaac, Sonora

Traditional Seri songs rebuilt as metal, at the request of Comcaac elders, so that young people would take the language in the form they would actually listen to. This is the band in the main square of Mexico City doing exactly that.

The first one
Jailanguru Pakarnu (Out from Jail)
Warumpi Band · 1983

The first rock song released in an Australian Aboriginal language, out of Papunya, a settlement of a few hundred people. It sounds like a band who have not been told this is unusual.

A named ancestor
Rū Ana Te Whenua
Alien Weaponry · 2018

Thrash in te reo Māori about the Battle of Pukehinahina, where the band's own ancestor was killed. Written by teenagers, in a language they were consciously using the music to teach.

Sāmoan power metal
Le Manu
Shepherds Reign

Islander drums, Sāmoan and English, and a sense of theatre that earns itself rather than borrowing from Vikings. A call to prepare, filmed as one, with the priests and elders in it.

Under three thousand speakers
Iɫee Tsimshian
Gyibaaw · live

Tsimshian black metal, performed live, from a band making work for a community of a few thousand people and providing no translation. The audience it is for is the audience it is for.

Brazil, in Tupi
Hêwaka Waktû
Arandu Arakuaa

Folk metal in Indigenous Brazilian languages with traditional instruments carrying the melody rather than decorating it. The guitars arrive late and are the least interesting thing in the arrangement.

Prehispanic metal
Guerreros de Cemican
Cemican

One of the largest bands in a whole genre that barely exists outside Latin America: death whistles, rattles and pre-Columbian drums opening the track before a guitar is allowed anywhere near it.

Six — How a scene checks itself

Bands who did the political work in public, and non-Native bands who got the history right without appointing themselves to it.

Punk with a medicine man in it
[Silence] Is a Weapon
Blackfire

Three Diné siblings, produced early on by a Ramone, touring rezzes and Wacken with equal seriousness, and lending their name to more campaigns than most organisations manage. The title is the thesis.

Further out than the band
Violator
Appropriation · Klee Benally

Benally's anti-colonial performance project, which went past even Blackfire's politics. This one is aimed at the abuse of women and queer people, and it is not interested in being liked by anybody.

Thrash, second Big Four
Native Blood
Testament · 2012

The longest-running band with a Native member at its centre — Chuck Billy, who is Pomo — writing directly about it only in the later era, after surviving cancer. The video walks a boy from playground racism to a land grab.

Non-Native, 1982
John Wayne Was a Nazi
MDC

Hardcore going after the man who was the friendly face of the genre this whole page is correcting. Not a Native band and not pretending to be — just the shortest available route to the point.

A specific standoff
Oka Everywhere
Propagandhi

Named for the 1990 Kanehsatà:ke resistance, and an example of the thing that is hard to do — solidarity that names an actual event rather than gesturing at a people. Homework, done.

Seven — Still here, and tired

Four ways of ending, none of them triumphant, all of them still going.

Instrumental, and unambiguous
Indigenous Sovereignty
Divide and Dissolve

Doom and drone with no words at all, from a duo whose track titles do all the talking. Two minutes that make the same point as Rumble: an instrument can be understood to be saying something.

The joke is the survival
We Will Remain
1876

Terminators sent back through time as Custerbots, Columbusbots and Pilgrimdroids, defeated by a Native woman from the future handing the band laser guns. It is a survival anthem and it is extremely funny, on purpose.

What came after Blackfire
We the People
Sihasin

Two of the Benally siblings continuing after their brother's death, with their father — a medicine man — singing alongside them. Bass, drums and a family, which turns out to be enough.

Censorship, then everything after
The Worst Among Us
Dead Pioneers feat. Jason Williamson · 2026

Written after Deal was asked to censor part of a talk at the Smithsonian, and tracing where that request leads if nobody stops it. Jason Williamson of Sleaford Mods on the other microphone.

The close
No One Owns Anything and Death Is Real
Dead Pioneers · 2023

The one to end on, and the least angry thing they have made. A patient argument that ownership was always a story, delivered to people who are certain it was not. The last word goes to the ground.

This rack adds thirty-eight embeds to a collection that already has no transcripts and no audited captions, and every card's written note is designed to stand on its own for that reason. The wrinkle: a substantial share of this music is untranslated by the artists' decision, for audiences it was specifically made for.
