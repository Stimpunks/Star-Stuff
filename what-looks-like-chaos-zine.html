What Looks
Like Chaos

Nest building is the textbook example of pure instinct. Seven weavers, twenty-one nests, and a camera say otherwise — and so does a room somebody told you to tidy.

Two · a completely curated space

To most, it appears
that I live in a state of chaos

David Gray-Hammond describes his room. There is a bed with enough pillows and a duvet that doesn't overheat him. A galaxy projector on the ceiling. LED lights wired to respond to the television, because synchronised sound and light is a sensory need and not a flourish. An oil diffuser — sweet peas, lavender, sometimes cedarwood — so that even his sense of smell is something he chooses rather than something that happens to him.

On the bed: snacks, medication, notebooks, and several books at once, because his attention moves between them rather than down through one. A tripod and a light ring within reach, so a thought can be recorded the moment it arrives. Nothing gets tidied away before sleep, because some days end in a crash rather than a decision, and the room has to be survivable on those days too.

To most, it appears that I live in a state of chaos, but in actual fact I have my needs fulfilled in this space, and what appears to be chaos is actually a completely curated space. I know where every item I need resides.

David Gray-Hammond · AuDHD and me: My nesting habits · 2023

He calls it nesting. Two people can stand in that room and see two different things, and only one of them is looking at a plan.

What Looks Like Chaos · Stimpunks 2
Three · the bird that already knew

The bird that was
said to already know

There is a claim about birds so old and so comfortable that most of us absorbed it without ever being taught it directly. A bird builds a nest it has never seen, without instruction, and gets it right the first time. The nest is therefore not a thing the bird works out. It is a thing the bird contains — a fixed action pattern, a genetic template, running to completion.

It is a good story, and it was for a long time the default position in the literature rather than a fringe one. Walsh and colleagues state it plainly as the thing they set out to test: if nest building runs on fixed action patterns or is predominantly under genetic control, as is often assumed, then the behaviours should vary little between birds and should repeat reliably within one (Walsh et al., 2011).

That is a real prediction, and it is falsifiable, which is the only reason any of this is interesting. A template predicts sameness — between individuals, and within the same individual across attempts. So somebody went and measured whether the sameness is there.

A template predicts sameness. Not beauty, not competence — sameness. That is the whole of what it predicts, and it is the only thing that can be checked.
What Looks Like Chaos · Stimpunks 3
Four · seven weavers, twenty-one nests

Seven weavers,
twenty-one nests

Southern Botswana, a farm outside Gaborone. Seven male Southern Masked weavers, each one colour-ringed so it could be told from the others. Territories searched daily through the building season, between half past five in the morning and quarter to seven at night, so that a nest was found at the moment it was started rather than halfway through.

Camcorders on adjustable tripods, set square to the nest. Three nests filmed per bird — twenty-one nests. Then the footage was coded by a researcher who did not know which bird they were watching or on what date, which is the part that makes the rest of it worth reading.

What got counted: which side of the beak the grass protruded from, which side of the nest it went into, which quadrant of the structure the bird wove in and for how long, and every time a blade of grass was lost or dropped (Walsh et al., 2011).

Not the finished nest. Almost everything known about how birds know what to build had come from examining structures that were already complete — which is a little like judging a life by the tidiness of the room.
What Looks Like Chaos · Stimpunks 4
Five · a hand of one's own

Four birds had a side.
The species had none

During the first phase of construction — attaching the first blades to a bare branch — not one of the measured behaviours was repeatable within a male. Not the direction he carried the grass, not how long he spent weaving, not which part of the structure he favoured, not how often he dropped a blade. The template predicted sameness and got none.

Then the individuality. Two of the seven consistently pushed the first end of a blade into the right side of the nest. Two consistently pushed it into the left. The remaining three had no side at all. And pooled across all seven birds and hundreds of insertions, the bias vanished — no lean either way (G = 0.06, p = 0.81).

Personal handedness, with no species handedness underneath it. If there were a template, that is precisely what could not happen.

What Looks Like Chaos · Stimpunks 5
Six · they got better

They dropped less
grass every time

Here is the finding that finishes the template off. Across a season, as each male built more nests, he lost hold of the grass less often (p < 0.01). Not the species improving over evolutionary time. One bird, over three nests, in a few weeks, getting better at the thing.

And the birds with the most consistent personal style paid for it in the right currency: the more strongly a male leaned to his own side, the faster he finished the ring phase (R² = 0.25, p = 0.01). A way of one's own was not an eccentricity to be corrected. It was the thing that made him quick.

None of this was new in kind. Elsie and Nicholas Collias had watched Village Weavers move from juvenile to adult in 1964 and found that experienced birds build neater, more tightly woven nests than inexperienced ones — the first nest is crude, and the bird is not born knowing better (reported in Walsh et al., 2011).

The authors are careful about what this does and does not show, and so are we: they attribute the change most readily to increasing dexterity, and say outright that whether cognition is involved needs further work. We are not going to sell you a clever bird. Practice is enough for the argument.

A template does not improve. Improvement is what something does when it was never finished to begin with.
What Looks Like Chaos · Stimpunks 6
Seven · forty-three families

Forty-three families,
forty-three styles

Thirteen years later, a different weaver and a much harder question. White-browed sparrow weavers live in family groups and build two kinds of structure: nests for breeding, and single-occupant roosts for sleeping. Forty-three groups were measured (Tello-Ramos et al., 2024).

The groups build differently from each other. Some make long roosts with extended entrance tubes; others make short ones with barely any tube. The differences hold across years, and they hold between groups whose territories sit metres apart in the same trees.

Then the eliminations, one at a time: not the weather, not the size of the birds, not the height of the tree, not how closely related the groups were. And the finding that closes it — the styles are robust to immigration. A bird arrives from a group that builds one way, and the structures keep coming out the other way.

That is not a template, and it is not the environment either. It is a tradition — a way of building held by a group, taught by living in it, outlasting the particular birds who happen to be there.

and it survives a bird moving in from somewhere else

What Looks Like Chaos · Stimpunks 7
Eight · the refusals

What this zine
is not

Two findings, thirteen years apart, in two species of weaver. It would be easy — and it would be wrong — to walk them straight across into a claim about us. So, plainly:

Not: permission from nature

The birds do not authorise anything. A universe that could approve of us could also decline, and we would not accept the verdict either way. Nobody's room is justified by a weaver in Botswana. Down to is not because of.

Not: instinct versus learning

These birds are not learning instead of being birds. Development is not a contest between what came with you and what you did with it, and the finding is not that the template was replaced — it is that there was never a template doing the work anyone assumed.

Not: a clever-animal story

The authors themselves say the likeliest explanation is increasing dexterity, and that any role for cognition still needs demonstrating. We are not going to inflate that, because the inflated version is the one that gets repeated and then quietly corrected years later.

Not: every mess is a nest

This is the honest one. Gray-Hammond is explicit that burnout collapses the architecture — spaces stop being maintained, and what is left can look, from outside, exactly like the curated version. A nest and its ruins are not the same thing. The difference is never visible from the doorway, which is the entire point.

What the science does kill is narrower, and sturdier for being narrow. It kills the inference that an order you cannot read is not an order. Two centuries of confident observers looked at a nest and saw a machine executing. They were describing the limits of watching, and calling it a fact about the bird.

What Looks Like Chaos · Stimpunks 8
Nine · an architecture of continuity

Every needed thing
where the attention already is

Gray-Hammond puts nesting beside lilipadding — the practice, set out by Tanya Adkin and David Gray-Hammond, of moving between small safe places by deliberate steps rather than leaps, honouring the tunnel of attention you are in while gently inviting the next one (Adkin & Gray-Hammond, 2025a). Nesting, in that frame, is the physical design of the lilypad. A hostile room makes us leap too far, too soon, just to get out of it. A well-nested one opens channels: a desk that runs into the bookshelves that run into somewhere soft to decompress, each area inviting the next, so that a transition costs a step instead of a rupture.

He calls the result an architecture of continuity. When his therapist suggested keeping a feelings journal across the room, he declined — because crossing the room in that moment is not two steps, it is fifteen. The journal within reach is a gentle pad. The same journal, six feet away, is a wall.

Which is why rest alone does not fix burnout. If burnout is the collapse of an architecture — the physical space, the relationships, the sense of continuity with yourself — then recovery means rebuilding the structure, not just lying still inside the rubble. Nesting is rehabilitative, not only preventative.

And it is why Prakash Nair's finding about schools stings. The cave — the space for going quiet and going deep — is one of the primordial learning spaces David Thornburg named, alongside the campfire, the watering hole, and a fourth that usually gets dropped, life (Thornburg, 2004). Nair's observation is that the cave is never the priority in a new school building, and that millions get spent on buildings that will not change outcomes because nobody costed a place to withdraw to. A room to be alone in is the one that gets value-engineered out, in a school and in a life.

Compassion is not simply an action performed by others. It is the environment we are invited to inhabit — and the space we provide to ourselves.

David Gray-Hammond · Autistic Nesting as the Physical Architecture of Lilipadding

Nobody is born knowing
how to build their own room.

Every weaver's first nest is crude. Every weaver gets better at it. No two of them do it the same way, and the species has no opinion about which side the grass goes in.

Our rooms are ours the same way — worked out, revised, improved by living in them, unreadable to anyone standing in the doorway with a clipboard. Stimpunks keeps two sentences side by side on the subject, and the pair is worth more than either alone:

It took me decades to learn how to change my environment to make myself comfortable.

@willaful

It took decades to learn I was allowed.

@steve_asbell

The weavers only need the first sentence. A bird gets better at building because it builds — that is the whole of it, and it is in the data. There is nothing in twenty-one nests in Botswana that corresponds to the second sentence, because nobody had ever told those birds they were not allowed to weave.

That is exactly where the rhyme stops. The first sentence is a fact about learning, and the birds have it too. The second is a fact about permission — and permission is not a feature of nature, it is something people hand out or withhold. Which means it is the half that can be changed by deciding to.

So when an institution takes a room away from us, it is not confiscating clutter. It is demolishing the thing that was holding us together, and calling the demolition tidiness. And when it takes decades to learn you were allowed, somebody spent those decades not telling you.

Credit where it is owed

Nesting, as used here — the curated sensory space, the room that is a plan — is David Gray-Hammond's, from his own writing about his own room, as is the essay that reads nesting as the physical architecture of lilipadding. He is an Autistic, ADHD and Schizophrenic author, independent academic and peer-support professional, and founder of NeuroHub Community, where his writing now lives. Lilipadding is not his alone, and a first draft of this zine said it was. Both pieces that set the practice out are co-authored by Tanya Adkin and Gray-Hammond, with Adkin's name first on each, and they are cited that way below. Caves, campfires and watering holes are David Thornburg's — a draft of this zine credited them to Prakash Nair, who uses them in school design, which is not the same as originating them. Nair's is the finding that new schools are built without cave space. Thornburg's set is actually four; the fourth, life, is usually dropped, and we say so rather than passing on the tidier three. The two sentences on spread 9 are @willaful's and @steve_asbell's, kept as a pair by Stimpunks and cited here by the handles they were published under, because we could not confirm which person each account belongs to. The weaver findings belong to the researchers named below.

What we checked, and what we did not

Walsh et al. (2011) was read in full, and every statistic on spreads 5 and 6 is taken from the paper itself, not from a summary of it. Tello-Ramos et al. (2024) was read at abstract level, together with the lead author's own account of the study in The Conversation; the full text sits behind a paywall and we say so rather than implying otherwise. The Collias & Collias (1964) result reaches us through Walsh et al. and is marked as such on spread 6. Gray-Hammond's essay Autistic Nesting as the Physical Architecture of Lilipadding was originally published on his Substack, which now returns a 410; the passages quoted here are preserved verbatim in the Stimpunks glossary entry, which is where we took them from, and it is the one source here we could not open at its origin. The other pieces are live and were read at source. Authorship on two of them was checked only after Ryan Boren corrected us — a draft credited both lilipadding posts to Gray-Hammond alone, and both carry Tanya Adkin's byline first. We had assumed a sole author from a shared subject. The same substitution happened a second time in the same build, with caves, campfires and watering holes credited to the writer we had a quotation from rather than to David Thornburg, who named them. Both were caught by Ryan Boren, and both are the failure Too Good to Check catalogues: the most quotable source standing in for the real one.
